# The Through-Tenon as Ethics

The arm of the settle does not die in the post. A tenon comes through the oak, its end grain showing, and a wedge is driven across the tenon so the joint cannot slip back. You can put a fingernail on the wedge. That is the argument. Everything else in this chapter is commentary.

A good place to see the argument at furniture scale is an even-arm Craftsman settle — shop number 208 is the type collectors handle most — or the giant knock-down 210 in Catalog D, about 1904. The 210’s own sentence is almost too helpful: the tenons projecting through the front and back posts “are pinned, and form a pleasing decoration.” Eastwood knew the joint was a picture. It also knew the joint was a joint. The two facts can sit together without becoming a religion.

## What the joint is doing

A through-tenon is a mortise-and-tenon that refuses to stop inside the post. The tenon’s end is visible on the far face. A pin, a key, or a pair of wedges keeps the shoulders tight. A keyed tenon uses a transverse wedge through a slot; a pinned tenon uses a peg; a wedged through-tenon splits the protruding end and drives wedges from the outside. The names get sloppy in dealer talk. The physics does not. Wood movement will try to loosen a joint. A wedge that is across the tenon tightens as the rail tries to pull out.

On a settle arm the joint is public. The sitter’s hand lands near it. A child can find the end grain. Compare a Victorian parlor sofa whose frame is a secret under tufting. The Craftsman hall (chapter 13, chapter 37) wanted the secret told. Irene Sargent’s early *Craftsman* essays had already translated Ruskin into a portable idea: a joint you can see is a moral; a veneer that pretends to be a board is a lie (chapter 2). The through-tenon is that idea at the scale of a thumb.

It is not, however, a ban on glue. Animal glue — hide glue — is how furniture of this period holds while the mechanical joint does its long work. Eastwood glued. The Hall shop glued (chapter 30). Morris & Co. glued. A sentence that treats glue as the enemy has mistaken a catalog photograph for a theology. The enemy is sham: a joint that claims, on the face, to be what the inside is not.

## What Grand Rapids could do instead

A Grand Rapids plant building a Mission suite for the next market (chapter 39) had machines that excelled at dowels, glue blocks, and clean shoulders. Dowel construction is fast. It is also, at its honest best, a good joint: aligned holes, hide glue, a shoulder that meets. Many factory chairs from Furniture City are still tight. Snobbery that has not sat in them is cheap history.

Sham begins when the factory wants the Eastwood photograph without the Eastwood minutes. A false through-tenon is an applied block of end-grain, or a shallow plug, glued onto a post that the rail never entered. A peg is driven into a hole that reaches no tenon. A “key” is painted. *The Furniture Record* did not usually confess these tricks; it printed a cut that read as honest from a buyer’s chair. Dealers a century later still tap a “tenon” and hear a tap, not a through-sound. The ear is a better tool than the adjective.

Some factory Mission shows a real through-tenon, pinned, on a chair that is otherwise dowelled. Mixed construction is common. Quality is a range. Roycroft’s range (chapter 20) taught the same lesson on a campus. A Lifetime settle and a mail-order rocker are not one sin. The question for this chapter is narrower: does the visible joint correspond to a load path, or is it hardware of virtue?

Albert Stickley’s Stickley Brothers in Grand Rapids sold Quaint furniture and English-looking oak that the city’s system already knew how to machine (chapter 17). Leopold and John George in Fayetteville sold Handcraft with its own marks (chapter 16). Neither factory is “Eastwood B.” Their best through-tenons are real. Their catalogs also had to meet a price. A price will ask a designer which joints stay public.

## Catalog D and the picture of structure

Catalog D’s settle 210 is 36 inches high, 84 long, 34 deep, fumed oak, a cushion in canvas or leather so large the copy says only one hide in a hundred will cover it. It is the only piece the shop shipped knocked down. The tenons are pinned. The pins are decoration and also pins. That double sentence is the Craftsman habit at its least hypocritical. They liked a structure you could see, and they liked you to like seeing it.

Harvey Ellis’s six months (chapter 12) did not invent this. The experimental New Furniture of 1900 already showed construction. The 1901–02 line thickened the posts and let tenons out. Ellis-period inlay sometimes made the public joint quieter because the surface had another story to tell. The through-tenon never required a genius. It required a decision not to hide the rail’s end.

Greene and Greene, once the Halls were on the bench, often hid the screw and showed an ebony plug that does not pin (chapter 30). That is a different ethic: the surface is modeled; the structure is millwork; the plug is rhythm. A reader who scolds the Halls for “fake pegs” and praises Stickley for “honest tenons” has not noticed that both shops are making pictures. Stickley’s picture is the end grain of the tenon. The Halls’ picture is a dark square. Neither picture is a church.

Wright’s Robie chairs (chapter 31) barely care whether you can see a tenon. The wall is the slatted back, not the joinery display. Prairie furniture can be overbuilt and still keep its fasteners to itself. Mission furniture, when it is being Mission in the 1904 sense, wants the fastener as a badge. Different jobs.

## Glue, sham, and the later refinish

A joint can be honest and still fail. Hide glue is reversible with heat and moisture; that is a conservation fact, not a how-to. A later repair that soaks a settle arm in a modern yellow glue and sanded the wedge flush has not improved the ethic. It has closed the sentence. Collectors who strip a fumed surface (chapter 35) sometimes sand the proud tenon level with the post, erasing the shadow line Eastwood wanted. The joint is still there. The picture is gone.

Sham can also be added later. A 1920s refinisher, or a 1980s dealer, can glue on “tenons” to help a plainsawn Grand Rapids armchair pass in a better room. The 1970s market (chapter 42) paid for visible joinery. Demand produces plugs. The test remains rude: does the rail come through? Can you see the end grain of the same board that makes the arm? If the “tenon” is a separate biscuit of oak, you are looking at a caption.

Pinned tenons on early Stickley Morris chairs — shop 2342, the 1902–04 reclining armchair — are a good teaching set. The front posts show the tenons of the lower rails; the arms sit on corbels; the construction is thick, a little proud, a little handmade in the way a factory still using more hand time looks handmade. Later chairs streamline the same grammar. The ethic does not require pride of surface. It requires correspondence.

## What the ethic cannot do

It cannot pay a wage. Boris’s *Art and Labor* will not let a tenon stand in for a payroll (chapter 2). Eastwood remained Stickley’s shop. Grand Rapids remained a manufacturers’ association that, in 1911, refused to bargain as the strike ran seventeen weeks (chapter 39). A beautiful joint on a chair made under piecework is still a beautiful joint. It is not a proof that the man who cut it owned his day.

It cannot make a style. A through-tenon on a Colonial Revival table in 1924 is a through-tenon. A through-tenon on a mail-order Mission rocker is a through-tenon. The joint is older than Ruskin and older than Syracuse. What 1901 added was a magazine willing to say the joint was a character. What 1906 added was a market willing to buy the character as a silhouette.

It cannot survive a religion of the unglued joint. Some later crafts talk treats any adhesive as a fall. Medieval furniture used glue. Japanese joinery, which American writers like to cite when they want purity, uses complex shoulders and also paste. The American factory argument was never “no glue.” It was “do not pretend.” A dowel that admits it is a dowel is cleaner than a plug that pretends to be a tenon. A glued through-tenon that you can see is cleaner than an unglued secret if the secret is a staple.

The residual fact is small enough to keep. On the settle arm, the wedge is a piece of oak standing across another piece of oak. If you can feel the wedge, the shop was willing to let you check the work. If you feel a flat plug that matches the stain too perfectly and meets no rail, the shop — or a later shop — was willing to let you believe. Belief is not a joint. The next essay is the brown that made those tenon-ends look like one wood: ammonia vapor on tannin, the 1904 color, and the refinish that strips the story without touching the wedge.

A keyed tenon — a through-tenon with a wedge driven across the projecting end — is the settle’s loudest version of the same sentence. The key is a third piece. It can loosen. It can be replaced. It photographs well, which is why later reproductions love it. Photographing well is not a vice. It is a warning. The 1904 shop that left the key proud also left a shadow. A 1990s shop that machines a key flush with a router has kept the silhouette and lost the check. The ethic, if the word is still allowed, lives in the check, not in the silhouette. Grand Rapids could cut the silhouette by 1906. The check was slower and more expensive. That is why the two chairs can look related and still not be the same work.


A keyed tenon on a settle arm is the loud version of the same check: a third piece driven across the projecting end, proud enough to cast a shadow. Grand Rapids could cut the silhouette by 1906. The shadow cost more. Later reproductions love the silhouette and mill the key flush. The ethic, if the word is still allowed, lives in the check.


Pinned early Morris chairs (shop 2342) teach the same correspondence: the rail you sit above is the rail you can see in the post. Correspondence is the ethic. Pride of surface is optional.
