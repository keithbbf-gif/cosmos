# Ammonia and the Brown of 1904

Two oak panels, same species, same mill, same year if the dealer is telling the truth. One is a cool brown. The ray fleck and the ground are nearly the same tone, distinguished by texture: the ray a little glassier, the earlywood a little open. The other is a warmer brown. The rays stand pale and the pores are dark, as if someone had outlined the figure with a pen. One was colored by ammonia vapor acting on the tannin in white oak. One was colored by a pigment stain sitting in the pores. A later refinish can make them look like cousins. They are not.

This chapter is a comparison, not a method. Shops in the first years of the Craftsman line used ammonia fuming as a factory process. *The Craftsman* for October 1905 printed Stickley’s account of how a home worker might approach oak color; the same essay admits that the Eastwood plant had “greater facilities” and did not simply copy the household paragraph. Later finishers, and some later factories, reached for dye and stain because vapor is hard to control. The brown of 1904 — the year this series uses as a handle for the mature early color — is a chemical story in the wood. It is not a recipe for a basement.

## What the vapor was doing

White oak (*Quercus alba*, chapter 33) holds tannins. Ammonia vapor, in a closed chamber the factory could manage, reacts with those tannins and changes the color of the wood itself. The hard, glossy medullary ray and the softer tissue take the change together. Stickley’s published preference is explicit: he wanted the figure marked by texture, not by a stained outline. A pigment stain lodges in the open pores and leaves the ray relatively untouched, so the flake “pops.” The 1904 eye did not always want a pop. It wanted a board that looked as if time, not a brush, had been there.

The color is a cool brown, sometimes with a green or olive cast before later warm coats went on. Catalogs and later writers call it fumed oak. The word is accurate if the piece was actually exposed to vapor. It is advertising if the piece was dyed to imitate the vapor. By about 1906, several historians of the Eastwood finish — Cathers among the cautious — see a shift toward aniline dyes and early lacquers as production grew `[VERIFY the 1906 turn as a plant-wide change rather than a mixed practice]`. Stickley never published the factory’s private formula. The October 1905 essay is a public paragraph. The plant is a different sentence.

Other American shops fumed. Grand Rapids knew the process; so did smaller Mission makers who wanted the Eastwood photograph. Not every dark oak chair from 1908 is fumed. Many are stained, filled, and varnished in the older golden-oak tradition with the hue shifted toward brown. The comparison panels at the head of this chapter are the test. If the ray and the ground are kin, vapor or a very even dye is in the story. If the ray is a pale lightning bolt in a dark field, a pigment has been at work.

## 1904 as a color year

1904 is not a magic date. It is a useful one. Harvey Ellis had just died; the inlay chairs were still on the floor (chapter 12). Catalog D was in the world. The Homebuilders’ Club had begun (chapter 13). The furniture had settled into the heavy, public-jointed forms this series keeps returning to. The brown those forms wore is the brown collectors mean when they say “original finish” and hope they are not lying.

Original is a pile of coats. Vapor first — if vapor was used — then, in many accounts, a film: shellac, an early lacquer, wax. Uneven boards, especially sapwood, needed touching up; Stickley’s public essay allows a dye in the film for that. A chair can therefore be fumed and dyed. The hierarchy still matters. The vapor colored the tannin. The dye corrected the board. A later gel stain poured over stripped oak is not a correction. It is a new object.

Leather and canvas sit on top of the brown and age on their own clocks. A 1904 chair with replaced leather and intact film on the arms is still, in the wood, a 1904 color problem. A 1904 chair with original tacks and a 1978 “Mission oak” stain on sanded arms is a 1978 color problem that happens to have old tacks. Dealers blur these clocks because a single adjective is easier to price.

The Eastwood plant did not begin as a fuming shop. The factory study that reads the 1901 inventory puts serious vapor work late that year. The January 1902 count is eighteen fumed pieces, all samples (chapter 11). That is a trial, not a medieval inheritance. Early color notes also mention gray-brown, green, and a darker gray — color-effects “largely from wood,” in the plant’s language. The brown of 1904 is what the trial became once the line thickened and the catalogs needed a word.

## What a stain is doing instead

A pigment stain is particles in a binder. They catch in pores, scratches, and the soft earlywood. They sit on the ray as a thin, often reluctant film. The photograph is dramatic. The board looks “figured” even if the mill did not quartersaw carefully. That is why a factory that wanted the look without the chamber liked stain. It is also why a refinisher in 1960, facing a piece whose film had failed, reached for a can that said oak.

Aniline dyes — dissolved color, not particles — sink more evenly and can mimic a fumed tone without the ray going pale. They also fade. A window-sill arm that was dyed in 1910 and lived in sun will tell you the truth the catalog did not. Vapor color, because it is a change in the tannin, is less of a surface fade and more of a wood fact. Conservators still argue about how to describe that permanence `[CITE NEEDED: a specific conservation paper]`. The argument is already enough to warn a writer off certainty.

Fillers complicate every comparison. Oak pores are large. A factory that filled the pores before coloring, or after, changed the way light hits the board. Some Craftsman surfaces look open and dry; some look satiny. Both can be original. A later polyurethane that fills every pore and reflects like a bar top is not original, even if the tint is “fumed oak” from a hardware aisle. The sheen is part of the 1904 brown. The brown without the sheen is a different brown.

## The refinish that strips the story

The collector market of the 1970s and 1980s (chapter 42) paid a premium for original surface and also created a fashion for “cleaning up” chairs that looked too dark, too dirty, too much like grandmother. A stripper does not ask whether the brown was in the tannin or in a stain. It takes what it can. Sandpaper takes the rest, including the proud edges of through-tenons (chapter 34) and the mill’s slight irregularities. What remains is fresh oak. Fresh oak is handsome. It is not 1904.

Some refinishers then “restored” the look with a stain chosen from a 1980s chart. The ray pops. The chair photographs well. The comparison panel on the right has replaced the comparison panel on the left, on the same object. A buyer who sees only the later chair will think fumed oak always looked like outlined flake. That error now travels in auction catalogs. It is a visual error with a chemical cause.

There is a quieter loss. Vapor color varies with the tree. Two slats from two logs will not match perfectly. Early Eastwood chairs sometimes show that variation as a kind of honesty the magazine would have liked if it had been willing to print a mismatch. A later refinish evens the chair into a single store brown. The tree is gone twice: once in 1904, when it was cut, and again when its differences were sanded off.

Do not try to put the vapor back. This is not a caution in the style of a magazine project page. It is a historical fact about the objects. The factory chamber, the plant’s ventilation, the strength of the reagent, the time, the species, the sapwood — those were industrial variables. They are not a household amusement. Ammonia in the concentrations shops used is a hazardous material. This series will not list strengths, times, containers, or steps. Readers who came here for a method should leave without one.

## What the brown meant in the room

The 1904 living hall wanted a low light and a fire. Dark oak reads as quiet in that light. Golden oak, the previous fashionable factory color, read as varnish and display — the Eastlake parlor’s grandchild (chapter 3). The Craftsman argument against golden oak is an argument against a shine that competed with the grain. Fuming, when it worked, made the grain the shine. Stain-and-varnish Mission, when it was hurried, made a brown version of the old shine. Both sold. They did not mean the same thing in a room.

Greene and Greene mahogany, oiled and modeled in the Hall shop, is another brown entirely (chapter 30). Roycroft oak runs a range from campus-careful to production-dark. Rohlfs is not in this color story. Wright’s Robie oak (chapter 31) is stained to the house, not fumed to a catalog. If you find yourself saying “Arts and Crafts brown” as if it were one chip on a card, you have already lost the comparison this chapter began with.

Winterthur’s Stickley papers and the Dallas 2010 object entries are useful here because they sometimes say, in a curator’s short clause, whether a film is believed original. A raking light will do more than a caption. Fumed oak and stained oak can look related in a catalog photograph and disagree when the light rakes: the stain sits on the ray as a thin reluctance; the vapor goes with the tannin. A later oil finish on a stripped chair is a third brown, and it is the commonest brown in the 1980s market. Do not call that third brown original. Do not publish a shop method for making the first. Keep the chamber in the factory, in 1904, and out of any kitchen.

The residual fact is the pair of panels. Left: ray and ground as kin, a cool brown, texture doing the work of outline. Right: ray as lightning, pores as ink. A museum armchair with an intact film will sit on the left even if a later cushion sits on the seat. A handsome “restored” settle on a showroom floor will often sit on the right and still be called original in a voice that has not looked. Collectors who sand to “fresh oak” are sanding off 1904. A raking light on an unstripped armchair is still the only comparison that does not lie for a camera. The film is the story; the vapor is a factory history; both stay in the past tense. Do not publish a household method. The kitchen is not a finishing room. The next essay leaves color for comfort: the American Morris chair, a shop number that borrowed an English type and then had to decide whether a manifesto could recline.
