# 29th Street and the Crash

The lease paper in Winterthur’s COL 60 is not a morality play. It is a loft lease. Printed copies of typical floor plans for buildings at 5 East 38th Street and 6 East 39th Street sit in the file. The through-block loft opened to the public in May 1913 as the Craftsman Building: twelve stories, showrooms, a drafting studio, the magazine’s offices, a restaurant on the top floor, and a name Stickley liked enough to print — Headquarters for Home Builders. The chapter title still says 29th Street because that is how a certain kind of New York mouth still locates the old wholesale furniture belt, the blocks that run from the Twenties toward the Forties, west of Fifth. The address on the letterhead was 6 East 39th Street, a few steps off Fifth, with a second face at 5 East 38th for freight. `[VERIFY: 6 East 39th / 5 East 38th as the paired entries; twelve stories in most accounts, eleven in one National Register sentence.]` If you go looking for 29th Street, you will find a different set of lofts. If you go looking for the crash, you will find 39th.

The lease was long. A twenty-year term valued in later summaries at about $1.3 million is the figure that travels; first-year rent of $50,000 and $61,000 thereafter is the figure that travels with it. `[VERIFY both figures against the executed lease in COL 60, not against secondary retellings.]` Either number is a midtown number. It is not an Eastwood number. Gustav Stickley had moved the magazine’s offices to New York in 1905. In 1912–13 he folded the shops, the publishing company, the house-plan operation, and the farm into The Craftsman, Inc. The building was the visible corporation: a place a customer could walk through from a pile of lumber to a finished room to a lunch grown in Morris Plains.

## What twelve floors were for

Emily M. Orr’s 2017 essay in the *Journal of Modern Craft* is the recent account that treats the building as an object, not as a cautionary tale with a roof. Stickley wanted object lessons, model rooms, a public view of making, and a restaurant whose kitchen could be inspected. The January 1914 *Craftsman* introduced readers to “New York’s Newest Institution for the Service of the Public” with a plate of the building (volume 25, number 4). Education is the word Orr keeps. Education, in this setting, meant selling a house and a chair in the same afternoon.

The floors divided the argument. Lower stories held furniture and furnishings a homebuilder could order. Somewhere in the stack — accounts differ on the exact floor list `[CITE NEEDED: a contemporary floor-by-floor directory]` — were building materials, garden exhibits, and the Craftsman Homebuilders Exposition, advertised as a treasure-house for the homelover. Drafting rooms made the club of 1904 (chapter 13) into a room you could stand in. A workshop made Eastwood briefly visible in Manhattan. The twelfth floor was the Craftsman Restaurant. Dairy, vegetables, and spring water came from the New Jersey tract by truck. Alfred W. McCann, a food reformer, used the kitchen as a model of sanitation and “pure food.” The restaurant opened with the building in May 1913 and was gone a few months short of three years later, when the retail business failed.

This is not a farm chapter. The farm is in chapter 14. The restaurant matters here because it is a line item: another payroll, another lease inside the lease, another promise that a corporation could be a life. Winterthur’s biographical note still mentions farm products on the top floor in the same paragraph as the March 1915 bankruptcy. The paragraph is right to keep them together.

## The numbers that came due

On 24 March 1915 Gustav Stickley was declared bankrupt. The date is in COL 60, in the Winterthur finding aid, in the Stickley Museum’s short history, and in a *New York Times* story whose clipping collectors still file as “24 MAR 1915.” The museum’s sentence is the one this series will carry unless a better ledger appears: he owed nearly a quarter of a million dollars. Later retellings give $229,000 in liabilities and $123,000 in assets, sometimes attached to a 1916 follow-up rather than to the March morning. `[VERIFY the Times figures and the date on the page; do not prefer a secondary $229,000 / $123,000 split until the clipping is read.]` Creditors’ letters, cash vouchers for 1915–16, and the dissolution papers of the Craftsman Publishing Company fill boxes 1–15A at Winterthur. Those boxes are the crash. They are not a mood.

A twenty-year midtown lease, mortgages on the Morris Plains tract, a magazine whose circulation was no longer climbing, and a furniture market that had already learned how to print “Mission” in Grand Rapids and in a Sears catalog: that is the set of pressures a historian can name without pretending to sit in Stickley’s head. The set is enough. “Overreach” is a sermon that can be preached about any failed lease. It becomes history only when the rent, the debt, and the last issue are on the table. Fifty thousand dollars a year in 1913 is more, one later writer noted, than David and Mary Gamble paid for their Greene and Greene house in Pasadena. `[VERIFY the comparison; useful if the two figures are both firm, ornamental if not.]` Whether or not the comparison holds, the rent was real. The Pasadena house is still a house. The Craftsman Building is still a loft, its 39th Street face altered, its 38th Street terra cotta easier to recognize.

Receivership followed the adjudication. Henry — Henkle in the surviving petition titles — appears in June 1915 paperwork about Washington store assets and a composition with creditors `[VERIFY the receiver’s name and the Washington branch against the 16 June and 21 June 1915 files]`. The corporation did not vanish on 24 March. It wound down. Stock that had sat on the showroom floors was sold at Gimbels in August 1916 at advertised reductions of 35 to 50 percent. The restaurant and the store had already gone dark earlier that year. *The Craftsman* printed through December 1916. Fifteen years and three months after volume 1, number 1 (October 1901), the monthly argument stopped. The last issue is a fact. It is not a eulogy.

## What the building was not

It was not Eastwood. The factory in the Syracuse suburb still made the chairs (chapter 11). Manhattan displayed them, explained them, and added a lunch. Confusing the two addresses is how later writers turn a manufacturer into a shopkeeper who forgot his shop. Stickley did not forget Eastwood. He added a cost center that Eastwood could not carry once credit tightened.

It was not the Popular Shop. McHugh’s Fifth Avenue rooms (chapters 1 and 6) had been a decorator’s business that manufactured a line. The Craftsman Building was a manufacturer’s business that tried to become a department of living. The difference is visible in the restaurant. McHugh did not need to feed you in order to sell you a settle. Stickley, by 1913, wanted the meal to prove the settle.

It was not failure the day it opened. May 1913 is a crowded, confident month in the magazine. Model rooms were installed. The Homebuilders’ Club had a headquarters with a street door. Fifty-odd furniture franchises, if the Winterthur note is right, still had goods to sell. A visitor could watch a draftsman and then buy a table. For two years the experiment ran. That is longer than Ellis lasted at Eastwood and shorter than a twenty-year lease. Duration is one of the numbers. Two years of midtown rent on a twenty-year paper is a particular kind of arithmetic.

## After the adjudication

The farm sold in 1917 (chapter 14). Leopold and John George, who had been competitors in Fayetteville since the Onondaga Shops years (chapter 16), appear in a 1918 *Furniture World* notice with Leopold as president, Gustav as vice president, and J. George as treasurer (chapter 18). Gustav was gone from that listing a year later. He lived until 1942. The *Times* obituary, when it came, was short and misspelled his name. That last fact is often told as pathos. Pathos is not a liability schedule. The 1915 schedule is the document.

What closed in 1915–16 was not “the Arts and Crafts movement.” Roycroft was still making oak and copper in East Aurora (chapters 19–20). L. & J.G. were still making Handcraft and then “the work of L. & J.G. Stickley.” Albert’s Quaint lines were still coming out of Grand Rapids. Greene and Greene’s Pasadena work was already a decade old. What closed was one corporation’s ability to pay its midtown rent and its New Jersey mortgage at the same time that a taste for brown oak was meeting a taste for other woods. Chapter 41 will take the 1920s. This chapter will not borrow that decade to explain a 1915 filing.

The Craftsman Building’s later lives — offices, a bar in the 38th Street face, a façade on 39th that no longer reads as 1913 — are ordinary Manhattan. The Headquarters for Home Builders lasted as long as the corporation could write checks. Orr is right to recover the educational ambition. COL 60 is right to keep the creditors’ letters. A series that wants the chairs without the rent is doing what Stickley’s own magazine sometimes did: printing the room and omitting the bill.

## A last number, then the brothers

December 1916. That is the last *Craftsman*. March 24, 1915, is the adjudication. Nearly $250,000 is the debt the museum is willing to say in a paragraph. $50,000 is the first year’s rent if the secondary figure holds. Twelve floors. Two street numbers. Twenty years promised, two years run, a third year of winding down. The chairs that left Eastwood before the filing did not become worse chairs. The plates in the 1909 house book did not become worse plates. The corporation that signed the lease became a file in Winterthur.

The next essays leave Gustav’s letterhead and walk the other Stickley shops: Leopold and John George in Fayetteville, Albert in Grand Rapids, then the two price lists that sat on the same buyer’s desk. The crash is not their origin story. It is the moment the oldest brother’s paper stopped being able to pretend it was the only paper in the family.

A building that still stands on 39th Street can tempt a pilgrim. The pilgrimage is optional. The Winterthur box is not. Creditors’ letters do more for this chapter than a façade. The oak from Eastwood did not fail because the posts were weak. It failed, in 1915, because a lease and a taste change arrived in the same season.
