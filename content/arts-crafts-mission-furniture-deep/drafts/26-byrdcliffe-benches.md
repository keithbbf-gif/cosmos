# Byrdcliffe’s Benches

The settle at White Pines is a low oak length with a carved panel in the back, the kind of seating a colony puts in a hall when it has decided that sitting should look like a drawing. The panel is a plant — lily, iris, chestnut, sassafras; the Byrdcliffe shop did not use one cartoon for every board — cut in shallow relief and stained so the leaf sits a little apart from the oak. The house around it is the Whiteheads’ house, dark pine siding, a ribbon of windows painted the blue the campus still calls Byrdcliffe blue. Ralph Radcliffe Whitehead (1854–1929) and Jane Byrd McCall Whitehead (1861–1955) named the colony from their middle names and the house from the trees. They did not name a successful factory. They named a place on the shoulder of Mount Guardian, outside Woodstock, where furniture was supposed to pay for a brotherhood and did not.

This is not the McHugh chapter. A branded Popular Shop chair did sit at White Pines — Jane bought it in New York, and D’Ambrosio published it — but that chair is how a name travels, not how a colony designed. Chapter 1 used the brand. The benches, chests, and chairs that left the Byrdcliffe woodshop in 1903–05 are a different authorship: drawings by Zulma Steele and Edna Walker, cases that later writers have tried to give to any man standing nearby, a production Winterthur’s finding aid is willing to count. In 1904, the busiest year, about fifty pieces. Tables, chairs, lamp stands, shelves, bookcases, sideboards, chiffoniers. Furniture making lasted a couple of years. Then the community went on as pottery, weaving, frames, and a summer place. That sequence is the essay’s problem. A colony is not a plant in Holland.

## Two failed coasts and a Catskill shelf

Whitehead was the son of a Yorkshire mill owner and had read Ruskin at Oxford. Jane Byrd McCall came from a prominent Philadelphia family and painted. They married in 1892 and tried, before Woodstock, to plant a community in California and in Oregon. Those tries failed. In 1902, with the writer Hervey White and the artist Bolton Brown in the search party, Whitehead bought land at the foot of Mount Guardian — the later civic number is fifteen hundred acres; Winterthur’s introduction says about a thousand in 1901 `[VERIFY: acreage in the deed]`. Construction began that winter. By the summer of 1903 some thirty buildings were up: shops for metal and wood, a library, guest houses, a dairy, the White Pines house. Nancy E. Green’s 2004 exhibition catalog, *Byrdcliffe: An American Arts and Crafts Colony* (Herbert F. Johnson Museum / Cornell), is the modern gathering of the story. The Milwaukee Art Museum’s later brochure and *The Magazine Antiques* “Looking back at Byrdcliffe” are the shorter public versions.

Whitehead published an intention in 1903, a sentence the Met’s drawing record still quotes: furniture of a simple kind, good in proportion, given distinction by color and carving from artists’ hands. The sentence is a colony sentence. It assumes artists. It assumes a shop that can wait for a leaf. It does not assume a 272-item booklet.

Bolton Brown found the site. Hervey White helped imagine the brotherhood. Both men fell out with Whitehead in the usual way utopian staffs fall out. The Woodstock Guild’s later history is frank about temperament and the demise of the “brotherhood of artists.” The furniture does not need the quarrel except as a date. The shop’s productive months sit inside the quarrel. They are not a footnote to it.

## Steele, Walker, and who designed the leaf

Zulma Steele (1881–1979) and Edna Walker (born 1880) arrived in the summer of 1903 from Pratt, where they had studied with Arthur Wesley Dow. By 1904 they were sharing a house on the campus called the Angelus. They designed the decorations that were carved or painted onto the furniture: Steele’s lily, used on chairs, chests, a dining table that later burned with Bolton Brown’s house Carniola; Walker’s sassafras on the linen press that went to the Metropolitan Museum (1991.311.1). The Met press is oak and tulip poplar, brass hardware, about 1904, 55 × 41 × 18¾ inches, pale green stain, the Whitehead family still attached to it when the museum bought it. The outline drawing of the same press (1991.311.3) is graphite, 1904. The museum’s note gives Whitehead the outline of the case and Walker the leaves. Other writers, looking at the Winterthur drawings, are less sure the case outlines are his. Robert Lang, who has published on the drawings, has said the sexist default was to hand any recognizable male the design and leave the women as “decorators.” The drawings at Winterthur, some marked “Ralph Whitehead personal copy,” are shop-used paper with dimensions. They do not settle a modern authorship fight. They do make Steele and Walker unavoidable.

The lily chairs are the benches’ cousins and, in the sale rooms, the objects that stand in for sitting. One is in Milwaukee (Layton), one at Munson-Williams-Proctor, one at Winterthur. A fourth, branded BYRDCLIFFE, cherry, leather, brass tacks, was still in private hands when Sotheby’s offered it in 2013. The splat is Steele’s lily. The colony used the lily as a cypher as well as a carving. A reader looking for a “Byrdcliffe settle” in the White Pines sense is looking at the same grammar on a longer rail: rectilinear case, local wood, a plant from the Catskill slope, a transparent stain. Poplar and oak were the common species; cherry and mahogany appear. The English comparison is real and limited. What might have been a thistle in Glasgow became, in Woodstock, a chestnut or a sassafras. That is reception, not a Cotswold transplant. Chapter 27 will have to refuse a similar cliché for Pennsylvania. Byrdcliffe’s refusal is botanical.

Jane Whitehead designed too — pottery, local flora — and later studied with Frederick Hurten Rhead in California. Jessie Tarbox Beals photographed. The Winterthur collection includes Beals negatives among more than six hundred other images. Furniture, Whitehead decided, would be the predominant craft. Intention is not output. Fifty pieces in the best year is a shop. It is not Grand Rapids.

## White Pines, and the other oak in the room

White Pines was the main house, the place later visitors mean when they say they sat at Byrdcliffe. The colony’s own benches and settles were made for that house and for the other cottages — Carniola, the Angelus — and for a hoped-for sale in New York. Self-sufficiency was the goal; a woodshop that could stock city stores was the plan. The plan failed. What did not fail, immediately, was the look of the rooms. A Green 2004 plate, or a Beals photograph, will show the rectilinear oak in a pine interior that is already a summer colony, not a factory town.

The McHugh chair in the same rooms is the fact chapter 1 used and this chapter must not lead with. Jane Byrd McCall Whitehead bought Mission at The Popular Shop and put it under White Pines guests. A colony that preached handmade leaves could still sit on a New York factory rail. That is not hypocrisy in the cheap sense. It is how American Arts and Crafts actually furnished itself: a few self-made objects, a few purchased ones, a great deal of talk. The Byrdcliffe benches are the self-made objects. They are rarer than the talk.

Nancy Green’s 2004 catalog remains the place that treats the colony as a collaboration rather than as Whitehead’s hobby. The Milwaukee brochure is useful on Steele as a “leading force” and on Zedware, the later pottery name Steele and Walker built from their initials. This series is a furniture series. Zedware is a pointer, not a chapter. The pointer matters because it shows the two designers staying after the woodshop’s couple of years. The benches did not become a line. The people did not all leave.

## Why the factory never happened

A factory, in the Grand Rapids sense, needs a catalog, a repeating number, a salesman, and a willingness to drop a model that does not telegraph well. Byrdcliffe had drawings, a brand used on signed pieces from 1904 onward (the Sotheby’s note warns that the 1904 brand appears on later-looking objects too), and a founder who had read Ruskin. It did not have Limbert’s two hundred and fifty men. It did not have Lifetime’s 272 Cloister items. When the skilled, slow work of carving a sassafras door proved to be a cost rather than an income, the shop stopped being the center. Winterthur is dry about the sequel: pottery, weaving, frame making. Ralph joined Jane at the kiln and worried about glazes. He also wove. The brotherhood became a seasonal arts colony, which is a different American institution, and a more durable one. The Woodstock Guild still occupies the ground. That durability is not a furniture success.

Robert W. Clark’s 1972 Princeton exhibition had already put Byrdcliffe on the national Arts and Crafts map. Green 2004 thickened the map. Neither book asks the reader to treat White Pines as Eastwood. A reader who came from a Mission search may still want to know whether the benches are “Mission.” They are Arts and Crafts in the society sense: reform, hand, a plant from the neighborhood, a refusal of factory gingerbread. They are not Mission in the McHugh sense. They are not Craftsman. They are Byrdcliffe, which is a place-name that never quite became a trade-name, which is why the word still needs a paragraph.

## A residual leaf

The Met linen press, which is not a bench, is the object a museum visitor will actually see. The lily chair is the object a sale-room visitor will see. The White Pines settle is the object this chapter insisted on because the house is where the colony’s furniture was meant to be used, not photographed as a trophy. All three share a shallow plant and a boxy case. All three were made in a window of two or three years on a Catskill shelf that could not become a mill town. Whitehead died in Santa Barbara in 1929. Jane lived until 1955. Steele painted in Woodstock for decades. The benches stayed few. The McHugh chair, if it is still in the story at all, is a guest. The leaf on the splat is the host.

Fifty pieces in a busy year, if Winterthur’s finding aid is right, is not a factory. It is a season. A reader who wants a national oak look will not find it on the Catskill shelf. They will find a linen press in the Met and a lily chair in Milwaukee and a settle that still belongs to a house. That is a colony’s proper scale. It is also why Byrdcliffe cannot answer a Mission search except by refusing the word.
