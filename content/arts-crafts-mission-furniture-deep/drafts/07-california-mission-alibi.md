# The California Mission as a Furniture Alibi

The chairs in the sanctuary at Lyon and Washington are maple, rush-seated, built without a show of nails. A. J. Forbes — Alexander J. Forbes in the Los Angeles County Museum of Art’s later label — offered, in a proposal dated 1 November 1894, to make them at $4.50 each, tule rush from the Sacramento–San Joaquin delta woven for the seats. He told the minister he had not made a rush seat in thirty-five years and thought he could remember. About eighty chairs were made for the room. The church, the Church of the New Jerusalem, opened for worship on 17 March 1895. A. Page Brown’s office was the architect of record. The Reverend Joseph Worcester had chosen the lot and the unpeeled madrone trunks from the Santa Cruz Mountains that hold the roof. LACMA later acquired a related side chair, designed 1894 and made about 1896 after the church designs `[VERIFY accession; do not invent a number]`. The object this chapter starts on is the sanctuary seat, in the building, in 1895. Everything later called Mission furniture that points at a California mission as its workshop is pointing at the wrong door.

The door on Lyon Street is a portico with Mission clay tiles and brick arches. That tile is an architectural quotation. It is not a furniture factory. The chairs are church chairs for a Swedenborgian congregation. The name that left San Francisco and became a New York brand is the theft.

## Worcester, Brown, and a room that is not a sacristy

Joseph Worcester (1836–1913), Boston- and Harvard-trained, son of a Swedenborgian minister, bought the lot in 1894 — the church’s own later address is 2107 Lyon at Washington; the Landmark nomination files the complex at 3200 Washington, northwest corner of Washington and Lyon, Pacific Heights. The building contract with a Mr. Gallagher was signed 20 August 1894. On 20 September a wagon brought madrone from Glen Ranch in the Santa Cruz Mountains. Brown’s specifications for “the Swedenborgian Church at Lyon and Washington Streets” survive in the church files: brick colors, rafter sizes, a preference for the rougher and darker shades. Brown died in 1896 after a carriage accident, a year after the dedication. A. C. Schweinfurth, in the office, is the designer many later writers give the largest architectural share. Bernard Maybeck, a draughtsman in Brown’s San Francisco office in the early 1890s, may have touched the plan. Worcester touched everything he could reach: the asymmetrical hearth, the pulpit placement, the idea that a church should feel like a room in nature rather than a stone quotation of Europe.

The sanctuary is board-and-batten Oregon pine (Douglas fir) below, hand-worked lime plaster above, a ceiling of diagonal redwood, iron U-brackets on the madrone trusses. William Keith’s paintings occupy the north wall. Bruce Porter’s stained glass — a dove in the east oculus, Saint Christopher in the south — occupies the light. Mary Curtis Richardson is given the pulpit and communion table. A copper-and-mica shade in the vestry is attributed to Dirk van Erp. The *San Francisco Call* of 3 April 1895 called the building a quaint church edifice. The National Historic Landmark nomination of 2004 called it one of California’s earliest pure Arts and Crafts buildings. Neither document called it a Spanish mission.

An iron cross in the garden came from Mission San Miguel and was given to Worcester. That is the only Franciscan object the complex honestly owns. It is a relic in a garden, not a chair in a sacristy. The chairs are maple. California missions, when they furnished rooms, used what a frontier church could get: adobe context, painted wood, imported pieces, later tourist restorations that are themselves a twentieth-century problem. They did not run a factory of through-tenon oak for a New York catalog. Treating the Lyon Street seats as “mission furniture” in the Spanish sense is a category error with a sales force behind it.

## What Forbes built, and who may have drawn it

The designer of the chairs is not settled. Freudenheim and Sussman, in *Building with Nature* (1974), say the maple rush-seated chairs were designed for the space and leave the draughtsman open. Adrienne Kopa, cited in the Landmark nomination, gives the design to A. Page Brown and the making to Forbes. The New Fillmore’s later popular account gives Forbes the making and Worcester the insistences: old-fashioned seats, tule, no nails. Forbes’s 23 January 1895 letter, “My Dear Dr. Worcester,” is in the church files. A furniture history that needs a single author will pick one of these names and stop. A furniture history that wants the chairs will say: Worcester wanted a certain seat, Brown’s office was drawing the building, Forbes was the craftsman who priced the job and taught the rush work, and the chairs exist.

They are not oak. That fact alone should have stopped the later Mission-oak genealogy. They are maple, with flared feet, a simple post-and-rail logic, a woven seat. Handmade, the church literature says, without nails. LACMA’s example, made about 1896 after the church designs, is the museum witness that the pattern left the sanctuary and could be repeated. Maybeck later put related chairs at Wyntoon for Phoebe Apperson Hearst and in the William P. Rieger house. Schweinfurth put them in the Volney C. Moody house. A Bay Area office style, then: a straight chair for a timber interior. Not a Spanish colonial survival. Not a Stickley prototype, though popular paragraphs still force that paternity.

The Guild of Arts and Crafts of San Francisco formed in September 1894, while the church was going up — six years after the English exhibition society, three years before Boston’s 1897 society if the Fillmore chronology holds `[VERIFY first meeting dates against Trapp 1993]`. Worcester’s circle is the weather in which the chairs make sense: a reform congregation, a local joiner, a minister who had read the same English books chapter 2 tracked, and a building that wanted to look as if it had grown on the lot. Ruskin would have recognized the beamed room more quickly than he would have recognized a Sears suite. He would not have recognized a New York brand.

## Gump, photographs, and the trip east

S. & G. Gump, the San Francisco art-goods house (Solomon and Gustave Gump; the firm dates to the 1860s), sold Japanese objects, frames, and the sort of portable taste a West Coast city could ship. Later writers put Gump on the path between the Lyon Street chairs and Joseph P. McHugh’s New York shop: photographs, perhaps a chair, a conversation in a store that already knew how to move a picture of an object across a continent. The paper trail from Gump’s counter to McHugh’s board is the sentence that still wants a letter `[VERIFY in D’Ambrosio and in Gump archives; do not invent one]`. What does not want a letter is the larger fact. Images of the chairs moved. McHugh, by his own story, saw photographs of church chairs, took them for mission furniture, and put a line into production (chapter 6). D’Ambrosio treats that story as a claim. This chapter treats the claim as a misreading with a motive.

The motive is a noun. Mission, in 1890s California, already meant something in architecture. Mission Revival — stucco, tile roofs, a bell-wall quotation of the Franciscan chain — was becoming a public style for stations, schools, and houses that wanted to look as if they belonged to the state. A bungalow could wear that skin and hold any furniture at all. Robert Winter’s bungalow books and the Oakland / Abbeville California Arts and Crafts catalog (Trapp, 1993) keep the house-style and the furniture-style from pretending to be one inheritance. McHugh’s shop needed a word that sounded Western, old, and simple. Mission was sitting on the table. The Swedenborgian chairs were simple. The tiles on Worcester’s portico were Mission tiles. A man in New York who saw a photograph of a straight chair in a tiled, beamed California building had every retail reason to say mission and no documentary reason to mean San Juan Capistrano.

Gump’s role, even if the letter never turns up, is the San Francisco version of The Popular Shop: an art-goods counter that could turn a local object into a transferable image. Japanese prints had already traveled that counter. A maple chair could travel it too. The theft is not that a photograph moved. Photographs are supposed to move. The theft is the caption.

## Architecture’s Mission, furniture’s alibi

Mission Revival architecture has a real genealogy: the Franciscan chain, the romantic photographs of ruined adobe, the late-nineteenth-century decision to make those ruins a state style. Furniture’s Mission has a fake genealogy: the suggestion that the through-tenon and the slatted oak back came out of those rooms. They did not. Spanish colonial and Mexican workshop furniture in California is its own subject — hide, carved wood, imported pieces, later revival reproductions for tourists. It does not produce McHugh’s X-braced magazine stand. It does not produce Stickley’s shop number 155. It does not produce a Grand Rapids suite. Using the missions as an alibi let a New York decorator, and then a national trade, attach piety and place to a factory look. The alibi worked because Americans wanted the West to furnish the East with simplicity. A church in Pacific Heights, designed by a Boston-trained minister and a Beaux-Arts office, was not Western enough once the caption said mission. The caption needed friars.

Stickley, when he bothered, dismissed Mission as a misleading word. He had his own word. Grand Rapids used Mission because it sold. Sears used Mission because it sold. The architectural style and the furniture style shared a color — brown, stucco or oak — and a decade. They did not share a joiner. A Pasadena bungalow with a tile roof and a Stickley settle is two inheritances in one room. Winter is the writer who keeps that sentence clean. This chapter only has to keep the chairs clean. They are Forbes’s, in Worcester’s building, from Brown’s office. They are not a Spanish workshop survival. They are not the source of the through-tenon as ethics (chapter 34). They are the source of a photograph that a New York shop was willing to misfile.

## What the chairs are, if they are not an origin myth

They are early Bay Area Arts and Crafts seating: local wood, a rush the minister wanted, a price ($4.50) that is a craftsman’s bid and not a catalog number. They belong with Keith’s paintings and Porter’s glass as part of a single interior. That is the English Arts and Crafts idea America actually used well — a room as a collaboration — without needing a Cotswold village. The First Bay Tradition literature (Freudenheim and Sussman; the Landmark nomination; later Maybeck studies) is the right shelf. The Mission-oak price guide is the wrong shelf.

A reader who came here from a search for Mission furniture will want a through-tenon. It is not in this sanctuary. The joint logic is simpler and, if the no-nail claim holds, older in a different way: a chair a church could commission from a man who remembered rush work. The American trade that borrowed the photograph added oak, leather, a brand, and a story about friars. The story is the alibi. The photograph was of these maple seats, under these madrone trunks, in a church dedicated in 1895 to a Swedish mystic’s American congregation. That is already a strange enough American fact. It does not need a sacristy.

The next essay leaves California and goes back to the years before *The Craftsman*: Osceola, Brandt, Binghamton, the Auburn prison workshop, and Stickley-Simonds at Eastwood — the furniture man before the magazine.
