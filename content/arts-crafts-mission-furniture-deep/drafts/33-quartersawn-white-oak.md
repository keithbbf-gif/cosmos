# Quartersawn White Oak

Look first at the end of the arm, not at the face. The saw has cut across the medullary rays of a white oak, *Quercus alba*, and the end-grain shows them as pale lines running out to the arris. Turn the arm. The same rays become flecks — flake, tiger, the figure a 1904 catalog could sell without naming a botanist. A chair seat, if you tip it and catch the light on the front rail’s end, will tell you whether the mill wasted a log on a plainsawn cut or paid for the radial board. This chapter starts there, on that end-grain, because the American movement’s favorite wood is a cut before it is a moral.

Species sermons are easy and mostly wrong. White oak is not a virtue. It is a tree that happened to be abundant, tannin-rich, and willing to show its rays if you sawed it on the radius. Factories loved it for reasons a plant manager can still list: the board cups less than plainsawn oak; the fleck photographed; the tannin took a fume (chapter 35); the Midwest already had the timber and the saws.

## What a ray is

A medullary ray in oak is a radial sheet of parenchyma. It stores starch. It runs from pith toward bark, like a wall inside the trunk. On a plainsawn board — the log sliced as if it were a loaf — those rays are mostly end-on or steeply angled; you see grain, not flake. On a quartersawn board the log is first quartered, then sawn so the face of the board is roughly radial. The rays lie on the face as glossy, irregular patches. Bruce Hoadley’s *Understanding Wood* is the shop manual behind that sentence. A furniture essay does not need his whole anatomy. It needs the fact that “quartersawn” is a geometry, not a brand.

White oak shows the fleck more usefully than many American hardwoods. Red oak (*Quercus rubra* and its cousins) has rays too; the furniture trade still preferred white for this look. White oak’s tyloses — balloon-like plugs in the vessels — make the wood tight enough for barrels. Cooperage and furniture are not the same industry. They shared a forest. Stickley’s catalogs and *The Craftsman* talk about oak as if the species were already a character. The character is *Q. alba* and its white-oak group, not a generic “oak” that includes a red-oak stair tread stained brown.

The end of a through-tenon (chapter 34) is a second lesson. You are looking at the same rays in cross-section, a pale constellation in a dark brown field if the piece was fumed. A later stain that leaves the rays pale on the face will often leave the tenon’s end looking busy and unfinished. The cut and the finish argue. They should. That argument is how you learn to see.

## Why a factory paid for the waste

Quartersawing wastes the log. You get fewer wide boards, more handling, more time at the saw. A plainsawn mill can turn a log into table tops with less scrap. Grand Rapids and Eastwood paid anyway. The reasons stack without being a triad of filler: stability, figure, and a market that had learned to read the fleck as honesty.

Stability first. A quartersawn board moves more in thickness than in width. A plainsawn board cups. A wide settle rail, a table top, a broad arm — the pieces this furniture wanted — stay flatter if the rings run perpendicular to the face. A plant that has already bought a kiln still wants the board that will not embarrass a retailer after a winter next to a register. The Craftsman settle that ships knocked down (shop number 210, catalog D, about 1904) is a freight problem and a wood-movement problem. The mill that cut those rails knew which way the rings ran.

Figure second. The fleck is a photograph. *The Craftsman* and the Eastwood catalogs show it even in coarse halftones. A customer who could not name a ray could still want the “silvery” patches. Grand Rapids trade papers sold the same glitter under the word Mission. Once the look was a look, a cheaper plant could stain a plainsawn board dark and hope the customer did not tip the arm. Many customers did not. Many still do not. The end-grain test remains the rude one.

Market third. American white oak was not an import problem. The Midwest and the Appalachian hardwood counties already moved oak by the carload. Chapter 4’s Centennial visitor saw state wood stacks before anyone had attached a moral to a fleck. By 1901 the moral was available and the lumber was still cheap relative to mahogany. Greene and Greene, once the Hall shop was running, often left oak for mahogany (chapter 30). Eastwood could not have furnished the middle of the country in mahogany at Craftsman prices. White oak was the species that could be both a manifesto and a freight rate.

## What the machines already knew

A bandsaw, a ripsaw, and a planer do not care about Ruskin. They care about a fence and a blade. Quartersawing at factory scale is a mill decision: how to break down the log, how to dry the radial boards, how to match fleck on a pair of arms so the chair does not look like two trees arguing. Eastwood was a factory with a guild vocabulary (chapter 11). Grand Rapids was a factory with a market vocabulary (chapter 39). Both already knew how to cut oak on the quarter for other styles — “antique oak,” golden oak, the export trades — before Mission wanted the fleck darker.

The Craftsman finish talk, which chapter 35 will keep away from a home recipe, depends on this cut. Stickley wanted the hard, glassy ray and the softer earlywood to take color together, so the figure would be a change in texture, not a cartoon outline. A pigment stain lodges in pores and leaves the ray pale. That is why a refinished chair and an unfumed stained chair can look loud in the same way. The mill’s cut is innocent. The later finish is the loudness.

Sapwood is the factory’s other problem. White-oak sapwood does not always announce itself on a rough board. It will not take a fume the way heartwood will. A plant that has promised an even brown must either cut sapwood away, dye the pale streak, or hope. Cathers and the Dallas catalog are careful about finish variation. A collector who demands perfect evenness on a 1902 chair is demanding a later factory’s control. Early boards are a forest.

## Seat, arm, and the places the fleck works

On a chair seat that is a solid slab — rarer in Stickley than in a Greene bench — the end-grain of the front edge is a map. Most Craftsman seats are leather over a frame or a spring. The fleck then lives on the rails, the posts, the slats, the broad arms of a Morris chair (chapter 36). The arm is the show. A flat-arm Morris, shop 332 or the earlier 2342, is a rectangle of radial oak you rest a book on. If the mill cheated and used a plainsawn arm, the cup will tell you in twenty years, and the figure will tell you in twenty seconds.

Slats are narrower. A slat can be quartersawn and still look quiet because the ray is cut into strips. Spindles are quieter still; there is hardly a face for a flake to occupy. That is one reason a spindle cube (chapters 32, 38) reads as architecture and a slat armchair reads as oak: the slat is a billboard for the cut. Wright’s Robie chairs are oak slats used as a wall (chapter 31). They are not selling fleck the way an Eastwood catalog page sells fleck. Different jobs, same genus.

A settle front rail is the largest ordinary billboard. Shop number 208, the even-arm settle, or 205, the hall settle, gives a craftsman-photographer a horizontal of flake above the leather. Tacks along that leather (chapter 37) punctuate the rail. The eye reads: wood, metal, hide — three materials, one bench. If the rail is plainsawn, the bench looks cheaper before you have checked a tenon.

## What the cut is not

It is not a California story. The missions did not invent radial sawing. It is not an English transplant. Cotswold workshops used oak; they did not invent the American mill’s quartered white oak as a national product. It is not a guarantee of honesty. A factory can quartersaw a board and dowel a joint you will never see. A small shop can plainsaw a board and cut a tenon you can touch. Chapter 34 is the joint. This chapter is the board.

It is also not “the only correct wood.” Stickley used willow (Met 2008.572.2), maple, mahogany on occasion, and canvas. Roycroft used what the campus had. Rohlfs used oak and also woods that do not fleck. Limbert’s cutouts often show oak that is quartersawn and oak that is not, on the same piece, because a cutout table has parts that need width more than flake `[VERIFY a specific Limbert example]`. The movement’s moral attached itself to *Q. alba* because the photograph worked. Morals that attach to photographs travel.

A contemporary shop that still quartersaws white oak is not, by that fact, making 1904 furniture. It is making a cut the 1904 factories already knew. Chapter 44 will be allowed to notice that the cut still has towns. Chapters 1–43 are not those towns’ advertisements. The end-grain on the arm is enough. The ray either shows or it does not. The next essay takes the place where the ray is allowed to come all the way through the post: the wedged through-tenon on a settle arm, which a Grand Rapids dowel can imitate only by lying.


A flatsawn oak board can be honest and still refuse the fleck. Factories paid for the quarter cut because the fleck photographed and sold. The ray is a sawyer’s decision before it is a moral. Stickley did not invent Quercus alba. He inherited a mill practice that already knew how to show the medullary ray and a stain shop that already knew how to brown it. The Craftsman caption made the ray look like ethics. The mill made it look like money. Both captions can be true of the same arm. A later refinisher who sands through the fumed face (chapter 35) is sanding through both captions at once.

Southern white oak and northern white oak entered the same catalogs under one name. The furniture trade was not a botanist. It was a buyer of thickness, length, and figure. Chapter 44 will walk the towns that still cut that figure. This chapter only has to keep the saw path visible: through the radius, not tangent to the rings, so the rays come to the face as flakes. That path wastes more of the log. The waste is why the fleck cost more. The cost is why it became a class mark.
