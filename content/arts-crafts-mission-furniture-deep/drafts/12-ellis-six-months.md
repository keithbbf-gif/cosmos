# Harvey Ellis: Six Months and a Myth

The armchair in the American Wing is oak with pewter, copper, and wood inlay and a leather seat: Metropolitan Museum of Art 2014.633, about 1903, made in Eastwood. It stands 47¼ inches high. The credit line is Leeds Art Foundation, in honor of Morrison H. Heckscher, 2014. The maker line now reads Gustav Stickley. A visitor who learned the trade in the 1980s still says Harvey Ellis before the number is out of the guard’s mouth. The museum’s own note will not let that sentence stand alone. The chair belongs to a second generation of Craftsman work, the note says — solid forms relieved by metal and exotic wood — and “while this furniture has traditionally been attributed to Harvey Ellis,” recent research treats the development as a collaborative effort among designers at United Crafts. The inlay, the same note adds, is a cousin to M. H. Baillie Scott and Charles Rennie Mackintosh. That is the whole problem of this chapter, sitting on four legs.

Ellis died on 2 January 1904 in Syracuse, of heart disease, three months past his fifty-first birthday. He had been on Stickley’s payroll since the spring of 1903 — May is the month most biographies give — after installing Stickley’s large Arts and Crafts exhibition at the Mechanics Institute in Rochester, where Ellis was president of the Rochester Arts and Crafts Society. Seven or eight months is the honest count. The title of the myth is six. The myth needs the shorter number because a short life at Eastwood is easier to turn into a golden age. Dealers still sell “Ellis-period Stickley” as if the calendar itself were a shop mark.

## What Ellis actually was

Harvey Ellis was born in Rochester on 17 October 1852, the oldest son of Dewitt and Eliza Haseltine Ellis. He lasted seven months at West Point and was discharged for academic insufficiency. By 1877 he was a founder of the Rochester Art Club. In 1879 he and his brother Charles opened H. & C. S. Ellis, Architects. The Rochester years produced Queen Anne houses and civic work that the city’s later growth erased; the paper trail is names and lots more often than standing buildings. From 1886 to 1893 he moved through midwestern offices that wanted a renderer who could win a competition: J. Walter Stevens in St. Paul; Leroy Buffington and the Orffs in Minneapolis; Eckel and Mann in St. Joseph; George Mann in St. Louis. The published perspectives in *American Architect and Building News* and *Inland Architect* made him famous among draftsmen who never met him. He signed his show drawings. Later collectors, hungry for a furniture genius, forgot that the signature was an architect’s.

He came back to Rochester in 1893, painted, taught, and in 1897 helped found the Rochester Arts and Crafts Society — Claude Bragdon and M. Louise Stowell with him. The society is the American door that matters here. Ellis did not arrive at Eastwood as a cabinetmaker. He arrived as an architect who had already spent twenty years putting furniture into perspective drawings so a house would look complete. David Cathers, in *Gustav Stickley* (2003), keeps that sequence visible. So does the Dallas Museum of Art catalog of 2010. Neither book needs Ellis to have invented the through-tenon.

The January 1904 *Craftsman* printed “Structure and Ornament in the Craftsman Workshops,” with a screen, a chair, a cabinet, a table, a desk, another table, another chair (pages 390–396 in volume 5). That is the portfolio the market still calls Ellis. The same month, Ellis was dying at St. Joseph’s Hospital after a fall down a stair and a long illness `[VERIFY: hospital and fall as commonly told; death date 2 January 1904 is firm]`. Stickley published a regret in the February number: a man who possessed “the sacred fire of genius.” Eulogy is not a shop drawing. The eulogy did more for the later myth than the payroll did.

## What the shop announced before the myth hardened

In July 1903 *The Craftsman* carried a two-page notice of “The New Models of the Gustav Stickley Furniture.” They were to be “lighter in effect and more subtle in form than any former productions of the same workshop.” Wood inlay, “designs of great delicacy, founded upon plant forms which are obscured and highly conventionalized,” was the added color. Metal is not in that July sentence. The January 1904 plates are full of pewter and copper. The Stickley Museum at Craftsman Farms, describing its own inlaid armchair (1999.60.1), is careful about the gap: the July advertisement and the January portfolio are not the same announcement. Only a music cabinet with a grape motif, the museum note says, lines up cleanly with the July wood-inlay language.

George H. Jones of New York manufactured inlay. His name sits on museum records as a supplier, not as a ghost in Syracuse. LACMA’s armchair (M.2011.128.18), given by Max Palevsky, still credits Ellis as designer, Stickley’s Craftsman Workshop as maker, and Jones as designer of the decoration — a three-part label that is already an argument. The Met’s 2014.633 refuses the first part of that argument. Dallas 2010 published related inlaid chairs as catalog numbers in the mid-thirties `[VERIFY exact cat. nos. 35–37 as cited in later sale notes]`. A reader who wants a single hand will be disappointed by every responsible label.

LaMont A. Warner was already drawing interiors for Stickley. Claude Bragdon, who had sat with Ellis on the Rochester society, also put inlaid pieces into rooms on paper. An enterprise that had a furniture department, an architecture department, a magazine, and a New York inlay shop is a poor place to hunt for a solitary genius. The Met’s phrase — “a collaborative effort among all of the designers at United Crafts” — is duller than the auction line. It is also closer to how a factory makes a chair.

## What the inlay is doing on the oak

The 2014.633 chair is not a slat armchair with a picture stuck on it. The posts are still oak. The seat is still leather. The inlay is a conventionalized plant that behaves like jewelry set into a rail: pewter and copper lines, darker wood, a pattern that reads from across a gallery and still reads when you are close enough to see the saw marks in the metal. Baillie Scott had already published furniture in which inlay was a structural cousin to the architecture, not a transfer. Mackintosh’s Glasgow rooms used a thinner vertical and a more nervous line than Eastwood ever wanted. *The Craftsman* had been printing English and Scottish names since Irene Sargent’s first volume. Ellis, who read those pages and who had been drawing English-looking houses since the late 1890s, did not need to invent the cousinship. He needed to put it into a Syracuse interior that a subscriber could imagine buying.

That is why the lighter rail and the arched apron matter more than the flower. Stickley’s earlier New Furniture and the first Craftsman chairs are ponderous on purpose: a moral about mass. The 1903 generation keeps the visible joint and loses some of the mass. A collector who has only handled the later cube chairs will feel the difference in the arm. A collector who has only read price guides will call every pretty Stickley “Ellis” and every heavy Stickley “before Ellis.” That sorting will not hold. Plenty of handsome Eastwood work has no inlay and no Ellis drawing behind it. Plenty of inlay left the shop after January 1904, when Ellis was already in the ground at St. Agnes Cemetery — an unmarked grave until 1997, when a Central New York society put a granite stone on it that says Architect, not Furniture Designer.

Cathers has spent years refusing the easy attribution. The Dallas catalog follows him far enough to keep Ellis in the architecture department and the inlay in a contested middle. Auction houses are slower. Christie’s and the private-sale rooms still print HARVEY ELLIS FOR GUSTAV STICKLEY over a pewter-and-copper armchair because the market pays for a name. The object does not care. The object has a shop mark, a leather seat that may have been replaced, and a pattern that a New York specialist cut.

## Six designs, not a golden age

Unsigned *Craftsman* illustrations from the last half of 1903 have been given to Ellis because the dates line up. The signed record is smaller. One careful count — the Rochester papers and the later biographical literature agree on the order of magnitude — gives Ellis six complete architectural designs in the magazine (five signed, one not), two signed interior projects, one architectural essay without pictures, and two paintings used as frontispieces `[CITE NEEDED: the exact signed list as tabulated by Eileen Manning Michels or the Rochester finding aid]`. He was hired to design houses for a magazine that had just invented the Homebuilders’ Club (chapter 13). He put furniture into those houses the way he had put furniture into Buffington perspectives in Minneapolis: to finish the picture. No one has ever claimed he designed Buffington’s chairs. The Craftsman claim grew later, when the chairs became expensive and the architect was safely dead.

The music cabinet with maple inlay that museums still attach to his name is the hardest case, because the object is so obviously a draftsman’s piece: a tall, thin case, a plant form that knows the page. Even there the honest sentence is “attributed,” not “signed on the back in Ellis’s hand.” He had no furniture apprenticeship. He had a renderer’s eye and a few months in a shop that already employed men who knew how to space a slat. The myth needs him to have changed everything. The payroll says he drew houses and died.

What did change in 1903–04 is real enough without the myth. Stickley had seen, in the Rochester exhibition Ellis installed, a roomful of English and American work that made the first Craftsman line look louder than it needed to be. The shop answered with lighter models and, briefly, with inlay. After Ellis’s death the inlay program thinned. The lighter proportions did not all vanish. A spindle cube and a drop-arm settle from later years can still show a rail that remembers 1903. Remembering is not authorship.

## What a label owes a dead architect

The Met’s revision is not an insult. It is a correction of a collector habit that flattened a shop into a biography. Ellis is not diminished by sharing a chair with Warner, with Jones, with the men on the Eastwood floor who cut the mortises. He is restored to the job he was paid for. The pretty Stickley that has no inlay, no arched apron, and no *Craftsman* plate behind it can go back to being a shop number. Chapter 11 already walked that plant. This chapter only has to stop the plant from being renamed Ellis Workshops.

A reader standing in front of 2014.633 can still like the chair. The pewter line is good. The oak is good. The collaboration is the fact that makes the chair American rather than a Glasgow souvenir. Baillie Scott and Mackintosh remain cousins, not employers. Ellis remains a Rochester architect who had six months, or seven, or eight, and then a January funeral. The myth wanted a longer season and a single hand. The museum, for once, has chosen the shorter season and the crowded shop. That is the better history. The next essay leaves the inlay rail and opens a house-plan plate, where Ellis’s actual assignment — a room that could be built — becomes the magazine’s monthly product.
