# Three Names for the Same Oak

The chair is branded under the front rail: *The McHugh Mission Furniture: Made in New York.* The letters are a manufacturer’s claim, not a historian’s. Joseph P. McHugh (1854–1916) had been selling rooms from The Popular Shop — first a corner of his father’s Eighth Avenue store in 1878, later Fifth Avenue addresses that moved as the avenue’s retail line moved — long before anyone in Syracuse printed *The Craftsman*. By the middle of the 1890s his shop was offering a line he was willing to call Mission. By 1906 the advertisements said “McHugh Mission Style,” as if the adjective had become a house trademark. Anna Tobin D’Ambrosio’s 1993 Munson-Williams-Proctor catalog, *The Distinction of Being Different*, is still the place that treats that claim as a claim.

A collector will point at the same silhouette — straight posts, a flat crest, a leather seat, quartersawn oak that shows the fleck — and say Arts and Crafts. A dealer who learned the language in the 1980s will say Craftsman. A search box will swallow all three and return the same brown chair. This series exists because the three words did not start as synonyms, and because a shop that still builds in this grammar needs the difference more than it needs a mood board.

## What the brand was selling

McHugh was a decorator who manufactured. Cooper Hewitt’s house biography is blunt about the mix: wall coverings, wicker, room settings, and, in the mid-1890s, oak furniture designed in the shop by Walter J. H. Dudley. The Popular Shop did not look like Eastwood. It looked like a New York interiors business that had found a linear oak suite it could repeat. McHugh later said Stickley copied him. Stickley’s people, when they bothered, said the opposite. Neither man needed to be first for the word Mission to do its work. The word did the work in the newspaper.

The origin story McHugh liked is the one that still travels. He saw photographs of chairs made for a San Francisco church, took them for furniture out of a California mission, and put a line into production. The chairs were real. The mission was not. A. J. Forbes made seats for the Swedenborgian Church on Lyon Street, the Church of the New Jerusalem, a building A. Page Brown finished in the mid-1890s. The church is a shingled Arts and Crafts interior with a beamed ceiling and a deliberate rusticity. It is not a Spanish colonial sacristy. Chapter 7 takes the Forbes chairs and the theft of the word. This chapter only needs the fact that Mission, in New York, began as a misreading that could be advertised.

D’Ambrosio published a low-down McHugh chair that had lived at Byrdcliffe — Jane Byrd McCall Whitehead bought it at The Popular Shop for White Pines — branded exactly as the sentence above. The colony that later became famous for its own benches was willing to sit on a New York factory’s Mission. That is not a scandal. It is how a name travels: a shop in Manhattan, a house in Woodstock, a catalog adjective that outlives both.

## What Stickley was unwilling to call it

Gustav Stickley (1858–1942) sold a different word. After the 1898 English trip and the experimental New Furniture of 1900, he organized the Eastwood plant as United Crafts in 1901 and began *The Craftsman* in October of that year. The magazine is the primary source for his language: honest construction, a house that would teach, oak that kept its grain. He did not need Mission. He needed Craftsman, which he could print on a cover, a house plan, and a shop mark. David Cathers’s *Gustav Stickley* (2003) and the Dallas Museum of Art catalog of 2010 are the modern spine. The Winterthur finding aid for the Stickley papers still gives the corporate sequence a reader can check: Stickley-Simonds, Gustav Stickley Company, United Crafts, The Craftsman, Inc., bankruptcy 24 March 1915.

Craftsman, in his mouth, was a program. It was also a trademark. When later American shops — and later American search engines — use Craftsman for any slatted oak armchair, they are spending a word Stickley spent money to own. L. & J.G. Stickley, down the road in Fayetteville, sold Handcraft and then their own oak. Albert’s Stickley Brothers in Grand Rapids sold Quaint Furniture and English-looking oak that Grand Rapids already knew how to machine. None of those lines is “a Craftsman chair” unless the mark says so. Chapter 11 will walk Eastwood. Chapter 16 and 17 will walk the brothers. The point here is narrower. Craftsman is a shop word that leaked.

Stickley could be severe about Mission when it meant McHugh, or Grand Rapids, or a suite in a Sears catalog. He could also live in a market that had already decided the look had a popular name. A reader of *The Craftsman* in 1904 is reading a monthly argument. A reader of a Grand Rapids trade paper in 1904 is reading a price. The oak can look related. The sentences are not.

## What the English phrase was doing in America

Arts and Crafts is the oldest of the three names and the least American. It is a London exhibition society (1887–88), a Morris lecture, a Ruskin paragraph about Gothic labor, a Boston society founded in 1897. Wendy Kaplan’s *The Art that is Life* (MFA, 1987) is still the American survey that keeps the phrase attached to a reform movement and not to a finish color. Eileen Boris, in *Art and Labor* (1986), keeps the politics attached to the furniture. Neither book thinks a through-tenon is a sufficient definition.

When an American writer in 1902 said Arts and Crafts, the sentence could mean a society, a moral, or a room. It did not yet mean a SKU. By 1910 the phrase had been asked to cover Stickley, Roycroft, Greene and Greene, Charles Rohlfs, a Cincinnati inlay cabinet, and a mail-order settle. That coverage is a later convenience. Rohlfs’s desks are not Mission. The Gamble dining chairs are not Craftsman. A Roycroft orb-and-cross is a campus brand, not a Syracuse shop mark. Chapter 22, 29, and 20 will refuse those collapses in detail. This chapter only has to refuse the collapse in the title.

The English furniture America actually imported — Morris & Co. Sussex chairs, Cotswold pieces that reached museums later, Baillie Scott drawings that Ellis-period inlay remembered — sat in a few houses and in a few magazines. They did not furnish the middle of the country. Quartersawn white oak did. The American movement is a reception history with a factory system already in place. Grand Rapids could cut a slat before Stickley printed a manifesto. That is why the names matter. Arts and Crafts named a reform. Mission named a line. Craftsman named a firm that wanted the reform and the line.

## A room in Buffalo, 1901

The Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo is the public hinge D’Ambrosio and the Cooper Hewitt note both keep. McHugh loaned furniture to the New York Building. Eight million people, the fair’s own count, walked the grounds. Charles Rohlfs, a Buffalo man, showed work that looked nothing like a McHugh suite and got the kind of press that still follows him. Stickley’s new language was a year old. The fair did not decide a style. It taught a crowd that oak with a straight post was a modern American interior and not a church-goods leftover.

After Buffalo the trade papers fill with Mission. After Buffalo *The Craftsman* fills with houses. The two streams share a color and a decade. They do not share a business. A decorator on Fifth Avenue, a factory in Eastwood, a Grand Rapids plant that could add a “Mission” page to next season’s catalog, and a California architect who thought in timber — those are four jobs. This series will keep them in separate chapters because the objects get stupid when the jobs are merged.

The word itself had a second American life in architecture. Mission Revival, on the West Coast, meant stucco, tile, and a bell-wall quotation of the Franciscan chain. A bungalow in Pasadena could wear that skin and still hold a Stickley settle that had never seen a mission. Robert Winter’s bungalow books and the Oakland / Abbeville California Arts and Crafts catalog keep the house-style and the furniture-style from pretending to be one inheritance. A reader who types “Mission furniture” in 2026 is rarely asking for a *presidio* bench. They are asking for oak with a visible joint. The search is honest. The etymology is not.

## How a collector learned to blur them

The 1970s and 1980s did the merging on purpose. Jordan-Volpe, the early Cathers books, the Streisand-era headlines, the price guides Bruce Johnson sold to a new market: all of them needed a category large enough to hang a show. “American Arts and Crafts” was that category. It was a good category for a museum. It was a leaky category for a rail brand. A McHugh chair from The Popular Shop and a Stickley settle with a red-decal shop number could hang on the same wall and teach a true lesson about oak and a false lesson about authorship.

Dealers still say Mission when they mean the look and Craftsman when they mean the money. Auction houses still say Arts and Crafts when they mean the sale title. None of that is a crime. It becomes a problem when a shop that builds a through-tenon settle is asked to be “authentic Stickley,” or when a factory suite from Grand Rapids is asked to be “true Mission” as if the California missions had a lumberyard. Chapter 42 will walk the collector market as a market. Chapter 7 will walk the California alibi. The vocabulary has to be cleaned first.

## What this series will do with the words

Mission, in these essays, means the American trade name and the slatted oak look that McHugh, Grand Rapids, Sears, and a dozen lesser marks sold under that name. Craftsman, unless the sentence is casual and then corrected, means Stickley’s magazine, mark, house plans, and Eastwood product. Arts and Crafts means the reform movement, the societies, and the larger American field that includes shops that refused the Mission label.

A reader who came here from a search for Mission furniture will still find Mission furniture. The chairs are here. The settles are here. The Morris chair, the fumed oak, the keyed tenon, the mail-order suite, the Gamble dining room that is not Mission at all — they are here in order. What the search box cannot do is tell you which door you are standing in. McHugh’s brand is one door. Stickley’s magazine is another. The English phrase is a third. The oak is what they shared. The names are what they fought over.

The next essay starts with a Ruskin paragraph an American actually printed. Not with a dawn. Not with a chair that changed everything. With a sentence that reformers liked and factories ignored.
