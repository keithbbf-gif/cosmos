# Stickley Before the Magazine

The Stickley Brothers wholesale rooms in Binghamton, opened after an 1883 start in Susquehanna, Pennsylvania, sold other men’s chairs as readily as their own. Gustav was twenty-five, newly married to Eda Ann Simmons on 12 September 1883, and not yet a monthly. Brandt chairs, Shaker seating, New England and Midwestern goods — the rooms were a furniture business in a river city that made cigars, leather, lumber, and chairs. The Craftsman mark did not exist. *The Craftsman* did not exist. United Crafts did not exist. Anyone who begins this man in October 1901 is beginning at a magazine and missing the shop.

This chapter stops in 1898, when he pushed Elgin Simonds out and named the Gustave Stickley Company. The English crossing and the New Furniture of 1900 are the next essay. The prison workshop, the brothers, the Eastwood plant of Stickley-Simonds — those are this one. The problem is a biography the later myth shortened.

## Osceola, Brandt, and a name that shifted

He was born 9 March 1858 in Osceola, Wisconsin, Gustavus Stoeckel, eldest of eleven children of Leopold Stoeckel and Barbara Schlager Stoeckel, German immigrants from Baden. (Some older cards say 1857; the 1942 *New York Times* obituary and the family work of Marilyn Fish keep 1858.) The surname moved, in the messy way immigrant names move, through Stoeckel, Stoeklee, and Stickley; by the early 1880s Stickley had stuck. Leopold was a stonemason. The household broke and mended. In 1869 the parents separated for a time; in 1875 Barbara left for Lanesboro, Pennsylvania, to be near her brother Jacob Schlager. Gustav’s formal schooling ended early. He had already been put to stone. In *Chips from the Workshop of Gustave Stickley* (1901) he later dated his love of wood to a “small chair factory in the hamlet of Brandt” — stereotyped work, he said, and still the start of his feeling for color, texture, and grain.

The Brandt Chair Company was his uncle’s. Charles (1860–1927), Albert (1862–1928), Leopold (1869–1957), and John George (1871–1921) also passed through that Pennsylvania orbit. By the time Gustav was twenty-one he was managing on the floor. In 1883 he and Albert and Charles founded Stickley Brothers Company in Susquehanna. A year later they moved the business to Binghamton, New York, at the meeting of the Susquehanna and the Chenango, a small city with a retail class and rail. The Winterthur finding aid (COL 60) and Fish’s *Heritage & Early Years* (1997) and *1884–1900* (1999) are the spine for these moves. Cathers’s 2003 monograph is the book that keeps them from becoming a prelude montage.

Binghamton is where he learned to sell. The brothers’ rooms were wholesale and retail. They did not yet need a philosophy. They needed stock. Gustav left that partnership in 1888. The sales operation continued as Stickley Bros., with George coming in. Charles would stay Binghamton for a long career of his own. Albert would go to Grand Rapids and build Stickley Brothers there (chapter 17). Leopold and John George would later open in Fayetteville (chapter 16). The family is a furniture industry, not a single genius. 1883–88 is the first firm, and it is a brothers’ firm.

## Streetcars, Simonds, and Auburn

The first Susquehanna year, 1883, was manufacture: chairs from a new brothers’ shop in a Pennsylvania town that already knew how to cut a post. Binghamton, from 1884, added wholesale rooms and other men’s stock. That split — make a little, sell a lot — is the early Stickley method. It is also why a later magazine that preached the handmade had to forget a warehouse.

In 1888 Gustav invested in the Binghamton streetcar line and, that December, formed Stickley & Simonds with Elgin Simonds (1854–1903), a salesman. The later *New York Times* obituary would still remember him as a furniture manufacturer, publisher, and operator of an early electric streetcar line. The streetcar is not a craftsman credential. It is a man who understood how shoppers reached a store. Simonds was president and ran sales from New York. Stickley was treasurer and oversaw making. In 1889 he worked with the financier G. Tracy Rogers on another rail project. The Henry Ford’s Benson Ford Research Center still holds deeds from this period (Stickley Family Collection, accession 1624). The later Craftsman is sometimes written as if commerce arrived in 1913 with a Manhattan lease. Commerce was there in the 1880s, in a streetcar and a partnership.

In 1890 Stickley & Simonds moved operations toward Auburn, New York. Gustav ran the furniture workshop at Auburn Prison. Cathers’s line, which Melissa Renn quotes, is the one to keep: the prison contract showed a man willing to flout industry norms when it suited him, and a man who liked the role of mentor — here, teaching inmates to make chairs. Leopold served with him as a foreman. Prison labor was cheaper than free labor. The practice was unpopular in the trade, which is a way of saying it worked. Cathers puts the workshop under Stickley’s hand from 1891 until it closed in 1897. The chairs that left Auburn were not Craftsman chairs. They were contract chairs. A series that starts in 1901 can afford to skip them. A series that wants the man who later printed a guild vocabulary cannot. The guild vocabulary and the prison time sheet are the same career.

He was still a trade furniture man. The 1890s product of Stickley & Simonds included the revival suites a hotel would buy. The Waldorf-Astoria ordered mahogany for a foyer `[VERIFY the 1896 commission as given in the immigrant-entrepreneurship account]`. After a European trip in 1896 he invested in machinery that could shape wood into imitation bamboo, a Japan-craze good that leads backward to the 1876 courts (chapter 4) and forward to nothing in the 1901 magazine. Rocking chairs with acorn finials and upholstered seats survive in the literature as the sort of thing the partnership knew how to make. This is not a fall from grace. It is the job.

## Eastwood, 1893: a plant before a creed

In 1893 Stickley & Simonds built a new factory in Eastwood, a Syracuse suburb with rail and labor. Leopold and John George came to work there. Gustav divided time between Auburn, where the family lived, and the new plant. Eastwood in 1893 is not yet the Craftsman Workshops. It is a factory address. Chapter 11 will walk the later plant, the fuming rooms, the shop numbers. This chapter only needs the fact that the address predates the argument. When later writers say Stickley “founded Eastwood” in 1898 or 1901, they are founding a brand. The buildings and the siding were already there.

He had already crossed to Europe. April 1895 is the first trip in the careful accounts (Renn, after Fish and Cathers). England, and some of the Continent; whether he reached Baden is not known. He had read Ruskin — *The Seven Lamps of Architecture* is the title American summaries like — and Morris as a name. He did not meet them. He likely saw a Morris chair in a Morris & Co. setting. He went again in 1896. Those crossings matter, and they are not yet 1898. The later myth likes a single pilgrimage. The factory calendar is messier. 1895 and 1896 are looking. 1898 is ownership. 1900 is a line. 1901 is a monthly. Collapsing them is how a magazine preface gets written.

On 5 May 1898, with Simonds absent, Stickley proposed to the board that Stickley & Simonds be dissolved and a Gustave Stickley Company created. The board — Gustav and Eda, Elgin and Jennie Simonds, and Leopold — approved. Stickley fired the president and made himself president and principal owner. A year later he floated a United Chair Manufacturers combination that Mary Ann Smith notes and that never formed. The 1898 act is the end of this chapter: a man in possession of a plant, a name (still Gustave, with an e he would drop in 1903), and no magazine. The English trip that Winterthur’s finding aid attaches to 1898, and that older accounts treat as the conversion crossing, belongs to the next essay. So do Henry Wilkinson, LaMont A. Warner, and the New Furniture shown at Grand Rapids in July 1900.

What 1898 does *not* do is invent a craftsman out of a stonemason’s son. The craftsman was already a manufacturer. He had hired prisoners. He had sold imitation bamboo. He had kept books with a salesman named Simonds and then removed him. He had brothers in the same trade who would compete with him for the rest of the oak years. The magazine would need all of that and would print almost none of it.

## Why the years before 1901 have to stay visible

The temptation, after 1970, was to start the American Arts and Crafts furniture story at *The Craftsman* vol. 1, no. 1, because that is where the sentences are. Irene Sargent’s Morris essays are better copy than an Auburn time sheet. The Binghamton rooms do not photograph as well as a fumed settle. The collector market (chapter 42) paid for the later object. It did not pay for a prison chair. A magazine history that accepts that payment will misread the later object. Eastwood’s mix of machine and hand (chapter 11) is not a fall from a handmade 1890s. The 1890s were already a factory. The 1901 language is new. The plant is not.

The brothers are the other reason to stay in these years. Albert’s Grand Rapids firm (1891 onward) and Charles’s Binghamton continuance mean that “Stickley furniture” was never one man’s output. Leopold’s years at Auburn and Eastwood mean the younger brother learned the floor before he learned to be a rival. A 1901 start makes Gustav the origin and the others copies. An 1883 start makes a family in the trade. Clark and Kaplan, when they are careful, keep the family. Popular captions do not.

Eda Ann Simmons (1859–1919) married him the year of the first brothers’ firm. The children — Barbara (1887), Mildred (1888), Hazel (1890), Marion (1893), Gustav Jr. (1894), Ruth (1897) — are Binghamton and Auburn years, not Craftsman Farms years. The family house in Syracuse comes later, with the Crouse stables showroom (chapter 11). Before the magazine he was a husband with a payroll and a plant in a suburb. After the magazine he was the same thing with a foreword.

The leftover fact is the one *Chips* allowed: the Brandt chair factory was commonplace, and he dated his feeling for wood from it. That feeling is not a design. It is a worker’s memory, written in 1901 when he needed a past that would fit a guild name. The Binghamton rooms, the Auburn workshop, and the 1893 Eastwood factory are the past that does not fit as neatly. They are also the past that built the chairs. The next essay takes the 1898 crossing and the New Furniture of 1900 — Wilkinson, Warner, Grand Rapids, Tobey — and refuses to put William Morris on the payroll.

Prison labor and a street-railway vice-presidency are not picturesque. They are how a furniture man in the 1890s paid bills. A series that starts Stickley in 1901 as a converted pilgrim has already thrown those years away. Keep the commonplace factory. Keep Auburn. The magazine will invent the conversion. The chairs were already in the shop, and they already had a price.


The 1880s Binghamton stock is not a rehearsal for a manifesto. It is chairs a prison shop and a partnership could already make. A later red decal does not unmake those years.
