# Eastlake on the American Parlor Table

The Boston copy in the Metropolitan Museum of Art is dated 1872 and numbered 65.672.2: Charles L. Eastlake, *Hints on Household Taste in Furniture, Upholstery, and Other Details*, James R. Osgood & Co. The London book had appeared in 1868. Osgood’s American edition is the one that sat on parlor tables from Boston to Cincinnati, often with notes by Charles C. Perkins, the Boston writer who had already been teaching Americans how to look at Italian painting. Six American editions were in print by 1881. A later New York issue still carried Perkins’s name. The object that matters here is not Eastlake’s English life. It is this Boston book, and the factory parlor table that stole its author’s name faster than any Mission chair would later steal California’s.

This essay will not turn Eastlake into Mission furniture. The slatted oak of 1901 is a different shop problem. What America did with *Hints* is the earlier theft: a reform grammar of straight lines and visible construction, cut into walnut and cherry by machines that Eastlake did not own and did not like.

## What the book asked a parlor to do

Eastlake was an English architect and critic, born in Plymouth in 1836, dead in London in 1906, nephew of the painter-director Sir Charles Lock Eastlake (the uncle spelled Lock without the e). American readers did not need the family tree. They needed a book that told them the curved rosewood parlor was a fraud. *Hints* preferred oak. It preferred a joint you could see. It preferred incised ornament that followed the construction instead of applied fruit and cabriole legs that pretended a table had grown in a French forest. Leather, nailed, was better than a stuffed seat that hid its frame. A small table that could be moved was better than a slab that existed only to carry a marble and a stereoscope.

The plates in the Osgood edition — medievalizing chairs, hanging cabinets, a sideboard that looks like a small church — are English Gothic Revival after Pugin and beside Bruce J. Talbert. They are not Arts and Crafts chairs in the 1902 Syracuse sense. They are a household argument. Perkins’s notes, where they appear, are the American door: a Boston voice telling a bride that this English book was usable. Chipstone’s furniture literature still treats the 1872 Boston edition as the text Tiffany’s circle had on the shelf by the early 1880s. The book was a wedding present. That is how a reform text becomes a style name.

Eastlake did not manufacture. He published. American factories manufactured, and they needed a word that sounded like a designer. They took his.

## The parlor table that wore the name

Walk a Midwestern auction barn or a New England spare bedroom and the American “Eastlake” parlor table is still there: walnut or cherry, a rectangular or oval top, sometimes marble, incised lines running in shallow geometric bands around the skirt, chamfered legs, a shelf below for a pot or a pile of *Harper’s*. The incising is the tell. A spindle or a trefoil is cut in, not applied. The corners are notched. The finish is often a dark varnish that hides the grain the book had asked you to honor. Porcelain casters survive on the better ones. The cheaper ones lost their casters and gained a coat of paint in 1948.

This table is not in the Met as a named Eastlake product, and it should not be. Museums collect the shop that signed. They collect Kimbel and Cabus. They collect Herter. The factory parlor table is the thing the book actually produced in quantity: a machine-readable version of a page. Grand Rapids could cut those incised lines with a router and a template. Cincinnati’s Mitchell & Rammelsberg could fill a catalog page with “Eastlake” suites — sofa, reception chairs, a center table — before a Boston society had minutes. New York jobbers advertised the same word in the daily papers. The parlor, not the hall, was the room that bought it. Horsehair was going out. Incised walnut was coming in.

A collector today will call the table Eastlake and mean the look. Eastlake, while he was alive, meant the book. The gap is the American reception.

## What the factories sold that the book refused

The American trade style of the 1870s and 1880s is angular, notched, and carved in shallow relief. It is often ebonized over cherry, with a line of gilt in the incision, especially in New York shops that had also looked at Japanese lacquer. It can be walnut with a natural face and a machine-cut gallery. It can be a bedroom suite with incised sunflowers that have nothing to do with Wilde and everything to do with a cutter that could repeat a flower. Wanamaker’s later sold both cheap and dear versions. Jordan & Moriarty’s illustrated catalogs of the 1880s show the range: a simple commode and a piled étagère under the same adjective.

Eastlake watched the advertisements and wrote the sentence American decorative-arts literature still quotes. He found American tradesmen continually advertising what they were pleased to call Eastlake furniture, the production of which he had had nothing to do, and for the taste of which he would be very sorry to be considered responsible. The sentence is in later editions and in the dealer literature that grew up around the word. It is not a museum wall text invented in 1980. It is the author refusing a SKU.

He was right to refuse. The book had asked for construction that told the truth. The factory had asked for a line that could be cut in a day. Incised ornament is cheap once the jig exists. A cabriole leg is expensive. “Eastlake” in Grand Rapids was, among other things, an accounting decision. That does not make every American piece a fraud. Some shop work — especially in New York — read Talbert and Eastlake with care. Most of it read a competitor’s catalog.

## Kimbel and Cabus, and the shops that actually read

The New York firm of Kimbel and Cabus, at 7 and 9 East Twentieth Street, is the American shop that makes the book look less stolen. Anton Kimbel and Joseph Cabus, German- and French-trained cabinetmakers, had been partners since 1862. By the mid-1870s they were selling Modern Gothic: ebonized cherry or oak, incised gilt lines, strap hinges, inlaid tiles, a gable that quotes a roof. The Metropolitan’s oak desk of about 1875, accession 2000.58, sits on trestle feet with mortise-and-tenon construction, nickel-plated hardware, linenfold panels, and a writing slide that still has its red baize. LACMA’s related desk is 88.4a-b, walnut, nickel-plated hardware, designed about 1875. These are not factory parlor tables. They are what a New York showroom could do when it took the English plates seriously and still ran a business.

Kimbel and Cabus showed at Philadelphia in 1876. Chapter 4 will walk that fair. This chapter only needs the fact that “Eastlake” in America was already two trades before the Centennial opened: a signed Modern Gothic shop, and a Grand Rapids adjective. Herter Brothers, on the dear side of the same city, could ebonize and inlay at a price Eastlake’s middle-class reader would never meet. Pottier & Stymus worked the same altitude. The parlor table in a clerk’s house in Springfield, Ohio, did not come from those rooms. It came from a catalog.

Cooper Hewitt holds a Kimbel and Cabus photographic trade catalog of the 1870s, more than a hundred plates of Modern Gothic stock. That catalog is the missing link between the English book and the American factory. It shows a shop repeating a grammar. Once the grammar can be photographed, it can be copied by a plant that never read *Hints*.

## Why this is not Mission, and why the years matter

Mission furniture, when the word finally sticks in the middle 1890s, is a different theft. It steals a California noun. Eastlake steals an English surname. The objects do not match. An Eastlake parlor table is incised, often ebonized or heavily varnished, still a Victorian parlor object — a center table under a gasolier, a place for a Bible and a vase. A McHugh Mission chair is a slatted oak seat with a leather or rush seat and a brand under the rail. A Stickley settle is a hall object with a through-tenon. You can force a family resemblance: straight lines, a refusal of the cabriole, a taste for oak. You cannot make Eastlake the father of Eastwood without skipping the shops that actually intervened — the Centennial, Wilde’s halls, McHugh’s Fifth Avenue, a Swedenborgian church on Lyon Street.

Kaplan’s *The Art that is Life* keeps Eastlake in the American reform stack without making him a furniture factory. Clark’s 1972 Princeton survey, which begins in 1876, treats the Centennial as the public shock and leaves the 1868–72 book as the private one. Both moves are right. Americans bought *Hints* in the 1870s the way they later subscribed to *The Craftsman*: as permission to simplify a room. The permission was used by people Eastlake would not have hired.

There is a second confusion the parlor table encourages. Because some Eastlake pieces show Japanese-looking incising — a linear flower, a key pattern, an ebonized ground — later writers fold them into the “Japanism” that the 1876 fair is said to have caused. The dates fight that story. Osgood’s edition is 1872. Kimbel and Cabus were already Modern Gothic. The Japanese house in Fairmount Park confirmed a taste; it did not invent the incised line on a Cincinnati table that was already in a catalog.

Ruskin, in the previous essay, supplied a Gothic paragraph American societies could quote. Eastlake supplied drawings a factory could trace. That is why his name stuck to furniture and Ruskin’s did not. A through-tenon can be called honest without being called Ruskin. A notched walnut table needed a designer on the invoice. Eastlake was available. He was in print. He was English. He was not in Grand Rapids to file a suit.

## What a shop in 1880 was actually arguing

By 1880 an American furniture man had three reform words he could put on a card: Gothic, Eastlake, Japanese. None of them meant Mission. None of them meant Craftsman. Arts and Crafts, as a society name, was still a London phrase (1887–88) and a Boston ambition (1897). The parlor was still the room that spent the money. *Hints* had tried to reform that room from inside — better tables, better chairs, better sense — not to abolish it for a living hall with a settle. Stickley’s magazine would later make the hall the moral center. Eastlake’s readers were still buying parlor tables. The difference is not a rise in virtue. It is a change in which room a household was willing to display.

Factory Eastlake died as a fashion in the 1890s the way factory Mission would die in the 1920s: a new revival ate it. Colonial Revival wanted turned legs and mahogany, or at least the idea of mahogany. The incised walnut table went to the attic. When the 1970s Arts and Crafts revival hung oak on a gallery wall, it did not hang the Eastlake parlor table next to a Stickley armchair. The table looked too Victorian. It *is* Victorian. That is the point. American reform furniture has a parlor chapter before it has a hall chapter. The Boston book is that chapter’s text. The factory table is its artifact.

The next essay leaves the parlor table and walks Fairmount Park in 1876, past a Japanese dwelling and a British furniture court, to see what American cabinetmakers actually stood in front of — not what later surveys needed them to have felt.
