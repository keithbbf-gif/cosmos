# McHugh’s Popular Shop

In the 1993 galleries at Munson-Williams-Proctor in Utica, Anna Tobin D’Ambrosio put a low-down oak chair on a platform and let the brand in the wood finish the sentence. The exhibition catalog, *The Distinction of Being Different*, numbered the piece in the show (cat. 21 in the published sequence). The letters were a New York manufacturer’s. They said Mission. They said Made in New York. They did not say a California sacristy, and they did not say Stickley. Jane Byrd McCall Whitehead had bought such a chair at The Popular Shop for White Pines; a colony later famous for its own benches was willing to sit on a decorator’s oak. Chapter 1 used that brand to split three names. This chapter stays in the shop that ironed the letters in.

Joseph P. McHugh (1854–1916) was a decorator who manufactured. The Popular Shop, opened in 1878, is the business. Walter J. H. Dudley, in from 1896, is the draughtsman. The Mission line is one product inside a store that also sold willow, wall coverings, and whole rooms. The origin story McHugh liked — chairs from a California mission, copied in New York — is the story this chapter will not swallow whole. The chairs were real. The mission was a misreading. Chapter 7 takes the San Francisco church. Here the job is the Fifth Avenue shop, the Buffalo fair, and the 1906 advertisements that treated “McHugh Mission Style” as a house trademark.

## 1878, and the addresses that moved

McHugh’s father had a dry-goods business; the firm of Joseph P. McHugh and Son is put around 1869 in the Cooper Hewitt house biography. In 1878 the son carved out The Popular Shop in a corner of the father’s Eighth Avenue store. Window shades first. Then the stock grew. Two years later he took his own address. One modern shop history says 912 Fifth Avenue, near Fifty-Second Street. Cooper Hewitt’s biography says 912 Sixth Avenue. D’Ambrosio is the monograph that should settle the avenue `[VERIFY 912 Fifth against 912 Sixth in the 1993 chronology]`. By 1884 the shop had taken a larger hold at Fifth Avenue and Forty-Second Street, the corner that would later look across at the New York Public Library. That is the address the later notices mean when they say The Popular Shop sat across from the Library. *The Upholsterer and Interior Decorator* 61 (1919), “Some McHugh Statistics,” is the trade obituary that still gets cited for the firm’s own numbers.

The shop sold rooms. That sentence is not a metaphor. McHugh offered wallpaper, textiles, wicker, furniture, and the claim that a customer could leave with a coordinated interior. “The Distinction of Being Different” was advertising language before it was a catalog title. “An oasis of good taste” was another line. The name Popular Shop told the truth about the ambition: not a guild, not a jury, a store that intended to be current. Colonial Revival, willow, Mission, whatever the season asked for. A 1915 piece in *Decorative Furniture* described McHugh as a man who took an idea from anywhere — reading, a client, the street — and had sketches and models in motion before the trade had finished its coffee. The tone is boosterish. The method is a decorator’s.

Fifth Avenue moved its retail line north and then organized itself around the Library and the hotels. McHugh moved with it. The oak line, when it came in the middle 1890s, entered a business that already knew how to photograph a room and how to place a notice in *House and Garden* and *The Upholsterer*. Stickley’s Eastwood plant would look like a factory. The Popular Shop looked like a New York interiors house that had found a repeatable suite.

## Dudley at the board

Walter John Hill Dudley was born 23 August 1862 in Douglas, Massachusetts, eldest of Arthur James Dudley and Christiania Sarah Hill. (A Winterthur note that once printed 1962 is a slip.) He had been postmaster in the town his family had held. In June 1888 he went to New York to become a designer. Night classes at the Art Students League and a term at the Brooklyn Institute of Arts and Sciences; days in the office of the architect H. Edwards Ficken, who insisted that a drawing carry dimensions. In 1896 he joined McHugh. He stayed, the Winterthur finding aid says, twenty-five years — through McHugh’s death in 1916 and into a retirement in 1923, back to Douglas, dead in 1947.

Dudley’s surviving sheets at Winterthur (about 1903–1915) are the opposite of a mission-sacristy romance. They name the object, the client, the address, the dimensions. Some are signed. Some mention Jos. P. McHugh & Co. Eleven sheets are furniture; ten are exterior architectural drawings. Willow appears. Mission frames appear. Later sheets marry a straight Mission post to a willow panel, which is a decorator’s solution, not a Franciscan one. Cooper Hewitt is blunt: the Mission pieces sold in the shop were designed in-house by Dudley. McHugh put his name on the brand. Both things can be true. A New York interiors firm is allowed a designer who is not the owner. The later fight over who “invented Mission” treated McHugh as a solitary origin. The drawings treat him as a shop.

Dudley is also why the furniture has a look that is not Stickley’s. McHugh Mission can be lighter, more willing to use an X-brace (the magazine-stand / encyclopedia stand that Wanamaker later sold is the usual example), more willing to stay in a parlor suite. It is shop oak. It is not a Craftsman house plan. Collectors who learned the 1980s price guides sometimes miss it because the money went to Syracuse decals. D’Ambrosio’s catalog is still the correction.

## The tale he told, and the tale he needed

McHugh said he had seen photographs of chairs made for a California church, taken them for furniture out of a mission, and put a line into production. The tale travels because it is short. It makes New York the copyist and California the source, which flattered both coasts in different decades. It is also the tale a decorator would tell: I saw a picture, I made a room. D’Ambrosio treats the claim as a claim. This chapter does the same.

What can be held without the church essay (chapter 7) is simple. There were chairs. They were made in San Francisco in the mid-1890s for the Swedenborgian Church — the Church of the New Jerusalem — on Lyon Street, a building A. Page Brown’s office finished for the Reverend Joseph Worcester. They were maple, rush-seated, built for a shingled Arts and Crafts interior. They were not Spanish colonial sacristy furniture. Photographs of them moved. San Francisco art-goods houses, S. & G. Gump among them, are the names later writers put on that movement `[VERIFY the exact Gump–McHugh paper trail; do not invent a letter]`. McHugh, or Dudley, or both, had a picture and a market. By about 1898 The Popular Shop was selling a related line. By 1906 the advertisements said “McHugh Mission Style,” as if the adjective had become a trademark. *House and Garden* 10 (1906) is the citation Cooper Hewitt and chapter 1 share; page 24 is the page commonly given `[VERIFY]`.

McHugh later said Stickley copied him. Stickley’s people, when they bothered, said the opposite. Neither man needed to be first for the word Mission to work in a newspaper. The word worked because it was short, pious, and Western. A Fifth Avenue customer could buy a straight-lined oak suite and feel she had purchased California without leaving Forty-Second Street. That is a retail success. It is not a documentary success. The mission-sacristy tale is useful to the shop and expensive to the chairs.

Do not repair the tale by saying the chairs were “mission-inspired.” They were church chairs for a Swedenborgian congregation, designed in a Bay Area office that had been reading Ruskin and looking at California timber. The Franciscan chain is a different architecture. McHugh’s willingness to let the two blur is the theft chapter 7 will name. The Popular Shop’s job was to sell the blur.

## Buffalo, 1901, and the year the trade papers filled

The Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo is the public hinge D’Ambrosio and Cooper Hewitt both keep. McHugh loaned furniture to the New York Building. The fair’s own count put eight million people on the grounds. Charles Rohlfs, a Buffalo man, showed work that looked nothing like a McHugh suite and got the press that still follows him (chapter 22). Stickley’s new language was a year old. The fair did not award a patent on oak. It taught a crowd that a straight post was a modern American interior.

After Buffalo the trade papers fill with Mission. After Buffalo *The Craftsman* fills with houses. McHugh had the decorator’s advantage: daily contact with the customer who would never read Sargent on Morris. He could add a willow to a Mission frame when the customer blinked. He could drop the line when the season turned. A factory in Eastwood, committed to a monthly argument, could not turn as fast. That speed is why McHugh is easy to forget. He did not need to be a prophet. He needed to be current. When Mission cooled he sold something else. The firm passed in the family after 1916; James S. McHugh is the son named in later shop histories. Those later years are not this chapter’s furniture.

John Wanamaker, the year before McHugh died if the collector literature is right — 1915 `[VERIFY the letter’s date and wording]` — published an apology for having credited the Mission style to Stickley. Wanamaker sold McHugh magazine tables. A department-store letter is not a genealogical proof. It is evidence that McHugh spent his last year still arguing priority. Priority is a shopkeeper’s wound. The objects do not require it. What they require is a label that says New York, Dudley, The Popular Shop, and a refusal to file the chair under a California mission that never employed Forbes to furnish a sacristy.

## A decorator’s oak, and why the shop is not Eastwood

McHugh Mission is easy to flatten into “early Stickley” or “New York Mission.” Both flattenings waste the shop. The Popular Shop was an interiors business with a factory capability. Eastwood was a factory with a magazine. McHugh hired a designer who had learned to put dimensions on a sheet. Stickley hired, later, Irene Sargent to put sentences on a monthly. McHugh’s customer stood in a showroom across from a library. Stickley’s customer, after 1901, could order from a page. The oak can look related — straight posts, leather, a dark finish, a visible stretcher. The businesses do not.

Willow is the tell. Dudley’s later marriage of Mission frame and willow panel is a Popular Shop idea. Stickley did not need it. Grand Rapids, when it added a Mission page, did not need it either. The hybrid belongs to a decorator who still thought in suites and in seasonal goods. So does the X-braced magazine stand. So does the willingness to sell Colonial Revival from the same counter. Arts and Crafts as a moral program is a bad fit for that counter. Arts and Crafts as an American field that includes shops which refused to be churches is a good fit. Kaplan’s big surveys have room for McHugh when they remember New York retail. Boris’s labor book has less use for him. Both judgments are fair. This series needs the shop because the word Mission starts here as a brand.

The branded chair in Utica is still the best object. It carries the claim on the wood. It lived for a time at Byrdcliffe. It is not a Craftsman shop number. It is not a Forbes maple seat. It is The Popular Shop, doing what a Popular Shop stated it would do: take an idea, make a model, and let the decorative world wake up to a new piece of furniture. The idea was a photograph of a church chair. The model was New York oak. The world that woke up said Mission, and meant McHugh, and then meant everyone.

The next essay goes to the church the photograph misnamed: A. J. Forbes’s maple chairs in A. Page Brown’s Swedenborgian building on Lyon Street, and the California mission that was used as a furniture alibi.
