# Albert Stickley in Grand Rapids

The Metropolitan Museum’s copy of *Quaint Furniture*, Catalogue No. 28, is dated January 1906. Offset half-tones, 7¹³⁄₁₆ by 10¾ inches, Elisha Whittelsey Collection, 59.521.16. The author line is Stickley Brothers Furniture Company, Grand Rapids, Michigan. There is no Syracuse suburb on the title page. There is no *Craftsman* masthead. The word the plant was willing to print is Quaint — an English shop adjective Albert Stickley put on a Michigan factory line and kept there long after the oak fashion had moved. This chapter starts on that catalog because the catalog is the argument. Albert’s shop was a Grand Rapids shop. It used the family surname, an English word, and the city’s system: seasonal catalogs, a trade-show floor, a price list dated like a contract.

Albert (1862–1928) `[VERIFY birth year]` took the old partnership name west in 1891 with John George, then about twenty. The Binghamton Stickley Brothers of the 1880s (chapter 8) had already broken into other letterheads. Charles went toward Stickley-Brandt. Gustav kept Eastwood. Albert kept Stickley Brothers and planted it in the city that already knew how to cut a chair before a manifesto arrived. John George left about 1900 for Fayetteville and Leopold (chapter 16). Albert stayed. The Quaint lines are his plant’s long memory: Quaint Mission about 1903, Quaint Arts & Crafts about 1904, later Quaint Tudor, Quaint Manor (about 1914), Quaint American, Quaint Colonial. The adjective outlasted the style. The plant closed in 1954. This series will not follow it there.

## What Quaint was doing

In English trade talk of the 1890s and early 1900s, quaint could mean the new simple furniture — Arts and Crafts as a shop-window word rather than a society word. Albert brought the adjective home. Some of the furniture that followed a trip to London — the trip is repeated in the collector literature without a passenger list — has an English weight: bulb-turned legs, a heavier stretcher, a floral inlay that is closer to a Liberty shop than to Eastwood’s slat. `[VERIFY the London trip and any London office or “Russmore” export label against Marek/Weiderman and a marked example.]` The Bewdley line, attributed to D. Robertson Smith about 1902, is the usual early instance: European-looking inlay on angular cases, a cousin to what Oscar Onken’s Shop of the Crafters would soon sell from Cincinnati (chapter 25). Bewdley is an English place-name on a Grand Rapids product. That is the reception history in one word.

Quaint Mission and Quaint Arts & Crafts are the lines an American collector now means. They are slatted oak, leather, a dark finish, metal tags. The tags went through paper, burned marks, gold decals, and then a brass plate in an Art Nouveau cartouche that said Quaint Furniture, or, in 1903, simply Quaint. A metal tag screwed to a drawer side is not a Craftsman decal and not an Onondaga label. It is Grand Rapids hardware: durable, repeatable, easy for a retailer to recognize in a warehouse.

The 1912 Catalogue No. 38, reprinted in 1993 with a preface by Don Marek and Richard Weiderman, is the thick public record: hundreds of half-tones, a section of Quaint furniture figured in mahogany, and a 1 January 1912 price list keyed to the plates. Mahogany in a “Mission” catalog is a Grand Rapids sentence. Eastwood’s moral was oak. Albert’s moral was a line that could be oak or mahogany as the buyer’s room required. Thirteen pages of figured mahogany in the 1912 book are not a betrayal. They are the city’s habit of offering a style in more than one wood.

## The Grand Rapids system

Grand Rapids in 1891 was already a furniture city: mills, finishing rooms, a labor pool, *The Furniture Record*, a calendar of markets that brought buyers from the rest of the country. A plant that opened there did not need to invent a guild. It needed a line, a catalog, and a booth. Stickley Brothers used all three. The system is why Albert’s oak could look like Gustav’s from across an aisle and still be a different business. Eastwood had a magazine that taught a household how to speak. Grand Rapids had a trade paper that taught a buyer how to price.

Factory Mission — the later subject of chapter 39 — is not an insult to Albert, and Albert is not the whole of Factory Mission. Lifetime, Limbert (after the Holland move), and a dozen marks that never had a famous brother will fill that chapter. Stickley Brothers sits on the better end of the city’s Arts and Crafts production: better wood than the cheapest suites, more design attention than a page added overnight, and still, unmistakably, a catalog operation. Machines and hands shared the work, as they did at Eastwood. The difference is the caption. Albert did not need Ruskin in the preface. He needed Quaint on the tag.

There is a story that Albert installed a restaurant in his showroom seven years before Gustav’s twelfth-floor dining room. `[VERIFY; the claim travels in collector essays and may be shop talk.]` If it is true, it is a Grand Rapids joke with a point. Hospitality as sales is not a Syracuse invention. If it is false, the point remains: Albert understood display. The 1906 and 1912 catalogs are display you can mail.

English influence, in this plant, is not a pilgrimage narrative. Gustav’s 1898 crossing (chapter 9) has been told as a conversion. Albert’s English shop sense is a product decision: use the word the London trade already used, hire or buy designs that read as English, and, if the export story holds, send American oak back across the water under a second name. A London office is repeated in identification guides; a marked Russmore piece would settle it. Until one is on the table, the English shop remains an influence, which is what this chapter’s brief asked for, not a second factory you can walk through.

## What the furniture looks like when it is Albert’s

A typical Quaint Mission armchair of about 1904–10 has the American slat, the leather seat, the dark oak. It may also have a corbel, a shaped stretcher, or a hardware set that Eastwood would have called fussy. Inlay, when it appears, is more willing to be floral than the conventionalized pewter plants of the 1903 Eastwood experiment (chapter 12). The plant’s later Quaint Manor and Austrian-looking cane panels (about 1914) show a factory watching Vienna and the English revival at the same time it watched Syracuse. Cathers will keep a reader from calling all of this “Stickley.” The tag already did that work if anyone looked.

Quality is a range. Grand Rapids could ship a first-rate case and a thin chair in the same season. The 1912 price list is the document that makes the range visible: numbers next to plates, not a speech about honesty. A historian who uses only the prettiest surviving examples will invent a boutique. A historian who uses only the cheapest will invent a villain. The catalog is the middle.

Albert’s “irascibility” appears in the biographical asides. It is useless for the furniture. What the furniture needs is the fact that he hired designers — Smith for Bewdley, others whose names the Marek preface is more likely to recover than a magazine paragraph — and that he kept the Quaint word through Colonial revival, which is a factory’s way of surviving a taste. Chapter 41 will take that survival as a national problem. Albert met it early, in one city, by refusing to let Quaint mean only 1904.

## Not Eastwood, not Fayetteville

The three oak shops get confused because the surname is loud. A short test helps. If the magazine is in the story, you are in Gustav’s world. If the mark says Handcraft or Onondaga or “the work of L. & J.G.,” you are in Fayetteville. If a brass tag says Quaint, you are in Grand Rapids. If there is no mark, you are in the market’s fog, which is where most American Mission actually lived.

Albert was not Gustav’s western branch. He had been in Grand Rapids for a decade before *The Craftsman* existed. He was not Leopold’s partner after 1900. He was the brother who stayed inside the city’s system and let the system make him rich enough that later writers still mention a fortune and a difficult temperament in the same sentence. The fortune is a Grand Rapids fact if the tax rolls say so `[CITE NEEDED]`. The temperament is gossip. The catalogs are evidence.

When the 1918 *Furniture World* notice gathered Leopold, Gustav, and John George under one heading (chapter 18), Albert’s name is the one a careful reader looks for and does not always find in the short version. Some reunion stories add Stickley Brothers of Grand Rapids to a 1916–18 Associated Cabinetmakers scheme; others keep the Michigan plant out. `[VERIFY Albert’s participation against minutes, not against a later clamp mark that wishes the family were one.]` This chapter will not drag him into a Syracuse letterhead to complete a set. His set is Catalogue No. 28, Catalogue No. 38, and a brass tag.

## A factory city’s Arts and Crafts

Grand Rapids did not need Stickley Brothers in order to have Mission furniture. It needed Stickley Brothers in order to have a Mission line that could spend the family name. The rest of the city — the Record, the markets, the finishing rooms — did what it always did: absorb a style, grade it, price it, and offer it in oak and in something else. Limbert would do a version with cutouts (chapter 23). Lifetime would do a middle-market version (chapter 24). Albert did a version that still said Stickley on the door and Quaint on the goods.

That combination irritated purists then and confuses search boxes now. It is also why a history of American Arts and Crafts furniture that stops at Eastwood is a partial history. The movement’s American life is a reception inside a factory system already running. Chapter 2 said Ruskin did not supply the quartersaw. Albert’s plant is what the quartersaw looks like when no one is quoting Venice.

The leftover fact is the 1912 price list at the back of Catalogue No. 38, dated 1 January, keyed to the plates, mahogany and oak on the same terms. A moral that can be priced in two woods is a Grand Rapids moral. The next essay puts that list next to a Fayetteville list and an Eastwood list and watches what a buyer did when the surname repeated and the numbers did not.

Quaint Furniture is a trade name with a smile in it. Craftsman is a trade name with a sermon in it. Albert’s plant could print both moods if a buyer wanted English and oak in the same season. That flexibility is Grand Rapids. It is not a failure to be Syracuse.
