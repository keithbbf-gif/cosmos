# Rose Valley’s Gothic Oak

The pins are carved as figures. They go through the trestle of a white-oak library table, 29½ × 66 × 39½ inches, stained dark, and if you pull them the table comes apart. The Metropolitan Museum’s example (1991.145) was made in 1904 in the Rose Valley Shops, Delaware County, Pennsylvania. A workshop ledger and a later pencil note on a photograph point to George K. Crozer Jr. of Philadelphia as the man who ordered it; a letter acknowledging receipt, priced at fifty dollars, is dated April 1904. The top is held with double-dovetail — butterfly — keys. The ends carry Gothic tracery a Mission slat would not know what to do with. William Lightfoot Price (1861–1916) designed it. He was a Philadelphia architect, not a Cotswold smith, and the village he had incorporated three years earlier was an American mill wreck on Ridley Creek, locally nicknamed Rogue’s Valley before he tried to make the rose mean something else.

This chapter refuses the easy English picture. Rose Valley is often introduced as if a Gloucestershire workshop had been crated and sent to the Main Line. The incorporation papers of 17 July 1901 are more precise and less pretty. Price and M. Hawley McLanahan wanted “the manufacture of structures, articles, materials and products involving artistic handicraft” used in the finishing and furnishing of houses. Price had bought about eighty acres of the old Rose Valley Mills on 29 April. There was a burned textile mill, a small gristmill, mill-workers’ houses in bad repair. Furniture making began in October 1901, in the rebuilt mill. The Gothic oak came from that sequence: a Philadelphia practice, a dead Pennsylvania industry, a medieval joinery Price could defend in print. It did not come from a transplanted hamlet of English wheelwrights.

## Price, McLanahan, and a village that had already failed

Price’s architectural office — Price and McLanahan, 1624 Walnut Street in one account, 1624 Chestnut in another `[VERIFY: street]` — was the other showroom. There was no national catalog. There were no set prices in the Lifetime sense. Most pieces were commissioned. The Decorative Arts Trust’s later summary is right about the range: Gothic, and also Mission, and also Colonial Revival, interpreted by foreign-born woodworkers led by John Maene (carving from 1902 in the Rose Valley Museum’s account). Edward Maene’s Philadelphia shop at 309 Griscom Street appears in the Chipstone 2012 article as a parallel bench, clay maquettes, and at least one cabinet that never reached its patron. The Rose Valley brand — a rose, a V, a buckled belt for fellowship — is on some surviving furniture and not on other pieces that construction would otherwise assign to the shop. Chipstone is honest: a marking system, if there was one, is not yet understood.

The village conversion was fast in the telling and slower in the payroll. By 1905 the wrecked hamlet had been made to look pastoral. Writers who liked William Morris’s *News from Nowhere* (1890) said the place looked as if it had stepped out of that book. That is a periodical sentence, not a bill of lading. Price had read Morris. He had also designed houses for Philadelphia clients who already owned Stickley settles — Yorklynne, built for John O. Gilmore, photographed about 1903 with Rose Valley chairs and a Craftsman box settle in the same room. Glenmede, for George S. Graham, kept another Stickley settle. The Chipstone article uses those rooms to keep Price from becoming a purist. An architect who sells Gothic trestle tables can also specify Syracuse oak when the inglenook wants it. Chapter 1’s three names are in these interiors without anyone asking them to be one name.

Horace Traubel edited *The Artsman*, which ran from October 1903 through April 1907 and was printed in the valley. The first number includes a drawing of a small branded trestle table (vol. 1, no. 1, p. 12) that Chipstone can still match to an object. Price wrote in the magazine; so did the community’s other voices. *The Artsman* is not *The Craftsman*. It did not have Stickley’s circulation or Stickley’s furniture list. It is the paper a small association issued to explain itself to itself and to the few outsiders who subscribed. Use it that way. A quoted Price sentence became, later, the title of Wendy Kaplan’s 1987 MFA survey — *The Art that is Life* — which is a nice afterlife for a mill-village monthly and not a reason to treat Rose Valley as the movement’s capital.

## Loose oak, fixed oak, and a Gothic that is a structure

Price and, the Chipstone author notes, Stickley both talked about a “structural idea”: construction as the design. On a Rose Valley Gothic table the talk is visible. Boards are thick. Joinery is meant to be read. Butterfly keys sit in the top. Pins are carved and, on the “loose” pieces, removable. The Decorative Arts Trust’s distinction is the one to keep. Loose construction: interlocking pierced tenons, the object able to come apart for moving, storage, or a medieval fantasy of the traveling table. Fixed construction: mortise-and-tenon locked with pegs and glue, not meant to travel. Portability, Chipstone suggests, may have mattered because the furniture was not sold through a string of city showrooms. It was sold from an architect’s office and by advertisement in *House and Garden* and in *The Artsman*. A table that knocks down is a table that can leave Walnut Street.

The Gothic is not a revivalist costume in the Chipstone argument, and it is not a costume in the Rose Valley Museum’s argument either. Ryan Berley’s later remarks, as quoted in *The Magazine Antiques*, are that the shop “operated on its own terms.” Carved Gothic chairs, the most common form, are rarely exact pairs. A simple chair now at the Met (2021.14.28) shows lancets and tracery in the back like a window that forgot its glass, a saddle seat, splayed tapered legs. The museum’s note repeats the dismantling tenons. Rich Americans, the Thunderbird Lodge history admits, already had a taste for dark carved oak that ran from the 1870s through the 1920s. How, then, is this Arts and Crafts and not a leftover sideboard from a church-goods shop? Price’s answer was in *The Artsman*: usefulness, beauty, visible making, a fellowship mark. The economic answer is the old Morris problem. Hand-carved oak at $150 for a grand table, next to a Stickley dining table the Lodge history prices at $66 in 1904, is a reform that only some households could join. The shop was busy anyway. The order book and the in-progress photographs suggest as much. It closed in 1906, the usual explanations being skilled labor and the cost of the standard.

John (and Edward) Maene’s carving is the hand a connoisseur looks for: more plastic, Chipstone says, than Edward’s work for his own Philadelphia jobs, perhaps because clay maquettes were modeled on Griscom Street and copied in wood on Ridley Creek. Henry Hetzel, a resident, made a “Rose Valley Gothic Bench” that *The Artsman* published in August 1905 as designed, drawn, and built by him. The House of the Democrat, photographed in 1906 for *American Homes and Gardens*, shows his furniture in a Price interior. The shop was not only Price. It was Price’s drawings and a small crew. A few hundred pieces, the Met’s table record says, each varying, before 1906. That is Byrdcliffe’s order of magnitude, not Limbert’s.

## St. Louis, Boston, and the offices on Walnut Street

Rose Valley sent work out. The Chipstone article lists the Society of Arts and Crafts, Boston; the Guild of Arts and Crafts, New York; the St. Louis World’s Fair of 1904 — three chairs, a bench, a “Gothic table.” Most of the public life, it says, was still advertisement. *House and Garden* could carry a Price notice to readers who would never walk Ridley Creek. The fair that same year gave Onken his Horti (chapter 25) and Charles Greene a Japanese intensification some later writers prefer to a 1906 voyage (chapter 28). Price’s Gothic table in St. Louis is the Pennsylvania answer to those other 1904 objects: not inlay, not a void, not a cloud lift. A pin you can pull.

Robert Judson Clark’s 1972 Princeton exhibition put Rose Valley furniture on the American map; Kaplan 1987 used a Price phrase for a national title. The Chipstone 2012 essay is the furniture-specific upgrade: order books at the Athenaeum of Philadelphia, photographs of Tower House, Yorklynne, the Shakespeare cabinet maquettes, the Crozer table’s paper trail. Catherine Hoover Voorsanger bought the Met table after it had appeared in *The Art that is Life*. Robert Edwards, who had owned it, later found the Crozer inscription and the April 1904 letter. A second table, made for Louis Clarke’s 1901 Price house, turned up looking identical. Uniqueness was a hope. The shop, like any shop, could repeat a cartoon and change a carved end.

## Not a Cotswold

The Cotswolds — Chipping Campden, Ashbee’s Guild after 1902, the American magazine dream of an English village that had solved industry by leaving it — are a real English history and a bad Pennsylvania metaphor. Rose Valley was a mill village that industry had already left. Price moved artists and a furniture crew into the wreck and wrote Gothic essays. The workers were often not residents; the Lodge history says most of the woodworkers, and the potter William Percival Jervis, were non-residents. Jervis’s pottery, authorized to use the mark, is the other craft the association actually institutionalized (1904). Bookbinding and metal are in the founding sentence and thinner on the ground. This is not Ashbee’s guild removed to a limestone street. It is a Philadelphia architect’s association on a creek, with a monthly, a brand, and a five-year furniture shop.

Calling it a transplant flatters England and skips the American facts: an abandoned Pennsylvania textile site, a Main Line clientele, a Gothic Price had already been thinking about as an architect, a structural idea he could share with Stickley without sharing a slat. The chairs look medieval because Price wanted construction to look like construction and because Gothic tracery is a way to carve a back without turning it into Mission. The tables look medieval because a loose tenon is a medieval solution to a modern hallway. The village looks medieval in photographs because boarded mills and new gardens photograph that way. None of that requires a Cotswold origin. It requires Ridley Creek and a man who died in 1916, the year *The Craftsman* printed its last number.

The Borough of Rose Valley was chartered in December 1923. The association’s tax life dragged later. Thunderbird Lodge, 1904, is the house museum that now keeps the argument on the ground. The Met table still has its pins. They still come out. That is the residual fact. A style that advertised honesty as a feeling here made honesty a hardware problem. The next essay leaves the pins for Pasadena, where two brothers were already building houses whose furniture had not yet become the Gamble dining room.
