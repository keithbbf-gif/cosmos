# Hubbard’s Campus at East Aurora

The Roycroft Inn faces South Grove Street in East Aurora, New York, a village twenty miles southeast of Buffalo. The door and the first rooms were not drawn as a hotel. They were a house and a print shop that ran out of space. Elbert Hubbard opened a press here in 1895. He printed *The Philistine*, a little magazine with a chip on its shoulder, and then books that looked handmade even when the edition numbers got large. Guests came to see the man who wrote them. The guests needed beds. The beds needed chairs. The chairs, once they existed, were for sale. That is the campus order. Printing first. Furniture because a building is empty without it, and because a visitor will buy what he sat on.

Hubbard is not Gustav Stickley’s copy. The dates alone refuse the claim. Roycroft is 1895. United Crafts is 1901. Hubbard had already been a partner in Larkin’s Buffalo soap business; he left that world with money and a taste for preaching to a customer. Stickley had already been a chair manufacturer in Binghamton and Eastwood. They shared English books, a public, and a decade. They did not share a plant. A series that folds East Aurora into Syracuse is doing what a search box does. This chapter keeps the Inn’s porch on its own street.

## Larkin money and an English summer

Elbert Green Hubbard was born 19 June 1856 in Bloomington, Illinois, the son of a country doctor. Soap, not oak, paid for the campus. He sold for the Larkin Soap Company, became a partner, married Bertha Crawford, and moved the household to East Aurora in the 1880s. The Larkin years matter to a furniture reader because they taught him premiums, catalogs, and the “valued customer” letter — the same tools he later used on books and chairs. He left Larkin in 1892 with enough money to try novels and a term as a special student at Harvard. The novels are not this series’ problem. The leftover capital is.

In 1894 he crossed to England and Ireland. He later told readers he had stood with William Morris at Kelmscott. Morris’s surviving letters do not mention him. The Kelmscott *Chaucer* he claimed to have seen on press was not yet printing that summer. Elbert Hubbard II — Bert — writing in *The Fra* in February 1916, remembered Cockerell and a Morris who was sick in bed `[VERIFY the 1916 Fra recollection against a Morris calendar]`. The furniture series does not need the handshake. It needs the fact that Hubbard came home wanting a press that looked like an English one and a village that could be photographed as a guild. Chapter 9 already refused to make Morris Stickley’s employer. This chapter refuses to make Morris Hubbard’s host on the strength of a lecture anecdote.

What he did print, once he was home, was a monthly series Putnam issued as *Little Journeys* — visits, some of them imaginary, to the houses of the great — and then, when Putnam was no longer the point, the same series from his own shop. The English trip became copy. The copy became a reason to own a press. The press became a campus.

## 1895: a magazine, then a shop

The first printing in East Aurora is sometimes given as early 1895 at the Pendennis Press in the Regulator Building on East Main, with Henry P. Taber and William McIntosh. Hubbard acquired *The Philistine* and the Roycroft Press from Taber on 19 November 1895 `[VERIFY that closing date against the Buffalo architectural notes]`. By 1898 the work had moved to South Grove. A larger print shop across the street was finished in 1901. The word Roycroft — royal craft, king’s craft, a Hubbard etymology that does not need to be true to have been useful; also a nod to the seventeenth-century London printers Samuel and Thomas Roycroft — sat on the title pages like a brand.

In 1899 he needed filler and wrote a short piece about a lieutenant and a message in the Spanish-American War. *A Message to Garcia* left the magazine and became a booklet that factories and armies handed out by the tens and then hundreds of thousands. The campus literature still calls it a third-best-seller of its kind; the exact rank is a publicist’s number. `[VERIFY circulation claims against a bibliography, not against the Inn’s tour script.]` What the furniture historian needs is simpler. Garcia paid for brick and stone. The 1901 print shop, the chapel, the later copper shop and furniture shop — those walls have a pamphlet under them.

Hubbard’s voice in *The Philistine* is a problem for a furniture series. He preached work, loyalty, the dignity of the job, and his own importance. He quoted Ruskin when it suited and sold advertising when it suited. Eileen Boris’s labor history and the later Roycroft monographs (Koch and after) will not let the preaching stand in for a wage. This chapter will not either. The campus employed, at its peak, on the order of five hundred people. Five hundred is a factory number, even when the factory calls itself a community of Roycrofters. Exercise breaks, lectures, and concerts appear in the friendly accounts. Payroll appears in the unfriendly ones. Both can be true. A 2006 Hyde Collection account of the campus put the contradiction in one sentence the tours prefer to skip: Hubbard ran it like a commune, but it was always a for-profit commune.

Alice Moore Hubbard, his second wife, is in the suffrage record and in the ship’s record. She had been an East Aurora schoolteacher. Their daughter Miriam was born in 1894; Bertha Hubbard obtained a divorce in 1901; Elbert and Alice married in 1904. After that date she is a partner in the management, not a footnote to the Inn’s china. *The Fra*, which he began in 1908, carries her name as well as his. The campus was a marriage as well as a press. The marriage ended on 7 May 1915.

## How a press grew shops

Hubbard’s own later story, told in the furniture catalogs and in sale notes that quote him, is the one to keep because it is a maker’s story even if it is also a salesman’s. The place got too small when they began to bind books. They built a wing. Then another wing. To keep the three carpenters busy, he set them to making furniture for the rooms. They made it as well as they could. People came along and bought it. The sequence — bindery, wings, leftover carpenters, visitors with money — is how East Aurora differs from Eastwood. Stickley designed a line and then a magazine to explain it. Hubbard printed a magazine and then filled the buildings the magazine made necessary.

A blacksmith shop of about 1899 produced wrought-iron latches, andirons, and lighting. A copper shop followed; campus notes disagree whether the 1902 stone building was already copper or a blacksmith shop converted in 1904 `[VERIFY the 1902/1904 copper-shop sequence against the Sanborn and the Koch chronology]`. The furniture shop, wood, went up in 1904. Leather, which had begun as binding, became a goods line and moved about 1905. Pottery experiments around 1900 failed; later china for the Inn came from Buffalo China `[VERIFY the Buffalo China service mark versus any early “R” ceramic]`. Dard Hunter’s glass and the Vienna year are chapter 21’s problem. The orb-and-cross on the oak, and the copper that shared the mark, are chapter 20’s. This chapter only needs the campus plan: print shop, bindery, inn, copper, oak, a chapel that was also a hall, paths a visitor still walks.

The Inn opened for guests in 1905, though some campus notes push the first rooms to 1903 `[VERIFY first paying guest against a register, not a brochure]`. Reception rooms occupied spaces that had been dining room, bank, and, in one reconstruction, part of a cabinet shop after the press moved. Alexis Jean Fournier’s murals in the salon are the painter’s claim on a furniture campus. The Inn is why Roycroft furniture has a public face that Eastwood’s factory never quite had. You can sleep in the argument. You could not, in 1910, spend the night at Eastwood.

Fourteen original buildings around South Grove and Main would later be named a National Historic Landmark (1986). The landmark is an afterlife. In Hubbard’s lifetime the campus was a going concern that sold books, copper, leather, and oak through its own rooms and, the friendly accounts say, through hundreds of retail outlets. `[VERIFY the “300 outlets” figure; it travels.]` Sears would later, under the son, take furniture — a short experiment and someone else’s decade. Before 1915 the important customer was often the person already on the porch.

## What Hubbard was selling besides chairs

He was selling a character. The Stetson, the hair, the lecture circuit, the pretence that a village in Erie County was a medieval shop — those are why later writers cannot leave him alone and why a furniture chapter has to be careful. After *Garcia* he was a platform name. Clubwomen and businessmen paid to hear work, loyalty, and the East Aurora experiment. When he was in town he filled the Inn with invited guests — writers, politicians, people who would be useful in the next *Philistine*. The furniture they sat on was the catalog. That is not a Stickley house-plan pitch. It is a hotel pitch.

The character bought the campus time and labor. It also bought a reputation for bluff that still sits on the objects. A coarse early table and a fine bookcase both say Roycroft. The character does not tell you which you are looking at. Chapter 20 will take the orb-and-cross and the quality range. This chapter takes the man who ordered the brand.

He was not a designer of record in the Ellis sense, and he should not be made one. German-trained cabinetmakers — James Cadzow and Albert Danner are the names the furniture literature repeats — ran the bench `[VERIFY both names against a payroll or a signed piece]`. Hubbard’s gift was to keep them busy and to write the caption. The caption is half the American Arts and Crafts problem. Ruskin wrote a paragraph (chapter 2). Hubbard wrote a monthly. Stickley hired Irene Sargent to write a better monthly. Three captions, three shops.

East Aurora is closer to the English campus fantasy than Syracuse was. Ashbee left London for Chipping Campden in 1902; Hubbard had already been playing guild in a New York village for seven years. The play included medieval type, handmade paper, illumination, and a mark borrowed from a monastic colophon. It also included a souvenir trade and a lecture style that Morris would have hated and that American clubwomen paid to hear. Partial uptake, again. The furniture shows the part that could be priced. The books show the part that could be mailed. On 7 November 1907 the *East Aurora Advertiser* reported that the printing shop was mailing 2,500 packages a day. That is not a Kelmscott number. It is a Larkin number wearing a medieval colophon.

## 7 May 1915

Elbert and Alice Hubbard were at sea on the *Lusitania*, bound for Europe. He said he wanted to see the war for his readers. A German submarine sank the ship off the Old Head of Kinsale, Ireland. Both Hubbards died. He was fifty-eight. The date is two months and two weeks after Gustav Stickley’s bankruptcy adjudication. The two facts are not a parable. They are a bad spring for American Arts and Crafts letterheads. One man lost a corporation. The other lost his life. The shops they left behaved differently. Eastwood’s corporation wound down. Roycroft passed to Elbert Hubbard II — Bert — and kept making goods into the 1920s and, with thinner and thinner justification, until a 1938 bankruptcy.

This chapter stops at the ship. Bert’s Sears experiment, the taste-change, and the 1938 closing belong to the death-of-the-style essay (chapter 41) and to a Roycroft afterlife this series does not need to tour. The Inn’s later restoration, the Wendt Foundation money, the 1995 reopening — those are preservation facts, not 1915 facts. The 1915 fact is that the press’s founder and the campus’s other adult authority were gone on the same afternoon.

What remained on the ground that summer was a village industry with a famous dead name, a furniture shop two years past its first decade, a copper shop, a bindery, and an Inn that still had keys. The workers who knew how to cut a tenon did not sink. The brand did not sink. The voice that had filled *The Philistine* sank. Roycroft without Hubbard’s voice is a quieter factory. Quieter is not worse for the chairs. It may be worse for the myth. This series prefers the chairs.

## A campus, not a copy

Compare, once, and then stop. Stickley: factory in a Syracuse suburb, magazine as argument, house plans as product, a New Jersey farm that failed as a school, a midtown loft that failed as a headquarters. Hubbard: press in a village, magazine as personality, buildings that became an Inn, shops added to keep craftsmen busy and guests supplied, no midtown lease of that scale, death by war, not by creditors. The shared English reading list does not make the second a knockoff of the first. If anyone copied a mood, American money copied an English mood, and both men were in that business.

The leftover object is the Inn door — “Head, Heart, Hand” is the motto the tours like — and the fact that the door opened onto a press before it opened onto a hotel register. Furniture historians who start with the orb-and-cross on a bookcase have already skipped the reason the bookcase exists. The next essay starts on that mark anyway, because the mark is how the furniture entered the trade, and because the mark sat on work that ran from coarse to fine. Not every piece a masterpiece. That is the East Aurora sentence the market still does not like to print.
