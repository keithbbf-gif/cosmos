# *The Craftsman* as a Monthly Argument

The first number of *The Craftsman* is dated October 1901. The title page says Eastwood, United Crafts. Gustave Stickley is the editor. Inside, Irene Sargent wrote every article. There is no price list in the front of the book. There is William Morris, five times. The magazine’s own later memory called the first effort “a small illustrated pamphlet, devoted largely to an exposition of Craftsman ideals.” The pamphlet is already an argument: a house and a chair belong to one life, and a monthly can teach that life while a factory in the same suburb builds the chair. Treating the run as a product catalog with essays attached is the mistake this chapter exists to block. Treating it as a pure reform journal that happened to share a publisher with a furniture plant is the opposite mistake. The pitch is one pitch.

Sargent (1852–1932) was a professor of the history of fine arts at Syracuse University from 1895 until her death. She wrote all five pieces in volume 1, number 1; all eight in November; all four in December. From October 1901 until March 1902 she wrote twenty-six of thirty-three features. In the first eight volumes, October 1901 to September 1905, Cleota Reed counted eighty-four contributions. Then she stopped. Robert Judson Clark later called her the initial force behind the publication. Barry Sanders called her the guiding spirit of the early years. Reed’s 1979 *Courier* essay is blunter: the voice of the early *Craftsman* is Sargent’s. Stickley owned the plant and the imprint. She wrote the sentences that taught readers how to talk.

## What the first number actually held

Cleota Reed’s 1981 bibliography lists the October contents with pages. “William Morris: Some Thoughts Upon His Life, Art, and Influence,” 1–14. “William Morris: His Socialistic Career,” 15–24. “The Firm of Morris and Company, Decorators,” 25–32. “The Opera of ‘Patience’ and ‘The Aesthetic Movement,’” 33–38. “Morris and Burne-Jones,” 39–45. A foreword, unsigned, accepted “the Morris principle” for United Crafts: more leisure and culture for the workman, drawing as a basis of the manual arts. The joiner’s compass and the Flemish *Als ik kan* appear as the shop’s device, “branded upon every object produced in the workshop of the Guild,” with a date and the signature of the Master — a claim the surviving furniture only sometimes obeys.

The first number is not a Syracuse factory tour. It is an American professor explaining an English decorator to a household that might buy an oak settle. Sargent’s Morris is a life, a politics, a firm, a joke (*Patience*), and a friendship with Burne-Jones. That is a syllabus. It is also a genealogy the plant needed. A new American line, six months after Grand Rapids and Tobey, required ancestors. Morris was the ancestor who could carry both a socialist career and a decorating catalog. Sargent was willing to write both. Stickley, in the foreword, was willing to accept the principle and remain the owner. Chapter 2 already named the gap. This chapter names the monthly that printed it.

November 1901 is Ruskin: a life, quotations, *The Seven Lamps*, “The Rise and Decadence of the Craftsman,” a review of Bradley Gilman’s *Back to the Soil*. December is medieval crafts guilds, “so important to the movement as a model,” and a note on a new Irish industry of hand-woven rugs. The first quarter of the magazine is a course. Furniture appears as illustration and as implication. The house, as a printed plan, is still slightly ahead — E. G. W. Dietrich’s first official “Craftsman House” is May 1903. What October 1901 already wants is a reader who will not separate the chair from the life.

## House and chair as one pitch

Mary Ann Smith’s sentence is the one the house-plan chapters will need: many architects designed furniture for houses they had designed; Stickley designed houses to fit his furniture. The monthly is how that inversion was taught. A reader who subscribed saw oak in a room, then a room in a plan, then a plan that could be ordered. The Craftsman Homebuilders Club, from 1904, sent elevations and interiors to members. *Craftsman Homes* (1909) and *More Craftsman Homes* (1912) bound the argument into books. Built-ins, an inglenook, a settle that is part of the wall — chapter 13 will walk a plate. This chapter only needs the editorial fact. The magazine was not a catalog that sometimes showed a house. It was a house argument that could not work unless the furniture existed.

Greene and Greene clipped it. Edward Bosley notes that Charles Greene’s work begins to show an intimate awareness of the magazine’s aesthetic message, and that the Culbertson house in Pasadena (1902) already held pieces illustrated in early numbers. That is California reading Syracuse, not Syracuse inventing Pasadena. The Gamble dining room (chapter 29) is another shop. The point here is the medium. A monthly can travel farther than a traveling man. Wilde had needed 141 halls. Sargent needed a press and a mail sack.

The furniture pages — when they come, and they do come — are not innocent. A settle in an inglenook is a product shot with a moral caption. United Crafts and then, from 1904, the Craftsman Workshops needed the caption. Peter Betjemann’s later academic account of words and things in Stickley’s business is useful if kept short: the magazine and the factory were founded near each other on purpose. Readers would want the theory if they bought the chair. Readers would want the chair if they bought the theory. That is not a scandal. It is a publishing model. The scandal would be to pretend the model was only one of its halves.

## What a monthly could do that a price list could not

A price list cannot run “William Morris: His Socialistic Career.” A price list cannot review René Bazin or tell a subscriber which dog belongs in a Craftsman house (an Airedale, if you trust a later number). A price list cannot argue civic improvement, manual training, or the servant problem in a small plan. *The Craftsman* did all of that, and also sold furniture and, from 1905, did more of it from New York City as the imprint moved downstate. The University of Wisconsin’s digital run is the way a reader now checks a number instead of trusting a caption. Marilyn Fish’s *New Craftsman Index* (1997) is the paper key.

Sargent’s departure after 1905 is a change of voice. The imprint was already moving toward New York City that year; Eastwood remained the plant, but the monthly wanted a downstate address. The magazine did not die. It became more of a house-and-garden and civic monthly with furniture in it, and more of Stickley’s own signed preaching. A reader of the May 1903 number got Dietrich’s first official Craftsman House. A reader of 1909 could buy *Craftsman Homes* bound. The 1913–15 numbers still argued a life while the Manhattan lease (chapter 15) ate the accounts. The table of contents kept doing work a price list cannot do. Harvey Ellis, on staff briefly in 1903–04, changed the drawings and the inlay (chapter 12). The house plans multiplied. German and Austrian exhibitions were reviewed, sometimes with praise (St. Louis, 1904), sometimes with a scolding for the Viennese Secession as a parasite on English roots. The range is the argument’s range. A catalog from Eastwood, even a thick one, could not have scolded Vienna.

What the monthly could not do is make the plant a guild. United Crafts remained Stickley’s company. Profit-sharing language and a compass mark do not rewrite a deed. Winterthur’s corporate sequence is still the check: Stickley-Simonds, Gustav Stickley Company, United Crafts, The Craftsman, Inc., bankruptcy 24 March 1915 (some accounts say 23 March; the Winterthur finding aid’s 24 March is the date chapter 1 used). The last *Craftsman* is December 1916. The title then folded toward *The Art World*. Fifteen years and three months of numbers. Not a catalog run. Not a monastery.

## Against the catalog-only reading

Dealers still pull plates from *The Craftsman* to date a chair. That is legitimate use. The magazine illustrated shop furniture, sometimes with numbers a collector can match. It is a primary source for the look. It is a bad sole source for the business. Eastwood’s machines, the Crouse stables showroom, the later Manhattan Craftsman Building — those are other papers (chapter 11, chapter 15). A reader who knows only the monthly will think the chairs were argued into existence. A reader who knows only a 1910 catalog will think the essays were filler. Both readers are holding one half of the pitch.

Grand Rapids trade papers, in the same years, ran price and style without Morris’s socialist career. McHugh’s notices in *House and Garden* ran a decorator’s adjective without a syllabus. *The Craftsman* is the American place where the English books, the Syracuse plant, and the suburban house plan were forced into one table of contents. That force is why the magazine still gets mistaken for the movement. It was a movement’s monthly, written at first by one woman in Syracuse, published by a man who made furniture two miles away. It was not the only American Arts and Crafts. Boston’s society, Chicago’s classes, Roycroft’s press, the Greenes’ office, Rohlfs’s Buffalo shop — none of them required a subscription. They could still be pulled into the magazine’s field, and often were, as subjects.

The last numbers, 1915–16, are a monthly arguing past a bankruptcy. The furniture plant was already in trouble. The house plans were still going out. The tone is not a close-out sale. That refusal to become a catalog even when the accounts needed a sale is either integrity or denial. Chapter 15 will have the lease numbers. This chapter only needs the last date: December 1916. A product catalog would have stopped when the stock stopped. An argument ran until the imprint was absorbed.

## A syllabus that sold oak

Open October 1901 again. Five Morris essays, a compass, a foreword about leisure and drawing. No shop number 155. No ammonia-fuming recipe. The reader is being trained. By 1904 the same reader can order a house plan and a settle. The training worked, if working means that Craftsman as a word leaked into American English and attached itself to oak it did not always make. The training also failed, if failing means that most of the oak sold in America under related names came from Grand Rapids and from Sears and from McHugh, who did not need Sargent’s Morris. The monthly is not the market. It is the argument the market could quote.

Sargent wrote the first three numbers alone. That fact should stay in the room whenever a caption says “Stickley’s magazine.” It was his imprint. It was her prose. The house-and-chair pitch is theirs together: he had the plant and the later plans; she had the genealogy that made a suburban settle feel like a chapter in a European story. American reception, again. Morris did not edit *The Craftsman*. A Syracuse professor did.

The next essay leaves the title page for the plant that had to exist if the pitch were to be more than type: Eastwood, a shop number, machines and hands, fumed quartersawn white oak, and a Syracuse showroom in the Crouse stables that was already calling itself the Craftsman Building.
