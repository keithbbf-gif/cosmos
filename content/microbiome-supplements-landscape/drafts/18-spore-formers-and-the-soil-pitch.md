**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). Spore survival is not a health claim. "Soil-based" is not a regulatory class.

Bacterial endospores can sit through heat, dryness, and a week on a dock that would ruin GG. That is why some houses love *Bacillus* and *Weizmannia* lines for room-temperature SKUs. Stability is a real advantage. It is also the entire advantage until a strain-specific human file shows up. "Soil-based organisms" (SBO) is not a scientific class Hill 2014 recognizes. It is a dialect that bundles spore formers, a wilderness story, and a suggestion that the modern gut is under-soiled. The wilderness story is not a deposit number.

## Names that moved

*Bacillus coagulans* was reassigned to *Weizmannia coagulans* (Gupta et al., 2020; PMID 33052805; `[VERIFY]` pagination when you open the PDF). Labels lag. Papers lag differently. A COA that still says *B. coagulans* may be describing the same isolate as a paper that says *Weizmannia*. It may also be describing a different line with a convenient old name. Ask for the strain designation.

*B. subtilis*, *B. clausii*, *B. licheniformis*, and friends occupy the rest of the aisle. Some have food histories. Some have drug histories in other countries. Some are industrial workhorses. Species-level morality is a bad habit here too. *Bacillus anthracis* is a cousin in a textbook and a reason not to treat "Bacillus" as a cuddly word. Identity testing is not optional.

## The soil pitch, taken apart

Humans encounter environmental spores. That is not evidence that a particular commercial isolate from a particular fermentation tank "restores ancestral dirt." Ancestral dirt is not a reference microbiome (drafts 03 and 06). A spore that germinates in a gut is an empirical question for that isolate, not a property of soil as a brand color.

"Found in soil" is also not a safety file. Environmental organisms can carry antibiotic-resistance genes and toxin genes. QPS and a sequenced genome are how a grown-up file talks (draft 41). A photograph of a garden is not a genome.

## Stability is not adequacy

A spore count that survives two years is still a count of *that* isolate. If the human study used vegetative cells of a different species, you have not inherited the study. If the human study does not exist, you have a stable live product with no Hill benefit. That can be sold as a live-culture food ingredient in some contexts. It cannot honestly wear "probiotic" as a participation trophy (draft 01).

Enumeration of spores versus vegetative cells is a method choice. Heat shocking to kill vegetative cells and then plating survivors is a common spore trick. Write it down. Do not compare a heat-shocked spore CFU to a GG vegetative CFU as if they were the same unit of virtue (draft 13).

## CMO and the shared spore rumor

Spore formers in a plant that also makes non-spore probiotics are a contamination worry in both directions. Houses that know this keep lines separate. Houses that do not learn it from a failed swab. Part 111 does not care about your wilderness story. It cares whether you control the process you actually run.

## Reading an SBO label

Look for genus, species, strain, deposit, end-of-shelf count, and a human paper on *that* string. If you find a dirt myth and a billion-count, you have found a vibe. Vibe is not a wave-3 organism file. GG (draft 16) is the control: boring identifiers, long paper trail. Ask SBO brands for the boring thing. If they offer a documentary about farms instead, you have the answer.

## Identity, because *Bacillus* is a wide word

*B. anthracis* is a textbook cousin and a reason not to treat the genus as cuddly. Commercial lines need strain-level ID, not a soil photo. *Weizmannia coagulans* versus a house that still says *B. coagulans* may be the same isolate or a convenient old name. Ask for the designation (Gupta 2020). *B. clausii*, *B. subtilis*, *B. licheniformis* each have their own food-versus-drug biographies in different countries. Species-level morality is a bad habit here too.

Toxin genes and resistance genes belong in the genome file (draft 41). "Found in soil" is not that file.

## Germination is an empirical claim

A spore that sits through a mailbox is not yet a cell doing a job in a person. Germination, if you need it for a mechanism story, is a measured event for that isolate, not a property of dirt as a brand color. In-vitro germination graphs are beaker cousins of draft 42.

Stability is still not adequacy (draft 01). A two-year spore count with no human endpoint is a stable live product without Hill's third word. That can be an honest, smaller claim. It cannot be a participation trophy.

## Shared rooms and the Monday swab

Spore formers in a plant that also makes non-spore lines are a contamination worry in both directions. Separate rooms, separate air, a swab program. Part 111 does not care about the wilderness documentary. Draft 43's objectionable-organism row is where the Monday swab becomes a PDF.

## Heat-shock plates and the integer that looks like courage

Enumerating spores by killing vegetative cells and plating survivors is a common method. Write it down. Do not compare that integer to GG's vegetative CFU as a moral ranking. A large spore number can be a large number of inactive tickets. Germination in a person is a different measurement. If the mechanism story needs germination, measure it for this isolate or drop the mechanism story.

Wilderness copy — ancestral dirt, indoor life, "we lost our soil" — is a restoration myth (draft 06). A garden photograph is not a genome. Ask for the genome and the designation. If the answer is a documentary, you have the answer.
