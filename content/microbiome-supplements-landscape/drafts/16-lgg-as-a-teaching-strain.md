**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). GG is a teaching isolate. Reviews of GG in antibiotic-associated diarrhea are research documents, not a reason to print a prevention claim on a consumer bottle.

Sherwood Gorbach and Barry Goldin isolated the strain that became *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG — now *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* GG — in the early 1980s; ATCC 53103 is the number a freezer understands. Gorbach's later short piece in *The American Journal of Gastroenterology* (2000) is one place the isolation story sat in print. The useful fact for this pack is not the origin myth. It is that a later reader can ask whether the organism in a paper is *that* organism. Most aisle names do not survive the question.

## What a teaching strain has that a blend lacks

A designation that follows the isolate through renames (draft 08). A deposit someone else can request. A stack of human studies that, whatever their flaws, at least *point at the same object*. Methods papers on acid tolerance and adhesion that are about GG, not about "lactobacilli." The ability to fail a test: a paper can say GG did not move this endpoint. Failure is a property of a real object. A proprietary blend that changes every year cannot fail. It can only rebrand.

None of that makes GG a vitamin. It makes GG a good example of Hill 2014's unit of evidence.

## What the review literature is, and is not

Szajewska and Kołodziej, 2015 (*Alimentary Pharmacology & Therapeutics*; PMID 26365389), published a systematic review and meta-analysis of GG in the prevention of antibiotic-associated diarrhea. That is a *research synthesis* on a named isolate and a named clinical question. Later updates may change weights and included trials. `[VERIFY]` before anyone treats a 2015 pooled number as current.

Antibiotic-associated diarrhea sits next to disease language. A supplement PDP that borrows a prevention sentence is in the wrong room (drafts 10 and 38). Educational description: researchers have studied GG in that setting. Intended use for a DTC SKU: not that sentence.

Pediatric versus adult, dose, and concomitant antibiotic class all move results. A meta-analysis is not a serving suggestion. This pack will not print a milligram or a CFU as advice.

## How the halo works

A brand prints "contains *L. rhamnosus*" and a GG citation. A brand prints GG on the front and a cousin in the hopper. A brand prints a 15-strain mix and a GG review. After 2020, even the honest old binomial on a dusty carton is a dialect. The halo is the same in each case: a famous file, a vaguer object.

Identity testing has to distinguish GG from other *rhamnosus* lines. 16S will not always do it (draft 04). A house that cannot tell them apart cannot honestly sell "GG."

## What to steal for other organisms

Steal the *shape* of the file: isolate, deposit, human studies that name the isolate, a rename history, a refusal to let the species inherit. Do not steal GG's citations for a spore, a yeast, or a next-gen anaerobe. Do not steal them for a gummy at a different dose.

Wave 3 will walk through organisms that lack GG's paper trail (*Faecalibacterium* in a bottle; draft 20) and organisms that have a trail but the wrong regulatory room (*S. boulardii* in disease rows; draft 17). GG is the control example: this is what "a strain" looks like when people have been paying attention since 1983.

If a founder has no GG and no equivalent file, the honest vocabulary is smaller: live cultures, a named isolate with no human endpoint, a research organism. Those phrases will not win a pitch meeting. They will win a later afternoon with counsel.

## What a deposit actually does

A collection number is a promise that someone will ship a vial that is supposed to be that isolate. It is not a promise that the hopper this month matches the vial. Houses still have to identity-test incoming lots against the deposit. 16S may not separate GG from other *rhamnosus* lines. A house that cannot tell them apart cannot honestly sell GG. Whole-genome or a validated strain-specific assay is how grown-up files talk (draft 43).

Patent and license weather exists around famous isolates. This pack will not referee licenses. It will say: a name on a carton without a chain to a freezer is ink.

## How reviews get flattened

Szajewska 2015 is a synthesis on a named isolate and a named clinical question. Flattening it to "GG is clinically proven" drops the population, the endpoint, the year, and the wall between research and aisle (draft 10). Later reviews may move the weights. `[VERIFY]` is mandatory before a SKU file quotes a pooled number. This pack will not print one as advice.

Pediatric versus adult, dose, and the antibiotic class are not rounding errors. A meta-analysis is not a serving suggestion.

## Stealing the shape, not the halo

Every organism in wave 3 should be asked: do you have a designation, a deposit, human studies that name you, a rename history, and a refusal to let the species inherit? Yeast (draft 17) and spores (draft 18) get the same questions in different kingdoms. Next-gen names (draft 20) usually fail the hopper question first. GG is the control that makes those failures visible.

## Incoming lot versus the freezer

A purchase order that says GG is not an identity test. Incoming lots drift. A CMO that also pours other *rhamnosus* lines will mix stories if the assay cannot see the difference. Write the assay. Keep a retain. If a paper from 1998 is the pride of the file, the 2026 hopper still has to be that isolate. License weather is counsel's job. Chain-of-identity is QA's job. This pack will not referee licenses. It will refuse a halo on a cousin.

When you teach other organisms, ask whether they can fail a test. GG can fail a test because it is a real object. A sliding blend cannot fail. It can only rebrand. That is the whole reason this isolate opens wave 3.
