Sokol, Pigneur, Watterlot, and colleagues (*PNAS*, 2008; PMID 18936492) helped make *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* famous: lower relative abundance in some people with Crohn's disease, and mechanistic work on anti-inflammatory effects in experimental systems. That is a research object with a serious paper trail. It is also a strict anaerobe. Oxygen is not a branding problem. It is a physics problem. A lot of "next-generation probiotic" decks print the name, show the paper, and skip the hopper.

## Famous, oxygen-shy, and not a vitamin

*F. prausnitzii*, *Roseburia*, some *Eubacterium*, *Akkermansia* (draft 21, which at least has a pasteurized subplot), *Christensenella* — the list of stool celebrities is long. Butyrate producers get special marketing love because butyrate is a molecule people can pronounce. A molecule in a paper is not a CFU in a bottle. Cross-feeding in the colon can make butyrate from fiber without your isolate surviving the UPS truck (draft 29).

Hill 2014 still wants live cells at an adequate dose if you want the word probiotic. A dead anaerobe is a different object (draft 23). A DNA fragment in a 16S kit is not an isolate (draft 04).

## What a manufacturing file would have to show

Anaerobic handling from seed bank to fill. Residual oxygen specs. Proof that the cells that leave the chamber are the cells on the plate at expiry, not a hardy contaminant that took over. Identity methods that work on a fastidious organism. A human study of the *finished* form. Most supplement plants were not built for this. Some drug-shaped LBP programs are. That category difference is the point of drafts 37–38 and the 2016 FDA LBP guidance.

If a house says they have solved oxygen with a coating, ask for the end-of-shelf plate in the marketed matrix, not a day-zero chamber count (drafts 14 and 42). If they say they have solved it with a "spore-like" story, ask a microbiologist. Most of these organisms do not do the *Bacillus* trick (draft 18).

## Association is not a hopper

Sokol 2008 is often read as "*F. prausnitzii* is missing; put it back." Crohn's is a disease. Putting it back, if anyone ever does it as a therapy, is a drug-shaped project. A supplement page that tells a customer their kit is "low in *Faecalibacterium*" and then sells a bottle with the name on it has crossed a diagnostic-looking line and a manufacturing-looking line in the same afternoon (draft 31).

Even the association is not a law of nature. Pipelines, cohorts, and disease activity move the taxon. A consumer V4 run is not Sokol's methods.

## How to write about these organisms without becoming a catalog

Name the paper. Name the oxygen problem. Name the regulatory box if someone is actually developing a live product for a disease. Refuse the SKU sentence. Refuse "next-gen" as a quality adjective — it is a chronology of hype.

Draft 21 is the exception that proves the rule: *Akkermansia* showed up in human work in a pasteurized arm that complicates the "live" story. Read that next if you want a case where the interesting product may not be a probiotic under Hill's first word.

## Why the names keep getting printed anyway

Stool papers love them. Butyrate is pronounceable. "Next-gen" sounds like a date stamp that improves quality. A kit can print a red bar. None of those is a hopper. A founder who added *F. prausnitzii* to a deck last quarter should be asked for anaerobic process control and an expiry plate in the marketed matrix. If the answer is a Sokol 2008 slide, the name is ink.

Functional redundancy (draft 03) makes the "replace this taxon" story weaker: other organisms can do pieces of the job, and fiber can feed the table (draft 29). A celebrity taxon is a poor cartoon of a lunch.

## Drug-shaped honesty

If someone is actually developing a live anaerobe for a disease, they are in the 2016 LBP conversation (drafts 37–38). That is a harder, cleaner sentence than "supplement, but next-gen." IBD-adjacent association papers stay in the research room. A kit-to-cart loop on a Crohn's-famous name is a diagnostic-looking line plus a manufacturing-looking line in one afternoon (draft 31).

## What an editor should cut

"Next-gen" as a compliment. "Missing in modern guts" as a deficiency. A chamber photo posed as the brand's plant if it is not. Any sentence that treats Sokol 2008 as a recipe. Keep the oxygen problem and the paper. Draft 21 is the pasteurized subplot that complicates "live" even when the plant can grow the organism.

## A manufacturing interview in five asks

Where is the seed bank held, and at what oxygen. What is the fill environment. What is residual oxygen in the sealed unit. What grows on the expiry plate, and is it the isolate or a hardy contaminant. Is the intended use a food use or a disease use. Four blanks means the name is ink. A disease yes means draft 38.

Butyrate fan-club slides should be sent to draft 29. Cross-feeding and fiber are how a colon makes the molecule without your isolate surviving UPS. Sokol 2008 remains a research paper about an association and a mechanism, not a recipe for a Monday hopper.

## Pocket card

Famous in stool papers. Dead in air. Butyrate is a table, not a mascot (draft 29). Sokol 2008 is association and mechanism, not a Monday hopper. Five asks: seed-bank oxygen, fill environment, residual oxygen, expiry identity versus contaminant, food use versus disease use. Disease use is draft 38. "Next-gen" is a chronology of hype, not a quality adjective. A kit-to-cart loop on a Crohn's-famous name is two lines crossed in one afternoon (draft 31). If the plant cannot talk like a process engineer, the name is ink.
