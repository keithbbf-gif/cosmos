This essay does not treat depression, anxiety, autism, or insomnia. It describes a research topic and a marketing dialect.

Cryan and Dinan’s 2012 *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* paper (PMID 22968153) collected evidence that gut microbes can influence brain and behavior — in animals, through routes that include neural, endocrine, and immune paths. The review is careful in ways the aisle is not. It is mostly preclinical. It is a map of a field. "Psychobiotic" arrived as a coinage for live organisms that, if the evidence existed, would affect mental health. The evidence for a consumer class of that name has not caught the coinage. The coinage caught the aisle.

## What the animal file can say

It can say the conversation is not crazy. Germ-free and colonized animals differ in stress physiology and behavior under protocols. Specific isolates have moved specific readouts in cages. Vagal and immune stories exist. Draft 11 already spent the translation warning. Gut-brain is the most emotionally expensive version of that warning, because the endpoints people want are diseases and because the copy writes itself.

It cannot say your anxiety is a missing strain. It cannot say a 16S bar predicts a PHQ-9. It cannot say a yogurt is an antidepressant. Those sentences are how a food-category product talks itself into a drug claim, and how a desperate reader gets a carton instead of care.

## Human trials, held at arm’s length

There are small human studies on mood scores and certain isolates. They are heterogeneous, often industry-adjacent (draft 12), often secondary scores, often in people who are not the population the carton winks at. This pack will not build a league table. If a founder has one registered trial on one isolate with a locked, non-disease endpoint, that is a file. It is not a psychobiotic category.

"Supports a calm mood" is still a claim FTC can read. It still needs competent and reliable evidence for the product. A 2012 review of mice is not that evidence.

## Autism, ADHD, and the line this pack will not cross

Community differences in those conditions appear in research. They are not a license for a DTC protocol, a kit-to-cart loop, or a parent-facing "rebalance." This paragraph is a refusal, not a review. If an editor needs sources for a later, clinician-facing, claims-guarded history, that is a different brief.

## House style

Do not use psychobiotic except to teach it as a coinage that outran the file. Do not use "gut-brain axis" as a section header on a product page. Do not put a brain on a probiotic pouch. Do not cite Cryan 2012 under a you-facing mood sentence. Point readers who are suffering toward clinicians, not toward our bibliography.

Draft 34 is the metabolic twin: another axis, another cage, another aisle.

## Coinage versus class

"Psychobiotic" names a hope: live organisms that would affect mental health if the evidence existed for a class. The evidence has not caught the coinage. Small human mood-score studies exist; they are heterogeneous, often industry-adjacent, often secondary, often in the wrong population for the carton. This pack will not build a league table. One registered trial on one isolate with a locked, non-disease endpoint is a file. It is not a category.

"Supports a calm mood" is still a claim FTC can read. Cryan and Dinan 2012 is a preclinical map. It is not that evidence. A 2012 review of mice does not go under a you-facing sentence.

## Lines this pack will not cross

Autism, ADHD, and similar conditions appear in community-difference papers. They are not a DTC protocol, a kit-to-cart loop, or a parent-facing "rebalance." This is a refusal, not a review. Readers who are suffering need clinicians, not our bibliography.

## House cuts

No brain on a pouch. No "gut-brain axis" as a product header. No psychobiotic except to teach the coinage. Draft 11 is the leap. Draft 34 is the metabolic twin. Same cage, silkier noun.
