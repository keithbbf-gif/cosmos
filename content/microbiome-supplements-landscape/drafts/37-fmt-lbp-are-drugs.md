**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). Recurrent *C. difficile* infection is a disease. This essay describes FDA approvals and a product class. It is not a treatment guide and not a comparison of products for use.

For years, fecal microbiota transplantation sat in a messy middle: hospital protocols, stool banks, enforcement discretion, and a public imagination that a neighbor’s stool was a frontier medicine. FDA’s June 2016 guidance on early clinical trials with live biotherapeutic products had already named the drug-shaped box for defined live organisms aimed at disease. Then the agency approved Rebyota (fecal microbiota, live-jslm) in November 2022 and Vowst (fecal microbiota spores, live-brpk) in April 2023 for recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection after antibiotic treatment of the episode — read the exact indication on the label, not on a podcast. Those labels are drug labels. They are the opposite of a 15-strain gummy.

## What an approval letter teaches

It teaches that a live community can be an article of commerce in the drug system: CMC, potency, safety, a defined indication, a label FDA has read. It teaches that "microbiome therapy" is not a vibe. It teaches that the endpoint was a disease endpoint. It teaches that the products are not dietary supplements and are not available because a kit was red.

It does not teach that any stool is now a medicine. It does not teach at-home transplant. It does not teach ulcerative colitis, autism, or metabolic disease — those are other research programs, other INDs, other disappointments. Mentioning them as "the future" on a supplement page is how you borrow a drug’s glamour without its file.

## Stool banks and the middle years

OpenBiome and hospital programs are history a reporter can tell. This pack will not turn them into a how-to or a nostalgia for unregulated stool. Enforcement discretion was a period fact, not a consumer right. If an editor needs dates, they come from FDA announcements, not from memory.

DIY videos are out of scope except as a refusal. Do not describe methods.

## The aisle’s temptation

Print "FMT-strength." Print "hospital microbiome." Print a spore claim that hopes you will hear Vowst. Print a disease row from AGA next to a cousin organism (draft 10). All of those are intended-use problems. Vowst is a spore product *and* a drug. *Weizmannia* in a tub is not Vowst (draft 18).

## House style

Name the products as teaching cases, once, with years. Point at the FDA page. Do not compare prices. Do not compare "naturalness." Do not say supplements are "the same idea, gentler." They are a different legal object. Draft 38 is the statute-level version of that sentence, without the brand names.

## What the labels actually are

Rebyota (November 2022) and Vowst (April 2023) have FDA labels with defined indications after antibiotic treatment of an episode of recurrent *C. difficile* — read the label, not a podcast. They are drugs. They are not dietary supplements. They are not available because a kit was red. CMC, potency, and a disease endpoint walked a path the 2016 LBP guidance had already named.

Other research programs — IBD, metabolic disease, autism — are other INDs and other disappointments. Mentioning them as "the future" on a supplement page borrows a drug's glamour without its file.
