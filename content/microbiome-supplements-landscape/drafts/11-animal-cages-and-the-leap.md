In 2006, Turnbaugh, Ley, Mahowald, and colleagues published a *Nature* paper (PMID 17183312) that the decade never got over: gut communities from obese mice, transferred into germ-free mice, moved energy harvest and adiposity in the recipients. The experiment is elegant. It is also a cage. A gnotobiotic mouse is a magnificent instrument. It is not a 35-year-old person in a grocery aisle. The leap from that instrument to a "lean microbiome" capsule is where this category keeps embarrassing itself (draft 34).

## What cages are good for

They can show that a community, or a fraction of one, is *sufficient* to move a trait in that animal under those conditions. They can rank mechanisms: bile acids, short-chain fatty acids, vagal signaling, immune tone. They can make a gene or a metabolite worth hunting in humans. Cryan and Dinan's 2012 review (*Nature Reviews Neuroscience*; PMID 22968153) collected a generation of behavior-and-microbiota findings, most of them preclinical. That collection is a research map. It is not a psychobiotic catalog (draft 33).

Germ-free and antibiotic-treated animals also show how strange a life without a typical community is. Development, immunity, and nutrition look different. "Different" is not "your customer is germ-free and this bottle will finish their development." Adult humans are not born into your isolator.

## What cages hide

Diet in a cage is chow. Stress is a protocol. Genetics are inbred. The recipient has no childhood viruses, no subway, no leftover doxycycline from a dentist. Human stool into a mouse is a chimera with its own politics. Human isolate into a mouse is still a mouse trial. Species-specific pathogens and species-specific immune readouts do not travel on a slide.

Effect sizes that look huge in a sterile animal often shrink or vanish in a colonized adult human. That is not a conspiracy. It is the difference between a simplified system and a crowded one. Hill 2014's "health benefit on the host" means, for a food or supplement file, a human host. Binda 2020 says at least one human study. The cage does not count.

## In vitro pages in the same pile

Caco-2 monolayers, simulated gastric fluid, and cytokine assays in a well plate are even earlier. They can kill a bad idea cheaply. They cannot prove a benefit. The 2011-style acid-bath graph that still travels with "survives the stomach" claims is draft 42. If the only "host" in the PDF is a cell line, you do not have a host benefit.

## How copy makes the leap

A sequence: mouse figure, human 16S association, product shot. The middle step is often a cross-sectional taxon difference that has not been shown to be causal in people. The product shot is often a cousin of the isolate that appeared in the cage. Three gaps. FTC 2022 does not grade on a curve because the mouse work was famous.

"Shown in preclinical models" is an honest phrase if you stop there. It is dishonest if the next sentence is a you-facing benefit. "May support" does not repair a missing human file. Fog is how these essays become the thing they are written against.

## A working rule

Use animal and in-vitro papers in this pack as *history of ideas* and as *warnings about translation*. Do not use them as silent substantiation. If a founder has only cages, the honest product language is: we are still in research. Or: we are selling a food with live cultures and no borrowed mouse story. Both are smaller than the deck they wanted. Smaller is how you stay out of letters.

When draft 33 talks about gut-brain, and draft 34 about metabolic papers, they will point back here. The leap is the same leap. The cage is not the enemy. The unannounced flight to the label is.

## Gnotobiotic vocabulary a copywriter should learn

Germ-free means no detectable microbes, not "clean eating." Specific-pathogen-free is a different, leakier state. Human-microbiota-associated mice are chimeras with their own politics: what engrafts is a filter, not a photograph of the donor. Antibiotic-treated mice are not germ-free; they are a wrecked community plus whatever the drugs did to the host. If a deck says "shown in germ-free mice" and the paper used a cocktail of antibiotics, the deck is already lying.

Litter effects, chow lot, and the vendor the mice came from are silent authors. Two labs can fail to replicate a behavior readout for reasons that have nothing to do with the isolate. That weather is why Binda 2020 wants a human study.

## The slide that should not exist

Left: mouse figure. Middle: human 16S association. Right: product shot of a cousin. Caption: "the science of the microbiome." Three gaps, one prefix. FTC 2022 does not grade on fame. Turnbaugh 2006 is famous. It is still a cage (draft 34). Cryan 2012 is famous. It is still mostly preclinical (draft 33).

"Shown in preclinical models" is allowed if the next sentence is not a you-facing benefit. "May support" does not repair the gap. Fog is how these essays become the thing they are written against.

## What cages still owe the pack

They owe us mechanisms worth hunting. They owe us humility about simplified systems. They owe us a reason to *run* a trial, not a reason to skip one. Draft 20's anaerobes are often cage-famous and hopper-absent. That is the leap in manufacturing clothes.

## A translation log an editor can keep

For every animal paper a deck wants to show, write four lines: species and facility; what was transferred or dosed; the readout; the human object the founder thinks this licenses. If line four is a SKU, the log has already failed unless a human trial of that SKU exists. If line two is a community and line four is a single isolate, the log has failed twice. Keep the log in the substantiation folder. It is cheaper than a letter.

Human-microbiota-associated mice filter a donor through a rodent. What grows is not a photograph. Citing that as "human stool proved X" is a category error. Antibiotic-treated mice are a wrecked community plus drug effects on the host. Do not call them germ-free on a slide. Draft 34 and draft 33 will keep sending readers here because the leap is the same leap.
