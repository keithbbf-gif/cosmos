Binda 2020 put "safe for the intended use" in the probiotic checklist next to characterization and a human study. Safety is not a vibe from yogurt history. It is a file about **this** isolate, **this** dose, **this** population, **this** matrix. A genome that shows a transferable resistance gene is a fact in that file. So is a toxin gene. So is a history of bacteremia in a catheterized host. So is a clean genome and a boring food-use story. The aisle prints the last one as if it were the class.

## QPS is a European shorthand

EFSA’s Qualified Presumption of Safety list is a rolling judgment about certain microorganisms for certain uses in Europe. It is useful. It is not a US GRAS conclusion (draft 40). It is not a license to skip strain-level work. Qualifications exist — resistance, for example. If you cite QPS, cite the year and the qualifications. Do not print "QPS certified" as a consumer seal. It is not NSF.

The US does not keep a cute parallel list for supplement microbes. History of use, NDI, GRAS, and the 2016 LBP guidance for the drug-shaped path are the adult vocabulary.

## Transferable resistance

A resistance gene that sits on a plasmid or a transposon is a different problem than a chromosomal gene that does not move. The scientific argument is about transfer to pathogens in a host or in a fermentation tank. The commercial argument is about whether anyone sequenced the isolate they actually pour. Species-level "lactobacilli are safe" does not answer a plasmid question. After Zheng 2020, species-level anything is already tired (draft 08).

Do not treat a resistome screen as a marketing badge ("we sequenced!"). Treat it as a gate. If the gate failed, the isolate is a different isolate or it is not a food isolate.

## Vulnerable hosts

Case reports of infection with organisms that are also sold as probiotics exist, especially when the host is not the healthy adult on the carton: central lines, severe immunosuppression, some preterm infants (draft 36). This pack will not inventory them. It will say: "safe" is intended-use language. A DTC site that says "safe for everyone" has left the file. A site that winks at hospital uses has left the box (draft 38).

Yeast fungemia (draft 17) and spore-former identity (draft 18) are the non-lactic versions of the same wall.

## Allergen and contaminant files

Growth media carry milk, soy, or egg stories. A "vegan probiotic" with a whey-grown isolate is a labeling problem. Heavy metals and objectionable organisms are finished-product COA rows (draft 43). Safety is not only the isolate’s genome. It is the hopper.

## House style

No "clinically proven safe." No hidden case-report bibliography meant to scare. No QPS halo. Ask for a genome, a resistome interpretation, a population statement, and a refusal to sell into NICU prestige. Draft 42 is the theater that tries to skip process: coatings and acid-bath graphs from a decade ago.

## Sequence as a gate, not a badge

A genome that shows a transferable resistance gene is a fact. So is a clean genome. "We sequenced" as a marketing line treats the gate as a flex. If the gate failed, the isolate is a different isolate or it is not a food isolate. Plasmid versus chromosomal, transferable versus not, are the scientific arguments. Species-level "lactobacilli are safe" does not answer a plasmid question (draft 08).

QPS is a European rolling shorthand with qualifications. Cite the year. Do not print "QPS certified" as a consumer seal. It is not NSF and not a US GRAS conclusion (draft 40).

## Media, allergens, and the hopper

Whey-grown "vegan" lines are a labeling problem. Heavy metals and objectionable organisms are finished-product rows (draft 43). Safety is not only the isolate's genome. It is the tank. Shared rooms with spore formers are draft 18's Monday swab.
