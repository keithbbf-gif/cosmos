**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). This essay describes a professional guideline. It is not guidance to use any product for *C. difficile*, IBD, pouchitis, IBS, or NEC.

In June 2020 the American Gastroenterological Association published a clinical practice guideline on probiotics for selected gastrointestinal disorders (Su et al., *Gastroenterology*; PMID 32531291). The technical review sat beside it (Preidis et al.; PMID 32562718). The panel used GRADE. They did not write "probiotics work for gut health." They wrote rows: this question, this direction of recommendation, this certainty, these organisms where they were willing to name them. A row about a disease in a hospital or clinic is a *practice* sentence for clinicians. It is not a structure/function sentence for a cart.

## What GRADE is doing here

GRADE forces a panel to say how sure it is and to separate the strength of a recommendation from the certainty of the evidence. Low certainty can still sit next to a conditional suggestion when the alternative is poor and the risk looks limited — or it can sit next to "only in a trial." Readers who want a yes/no banner hate this. That is a reader problem.

The public AGA page (dated 9 June 2020 on the society's site) is the version a clinician will quote. The journal papers are the version a methods person should quote. If they ever diverge in a later update, the dated page and the dated paper both need to be in the file. This draft is a 2020 snapshot. `[VERIFY]` before anyone treats a row as current practice.

## Rows that marketers should not touch

Prevention of *Clostridioides difficile* infection in people on antibiotics, treatment of *C. difficile*, pouchitis, Crohn's, ulcerative colitis, IBS, acute infectious gastroenteritis, and prevention of NEC in preterm infants are **disease or hospital** questions. Even where the panel was willing to discuss specific organisms or combinations, the indication is not a dietary-supplement intended use. Repeating a *C. diff* prevention sentence next to a DTC SKU is how a food-category product talks itself into 21 U.S.C. § 321(g). Drafts 36–38.

IBS is the wall of SKUs. The 2020 document was not kind to a general "use probiotics for IBS" reflex. If you need the exact wording, open the row. Do not paraphrase from memory onto a landing page. Later society updates or AGA re-reviews, if any, belong in a dated note — not in a silently refreshed banner.

## Strain specificity, then a hole in the table

The science the panel accepted is strain-specific. ISAPP said the same in 2014. The implementation problem, which ISAPP noted at the time, is that some recommended options were written at species or combination level without a deposit number on every line. That hole is how a marketer says "we have *L. acidophilus*, the guideline likes *L. acidophilus*." The operational reading for a brand that wants to stay out of letters is stricter than the weakest wording in a table: genus, species, strain, CFU through shelf, or you are not selling the evidence.

De Simone–type eight-strain combinations and *Saccharomyces boulardii* appear in these conversations as *named research objects*, not as permission to invent a cousin mix and keep the sentence (drafts 17 and 22).

## Certainty is the part the press release drops

A conditional suggestion with low or very low certainty is a whisper. Advertising turns whispers into "clinically recommended by gastroenterologists." AGA did not sit for that photo. Society guidelines are not endorsements of house brands. Trademark law and the society’s own rules sit on top of the science. Do not put the AGA logo on a tub.

## How this pack uses the document

As a teaching case in how a serious society talks when it is not selling. As a wall between hospital rows and aisle copy. As a reminder that "the science is mixed" is sometimes the official sentence, not a hedge you add for tone.

We do not use it as a shopping list. We do not use it as a protocol. We do not let a founder "align the catalog with AGA" by matching species names. Alignment, if the word means anything, would mean: you sell nothing for those disease questions, and you print strain IDs for anything you do sell with a structure/function file.

If a later editor needs one line for a hed: **GRADE rows are not aisle copy.** The rest of the essay is that line with citations.

## How a society page dies on a landing page

The path is boring and common. A founder reads a hed that says AGA and probiotics. A designer puts the logo-adjacent colors on a banner. A copywriter writes "recommended for gut health." The row was about a disease, a population, and a named combination. The banner is about a blend. Four compressions. Any one of them is enough to make the banner false.

ISAPP's contemporaneous complaint about species-level wording in some rows is a real implementation problem. The brand response that stays inside this pack is to get *stricter* than the weakest cell, not to park a cousin in the hole.

## Updates, snapshots, and the duty to date

This draft is a 2020 snapshot. Societies review. If a later AGA page changes a row, the dated paper and the dated page both go in the file. Silent refresh of a banner is how a 2020 hedge becomes a 2026 slogan. `[VERIFY]` is not decoration here. It is the difference between education and a stale claim.

Technical reviews (Preidis 2020) are where the certainty language lives. Press summaries are where it dies. Cite the review when you argue about certainty. Cite the public page when you argue about what a clinician saw.

## What this pack will never do with AGA

Never a shopping list. Never a logo. Never "align the catalog." Never a *C. diff* or NEC sentence next to a cart (drafts 36–38). The guideline is in the folder to teach GRADE and to build a wall. If an editor cuts this piece to a hed, keep the wall.
