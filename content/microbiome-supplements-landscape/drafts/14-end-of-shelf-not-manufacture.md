A hopper can be honest at 8 a.m. and a two-year label can still be a fiction. Live cells die. They die faster in heat, in water, in oxygen if they hate oxygen, and in a gummy that is basically a soft drink with a culture sprinkled in. Hill 2014's "adequate amounts" is the amount at administration. Binda 2020 says the product must provide, until the end of shelf life, a sufficient level of live microbes to deliver the studied benefit. Manufacture-day CFU is a process control. It is not the consumer fact.

## What Part 111 is asking, without romance

21 CFR Part 111 wants specifications, tests, and a production and process control system that makes the finished product meet those specifications. If you *claim* a potency through a dating period, you need data that the claim is true. "We plated the blend when it arrived from the supplier" is a receiving story. It is not a finished-product stability story. The supplier's COA is not your COA (draft 43).

Real-time data in the marketed matrix beat a hopeful accelerated chamber when the organism is moody. Accelerated studies can lie about spores versus vegetative cells, about glass versus blister, about desiccant that saturates. If you use accelerated, say so, and do not treat it as a two-year passport.

## The matrix is the other isolate

A freeze-dried capsule with low water activity, nitrogen flush, and a desiccant is one environment. A refrigerated liquid is another. A gummy is a third, usually worse. Enteric coatings add a fourth story (draft 42). You cannot borrow a stability file across those forms. You also cannot borrow across strains. GG's file is not a cousin's file (draft 16).

Houston in August is a stability study. So is a mailbox in Phoenix. So is a bathroom cabinet over a shower. "Store in a cool dry place" is not a substitute for a ship test if you sell through channels that will not be cool or dry. 3PLs have killed more live products than critics have.

## Overfill is a practice, not a miracle

Houses overfill so that the end-of-shelf number is still the labeled number. That can be legitimate process design. It becomes theater when the front of the pouch prints the overfill ("50 billion at manufacture!") and the end-of-shelf spec is a quiet 2 billion in a footnote the color of the pouch. Print the number the person is supposed to get. If you are proud of the overfill, keep it in the batch record.

Overfill also changes what a trial means. If the study administered 10¹⁰ and your end-of-shelf spec is 10⁹, you are not selling the study. You are selling a hope that dose-response is flat. Often nobody checked.

## Dating period is a claim

Every extra month is a promise about death rates. A 24-month date on a fragile anaerobe in a cheap bottle is a brave promise. Short dating and refrigeration are not moral failures. They are how some honest files look. "Room temperature stable" is a data sentence, not a brand personality.

Refrigerated products still need a chain. A sticker that says "keep cold" on a box that sat on a dock is a short story with a bad ending. Ask the 3PL what "cold" meant last July.

## How to read a number you did not make

If you are an editor or a founder looking at someone else's carton: find the time point. If it does not say "through end of shelf" or "at expiry," assume manufacture until proven otherwise. Then find the matrix. Then find whether anyone plated *finished* product, not input blend. Then stop talking about Hill's adequate amount until those lines exist.

The next essay is about the phrase that tries to skip this work: "clinically studied blend." Blends die in dating periods too, and they die unevenly — one isolate drops out, the label still lists eleven names. That is a stability problem wearing a marketing problem's clothes.

## Overfill stories that still fail

A house overfills to 5× so that expiry still meets the label. Legitimate if the expiry plate is real. Still a failure if the trial used the overfill number and the label uses the expiry number without saying so — or the reverse. Dose-response is often unmeasured. Flat hopes are not data.

A house prints both numbers, manufacture in 48-point and expiry in a footnote the color of the pouch. That is a claim about what a reasonable person will read. FTC reads the 48-point.

## Channels as stability authors

Amazon warehouses, bathroom steam, a car dashboard, a 3PL that treats "cool dry place" as a sticker. Ship tests and worst-case lanes belong in the file if those channels are real. Refrigerated SKUs need a chain, not a snowflake icon. Spores will survive more of this (draft 18). Anaerobes will not (draft 20). The organism is part of the dating period.

## What Part 111 will ask in English

What is the spec. What test shows the spec at the time you claim it. What happens when a lot fails. If the spec is a manufacture-day integer and the claim is a two-year banner, the file is arguing with itself. Draft 43 is the document where the argument becomes visible. Draft 42 is the coating that people hope will make dating periods optional.

## Dating period as a sentence you can read aloud

"This lot, in this bottle, held at least N live units of isolate X by method Y at the labeled expiry, after a ship test that included Z." If you cannot say that sentence, you do not have Hill's adequate amount. You have a hopper number and a hope. Refrigerated SKUs add a chain-of-cold clause. Gummies add a water-activity clause. Coatings add a recovery-from-coat clause (draft 42). Mixes add an each-line clause (draft 15).

Amazon in July is not a footnote. It is a lane. If that lane is real, the chamber that never saw 40 °C is not the file. Draft 43 is where the sentence becomes rows.
