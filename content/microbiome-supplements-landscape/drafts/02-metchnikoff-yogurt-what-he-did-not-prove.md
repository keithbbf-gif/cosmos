Élie Metchnikoff published *The Prolongation of Life: Optimistic Studies* in English in 1907, building on *Études sur la nature humaine* (1903). He was already a Nobel laureate (1908, with Ehrlich, for phagocytosis — the prize citation is immunology, not dairy). In the longevity essays he argued that the large intestine was a poorly designed sack in which putrefactive bacteria made "poisons," that this autointoxication hastened senility, and that sour milk fermented with the Bulgarian bacillus might crowd those organisms out. People still tell that story when they want probiotics to feel ancient and inevitable. The book does not contain a randomized trial, a deposit number, or a CFU-through-shelf table. It contains a theory and some village anecdotes.

## What he actually wrote

Metchnikoff had watched lactic fermentations and had heard — through Stamen Grigorov and others — that certain Bulgarian populations ate a great deal of yogurt and seemed to live long. He treated that as a clue, not as a Kaplan–Meier curve. He believed intestinal putrefaction was a central fact of aging. He believed lactic organisms could acidify the gut and make life harder for the putrefactive set. He recommended soured milk as a daily practice.

He did not identify a strain the way a 2020 ISAPP checklist would require. *Bacillus bulgaricus* of that period is not a drop-in synonym for today's *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus*, and even that modern name is a yogurt culture, not a capsule file. The organisms that survive gastric passage in a modern "Bulgarian" product are an empirical question, not an inheritance from 1907.

He also did not prove that long-lived villagers were long-lived *because* of yogurt. Diet, work, infection, records, and selection effects were not separated. Autointoxication itself is a period medical idea. Later gastroenterology did not keep it as a mechanism of aging. When a brand writes "as Metchnikoff discovered," the honest completion is: "he proposed."

## What the century did with the story

Dairy industries and, later, supplement marketers loved a Nobel name. Isaac Carasso's Danone origin story (Barcelona, 1919; later France) sits in that slipstream: yogurt as a hygienic food for children, sold in pharmacies, wearing scientific clothes. That is business history. It is not a strain-specific human endpoint.

The mid-century work that actually built the modern category looks different: named isolates (LGG in 1983; see draft 16), deposit numbers, antibiotic-associated diarrhea trials, and then the FAO/WHO attempt in 2001 to stop every fermented thing from inheriting the word. Hill 2014 is closer to that cleanup than to Metchnikoff's book. The book is the folklore the cleanup had to step around.

A second misuse is chronological flattening. "Humans have always eaten probiotics" is false twice. Humans have long eaten fermented foods. "Probiotic" as a defined scientific object is a late-twentieth-century word with a 2001/2014 definition. Kimchi is older than ISAPP. It does not automatically meet Hill's three words. Draft 27.

## Autointoxication, "toxins," and the modern echo

Metchnikoff's poisons were a theory of putrefactive metabolites. Today's copy talks about "toxins" and "resetting" after a weekend, which is the same shape of story with new nouns: lipopolysaccharide, "leaky gut," dysbiosis. Those nouns have research lives. They are not Metchnikoff's file, and they are not diagnoses you hand a reader with a capsule. Draft 06.

When a paper measures a named metabolite after a named strain, cite the paper. When a PDP says "helps remove toxins" because a 1907 chapter mentioned putrefaction, that is intended-use trouble wearing period costume.

Longevity is the other echo. Metchnikoff wanted to prolong life. NAD/NMN aisles want the same noun. A yogurt hypothesis from 1907 does not underwrite a 2025 aging claim. The FTC's 2022 health-products guidance does not have a grandfather clause for Nobel memoirs.

## What a history piece is allowed to give a modern file

It can give humility. The category began as a speculation by a great phagocytosis scientist who was not running double-blind CFU studies. It can give a warning about borrowed prestige. It can send the reader to the later documents that actually define the word (draft 01) and to the 2020 name split that makes old binomials treacherous (draft 08).

It cannot give a dose. It cannot give a strain. It cannot give "clinically proven gut health since 1907." It cannot turn village longevity into a structure/function claim.

If you keep one sentence from the book, keep the one that is a *practice*: he told people to drink soured milk. That is a food habit. The modern capsule that wants his portrait on the carton still needs live cells, an adequate count, and a benefit measured on the isolate inside — or it needs to take the portrait off.

## A note on reading the primary

English 1907 printings (Heinemann, Putnam) are easy to find in libraries and scans. Read the intestinal chapters, not a marketer's extract. Watch how often he reasons from comparative anatomy and from general bacteriology rather than from a table. That method was normal for his desk. It is not enough for a 21 CFR 101.93 substantiation file.

When later writers say he "discovered probiotics," correct them. He named a hope and a dairy practice. The word, the checklist, the deposit, and the human endpoint came later, and they still fail a lot of products that like his beard.

## How a 1907 chapter shows up on a 2026 slide

A typical deck compresses the book into three beats: Nobel, Bulgaria, yogurt. The Nobel is phagocytosis. The Bulgarian villages are an uncontrolled observation. The yogurt is a food practice. If the next slide is a CFU number, the deck has committed a category error. Put the 1907 title page in an appendix if you must have the beard. Do not put it under a structure/function sentence.

A second slide sometimes quotes "intestinal putrefaction" as if it were a modern metabolomics peak. Metchnikoff did not have those assays. When a modern paper measures a named metabolite after a named diet or isolate, cite the modern paper. When a founder wants the 1907 chapter to underwrite "helps remove toxins," the honest edit is deletion.

Translators and editions differ. If you quote, quote a dated English printing and a page. If you cannot find the page, do not invent a crisp sentence that sounds like a label. Period scientific prose is baggy. Baggy is a clue that you are not holding a methods section.

## What later dairy science actually added

Twentieth-century yogurt microbiology named the two classical cultures, worked out lactose fermentation, and built an industry that could ship a cup that still tasted like itself. That is food technology. It is closer to draft 27 than to Hill 2014. When a cup later receives a studied isolate at a studied dose, the isolate can have a file. The 1907 book is still not that file.

Isaac Carasso's pharmacy yogurt is a distribution story: parents, children, a white coat at the counter. White coats at counters are how foods borrow clinical furniture. DSHEA later tried to police the furniture in the United States. A 1919 shop does not grandfather a 2026 disease noun.

Then open Hill 2014 and write down the three words. The distance between the sheets is the point of this essay. Draft 01 is the far sheet.
