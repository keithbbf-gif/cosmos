**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). A scientific definition is not an FDA product category and not a license to copy a hospital paper onto a bottle.

In October 2013, ISAPP sat a panel in a room and asked whether the FAO/WHO sentence from 2001 still earned its keep. The published answer (Hill et al., *Nature Reviews Gastroenterology & Hepatology*, 2014; PMID 24912386) kept the sentence and tightened the grammar: live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host. Three words do the work. Everything else on a tub is decoration until those three are true of *this* isolate, in *this* matrix, at *this* count, for a benefit someone actually measured.

## The 2001 sentence, and the 2002 working rules

The Córdoba consultation (1–4 October 2001) rewrote an older, sloppier use of "probiotic." The 2002 London, Ontario working group then wrote evaluation guidelines: identify the strain, show safety for the intended use, show a human benefit, keep the cells alive at a useful count. Those documents are not statutes. They are the texts serious people still argue from when a marketer treats "probiotic" as a color.

Hill 2014 did not invent a new religion. It said the FAO/WHO wording still fit, then named the abuses: products with no live cells at the end of shelf, products with no human data, products that borrow a neighbor's trial because the species name matches. The panel also said fermented foods with undefined cultures are not automatically probiotics, and that fecal mixes used as transplants sit in a different scientific and regulatory room. That last point matters more in 2026 than it did in 2014; see drafts 37 and 38.

Binda et al., 2020 (*Frontiers in Microbiology*; PMID 32793153) translated the definition into a checklist a food or supplement file can fail out loud: characterization to strain, valid nomenclature, a strain designation, safety for intended use, a culture-collection deposit, at least one human study, and enough live cells through shelf to deliver the studied benefit. If you cannot tick those, you can still sell a powder. You just do not get the word for free.

## Live

Live means viable cells at the moment of use, not a beautiful plate count on the day the hopper was filled. Heat-killed, tyndallized, and many "postbiotic" preparations can be interesting research objects. They are not probiotics under Hill 2014, because the first word is gone. Draft 23 is the longer version of that sentence.

A genome copy is not a cell. qPCR on a dead soup will still light up. Flow-cytometry "active fluorescent units" (ISO 19344) are not colony-forming units. Plate counts miss injured cells and argue with anaerobic technique. None of those methods is a moral ranking. Each answers a different question. If the trial that is being borrowed used CFU of a live isolate, a dead-cell number is a different product. Draft 13.

Yeast are alive too. *Saccharomyces boulardii* is not a bacterium. A CMO SOP written only for lactic acid bacteria will lie about a yeast without anyone intending to lie. Draft 17.

## Adequate

Adequate is the dose that appeared in the human study you are pointing at, delivered in a matrix that does not secretly kill the isolate. "50 billion" printed beside a blend of fifteen untyped lines is not adequate. It is a large integer.

End of shelf is the honest denominator. Manufacture-day CFU is a process control, not a consumer fact. Water activity, heat in a Houston warehouse, a gummy's water, and a two-year dating period are how "adequate" dies in the wild. Draft 14.

If the trial used 10¹⁰ CFU of one strain for eight weeks, a 2×10⁹ CFU untyped "lactobacillus blend" taken "as needed" is not a smaller version of the same object. It is a different object. Dose-response data in this category are often thin. When they are missing, the honest sentence is that they are missing — not that more CFU is obviously better.

## Benefit

Benefit is a named endpoint in a host, usually a human, measured on the isolate you sell. "Gut health" is not an endpoint. Neither is "supports the microbiome," as if that community were one organ with a reference range.

Hill 2014 allowed that some well-studied species might share a core benefit (for example, aiding lactose digestion in yogurt cultures), but the panel did not hand every *Lactobacillus* a participation trophy. Strain specificity is the default. After Zheng et al. 2020 split the old genus, even the word on the old paper may no longer match the word on the label. Draft 08.

A mouse stool transfer that changed weight is not a human benefit. A 16S shift after a capsule is not a benefit unless you pre-specified why that shift matters to the person. A guideline row about a disease in a hospital is a *research and practice* document. It is not a structure/function sentence you can park next to an add-to-cart button. Drafts 10 and 38.

## What the definition refuses

It refuses the shotgun mix that has never been in a trial as a mix. It refuses the species-only line that borrows GG's file because both used to say *Lactobacillus*. It refuses the stool kit that "prescribes" a SKU from an amplicon table. It refuses the claim that more strains are wiser. It refuses "probiotic" as a synonym for "fermented" or "from the soil" or "found in the gut of a lean person."

It also refuses a common legal confusion. The Hill sentence is a scientific definition. FDA does not maintain a monograph called Probiotic. In the United States a live microbe in a capsule is usually offered as a dietary supplement (food category, DSHEA) or, if intended to treat disease, as a live biotherapeutic product — a drug. The word on the science paper does not pick the box. Intended use does. Draft 38.

## A reading habit, not a slogan

When a paper, a sales deck, or a draft label says probiotic, ask three questions in order. Were the cells alive at the dose the person took? Was that dose the one in the study, in a matrix that keeps them alive through the dating period? Was the benefit a named endpoint on that strain, in humans, written without turning a disease paper into aisle copy?

If any answer is no, the honest vocabulary gets smaller: live cultures, a named isolate, a fermented food, a research organism, a heat-killed preparation, a drug candidate. Those are not insults. They are smaller claims. Smaller claims are how this file stays educational.

The rest of the pack is the same habit applied to methods, names, trials, organisms, foods, tests, hospital rooms, and COAs. This piece is only the door. The three words still do the work.
