**Disclaimer.** Educational only. Not medical advice. Dietary supplements are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease (DSHEA; 21 U.S.C. § 321(ff); 21 CFR 101.93). "Clinically studied" is advertising language until a file makes it a fact about *this* product.

The phrase is doing three jobs at once. It wants the prestige of a trial. It wants the flexibility of a house formula that can change when a supplier ships something else. It wants to avoid naming a strain, a dose, or a paper. Hill 2014's default — strain specificity, adequate amount, a host benefit — does not offer a fourth job called "near enough." FTC's 2022 guidance asks what a reasonable consumer takes away. A reasonable consumer takes away: this bottle was in a clinic. Usually it was not.

## The neighbor's isolate

A paper used *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* GG at a named CFU in a capsule. The blend uses "L. rhamnosus" at an unstated fraction of a 15-strain pile. After Zheng 2020, the old genus word is even less of a bridge (draft 08). GG's file is GG's file (draft 16). Species-level inheritance is the oldest trick in the aisle.

Sometimes the neighbor is honest in the methods and the blend is honest on the COA, and they are still different objects. Honesty does not merge them.

## The neighbor's mix

A trial used an eight-strain combination at set ratios. The house "based on" that combination swapped two strains, changed the ratios, and changed the CMO. The De Simone naming fight exists because this object is legally and scientifically brittle (draft 22). If you did not test the mix, you do not have mix evidence. Additivity is a hope. Antagonism in the hopper is a known hobby of live products.

## The neighbor's dose and matrix

Yogurt at 10¹⁰ is not a gummy at 10⁹. Eight weeks is not "as needed." A refrigerated vial is not a mailbox. Drafts 13 and 14 are the counting and dating versions. "Clinically studied" that quietly changes unit, time point, or form is a new intervention wearing an old adjective.

## The poster, the open label, and the n=12

"Clinically studied" sometimes means an uncontrolled series in a clinic that also sells the bottle. Sometimes it means a conference poster. Sometimes it means a trial that was registered, run, and never published because the primary endpoint missed. Draft 12. An adjective that covers those cases without distinction is not information. It is fog.

If there is a paper, print the citation, the isolate, the dose, the n, the duration, and the endpoint. That sentence is longer than the adjective. It also cannot be used when the file is empty.

## Blends and the disappearing isolate

Eleven names on the label, ten on the late-shelf plate. One organism was a tourist. The blend is now a different blend. If the "study" was on eleven, the dating-period product is unstudied twice. QC on blends is not eleven times a single-strain QC. It is a method problem: can you enumerate each isolate in the presence of the others? If you cannot, your spec is a story.

## What the phrase can mean, on a good day

It can mean: this finished product, this isolate list, this dose through shelf, was the intervention in a registered human trial with a named endpoint, and here is the ID. That is a rare, boring, excellent sentence. It does not need the adjective. The citation does the work.

21 CFR 101.93 still wants substantiation, notification, and the disclaimer for a structure/function claim. "Clinically studied" is often a claim about the *product's* relationship to science. That is an FTC problem even before you pick a structure/function verb.

## A house style for this pack

We do not use the phrase except to teach it. We name papers. We say when a SKU is not the intervention. We treat a blend as a new object until a trial of the blend exists. Wave 3 will talk about organisms people actually sell. Each of those essays will refuse to let a famous cousin do the talking.

If an editor needs a hed: **near enough is not a strain.** The rest is examples.

## A taxonomy of the adjective

1. Same isolate, same dose, same matrix, registered trial — you do not need the adjective; you have a citation.
2. Same isolate, different dose or form — you have a cousin.
3. Same species, different strain — you have a neighborhood (draft 08).
4. A mix "based on" a studied mix — you have a lookalike (draft 22).
5. A poster, an open label, an n=12 in a clinic that sells the bottle — you have weather (draft 12).
6. A trial that missed and stayed in a drawer — you have a graveyard.

The aisle uses one phrase for all six. A reasonable consumer hears number 1. FTC 2022 cares about that hearing.

## Change control as the adult practice

When a supplier ships a different line, the blend is new. When a ratio moves, the blend is new. When a CMO's freeze-dry curve moves, the blend may be new even if the names match. Trigger a new thought: new plates, and a new decision about whether the citation still attaches. If that sounds expensive, the blend was expensive. Eleven names are not a discount on evidence.

## What this pack will print instead

Isolate, dose through shelf, matrix, paper, n, duration, endpoint. Or we will say the SKU is not the intervention. We will not use "clinically studied blend" except to teach it. Wave 3's organism essays refuse famous cousins for the same reason.

## A red-pen exercise

Take any carton that says the phrase. Draw a line from each isolate to a paper. Draw a line from the dose on the carton (expiry, not manufacture) to the dose in the paper. Draw a line from the matrix to the matrix. Draw a line from the mix, if any, to a mix trial. Count the lines that actually land. If the count is zero, the adjective is about someone else's bottle. If the count is one isolate in a pile of eleven, the pile is still unstudied.

Do this on a printout, not in your head. Heads forgive. Red pens do not. FTC's reasonable consumer is a red pen.
