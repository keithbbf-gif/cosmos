A brown fig can be a week away. A green fig can be dessert. If you harvest by the color you saw in a listing photo, you will eat plywood and leave the good ones for the birds.

Celeste goes violet-bronze and people still pick it like an apple the day it blushes. A honey fig can stay a pale green-gold and be dripping. Dark berry types look “ready” as soon as they lose chlorophyll, which is sometimes ten days before the neck gives. Color is a clue. It is not the sentence.

I want give. I want the neck to look like it got tired of holding its head up. I want the fruit to come with a twist, not a tear. If I have to saw it off the branch with my thumbnail, it is not a harvest. It is a kidnapping.

PapaFig’s line on the intro page is the one I will not soften: the only way to know a good fig is a fully ripe fruit of an excellent variety. Grocery color is a truck color. Black Mission in a clamshell is often purple enough to photograph and firm enough to survive a warehouse. That fruit can finish softening in a cold room. It will not become the fig you ate standing at the tree.

Our 8a light lies in the afternoon. West sun makes a pale fig look washed and a dark fig look black. Morning light is how I decide. I pick with my fingers, not with my memory of a review photo. We have scored fruit in August and in October. The dates are on the reviews. The color on those plates is the color of *that* ripe fruit, not a paint chip for every week of the season.

Yellow jackets will lecture you. If they have found the ostiole, you are late or right on time. If they are ignoring a dark fig, it may still be a rock in a costume. Do not let a wasp be your only teacher, but do not ignore a wasp convention.

Some names show sugar cracks when they are honest. Some names never crack and still eat like jam. Cracks are not a requirement. Cracks in a rain week are also how souring starts on an open eye. I do not wait for a fig to split so I can be sure. I wait for the neck and the give.

Green-skinned ripe fruit is how you hide from birds a little longer. PapaFig already said birds go for dark first. A ripe green fig is not a trick you play on yourself. It still has to give. “It stays green” is not permission to pick hard.

I have sent people away from the shop with a hard brown fig because they asked for “one that will last until Saturday.” Saturday fruit is a different job. That is the canning-firm habit, or a bird-save, or a fool’s errand. I will write those later. This piece is about the harvest that is actually the harvest: color in the background, neck and give in the foreground.

If you need a drill: look, then touch, then twist a candidate, then taste one from that plant that day. One. Not a bowl. If the one is empty in the middle or still milky-white and bland, the color fooled you. Leave the rest. Come back tomorrow. 8a August will not save your Saturday plans. It will save the fruit if you let it.

A phone photo flattens shine. The dull, almost matte skin on a ripe fig is easy to miss in a JPEG. In the hand it looks like the fruit stopped trying to be pretty and started being food. I trust that more than a high-gloss marble that still has the posture of a bud.

Color is for the review plate after the neck has spoken. Not before.
