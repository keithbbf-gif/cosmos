Charts lie with a smile. “Celeste: July. Brown Turkey: August–October. Alma: late.” Then you plant a tag and the tag was a crowd, and your 8a clay is not their 8b sand, and the chart still wants to be your religion.

I will give you tendencies, not a calendar. In this yard, the small tight-eye sugar figs — the Celeste kind of plant — come first if the wood lived. The bronze turkey pile comes next and hangs around. Late berry names we have actually plated can sit into October. We wrote October 1, 2024 on a Red Sicilian and an LSU Jack Lily. We wrote August 15, 2024 on another dark berry. Those are plates, not a law for your fence line.

GDDs eat charts. PapaFig already said it: a wonderful fig with not enough heat is a green ornament. 8a has heat. What we do not always have is an early spring, or a plant that was not frozen to the crown, or a pot that did not sulk through June because somebody missed water on concrete. A “July fig” on a plant that restarted in April is a September fig. The chart does not know that.

Name confusion is not a footnote. UAEX says don’t be surprised to see the same plant grown with several names. Brown Turkey and Texas Everbearing get stacked in Arkansas writing. In the forums they are a knife fight. I will not settle the knife fight in a harvest draft. I will tell you to learn *your* plant’s first ripe week and write it on the pot with a paint pen. Next year that note is worth more than a blog table.

A pot on the driveway and an in-ground of the “same” name can be ten days apart. That is not a sport. That is root volume and heat. If you mix those dates into one chart, you will pick the in-ground hard because the pot already went.

Late names are a gamble we take on purpose. They are not a failure when the first cold night in late October catches green fruit. They are a gamble. Eat what finished. Kitchen-firm the ones that are close. Leave the marbles to the compost. Do not stand in the frost and feel betrayed by a chart that said “October.”

I dislike “everbearing” on a label the way I dislike “seedless” on a grocery tomato. Some plants ripen a long parade on new wood. That parade still has a start and a weather limit. It is not a faucet.

If you want a longer season, plant two honest windows, not one myth. An early tight-eye and a later bronze, or an early pot and a later in-ground. Do not plant seven late berries because the reviews were romantic and then wonder why the first week of November is a funeral.

Zone 7 charts start later and end earlier. Zone 9 charts start earlier and get cocky. When you read either, translate. Our first real bowls are August. Our last interesting plates can be October. The middle is the farm.

I keep a cheap notebook in the shop with first-ripe dates, not because I am building a cultivar trial, but because next June I will believe a chart again if I do not have last year’s Tuesday written down. The notebook has holes, clay fingerprints, and three names I later crossed out because the fruit was not the tag. That crossing-out is the only honest science I run.

I will not publish a week-by-week grid for 40 names. That grid would be wrong by the second rain. Walk the plant. Neck. Eye. Give. Write the week you actually ate one. That is the only chart I trust, and it only works for that plant, in that hole, in that year.
