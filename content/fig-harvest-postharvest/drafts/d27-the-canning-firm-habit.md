Janet Carson said if you are preserving, pick several days before fully ripe so they hold together and you take less souring and splitting. That is an Arkansas kitchen sentence. It is not a dessert sentence.

I will not write a jam recipe here. That is another set. I will write the harvest fork in the road.

Dessert: neck, give, twist, mouth. Kitchen: color of the name, still a spine in the fruit, twist or a clean snip, into a tray that is going to a board the same day or the next morning. Those trays do not share a story. If you mix them, you will overcook the dessert figs into paste and you will under-sugar the hard ones and then blame the variety.

A canning-firm fig is still not a marble. Several days before fully ripe is not two weeks. You should see the color change. You should feel a little give, not a rock. If you need a knife to argue with the shoulder, you are early even for a pot.

I pick kitchen fruit on a day I can stand at the stove, or I freeze the glut as-is and cook later. I do not pick a five-gallon kitchen heap on a Wednesday because I am ambitious and then go to town. Ambition plus a meeting is a sour smell.

Latex is worse on a firmer pick. Gloves. Stem on if it will come. Cut if it will not, and accept the sap.

This fruit does not get a ripening plate. It gets a job. If you leave canning-firm fruit on the counter “to get sweeter,” you have wandered back into the banana church. Either cook it or admit you picked dessert too early and eat the ones that made it.

Closed-eye names still make the best kitchen fruit in a wet week because they arrive without a vinegar subplot. Open-eye names I process faster.

I will take a neighbor a bowl of dessert figs. I will not take them a lecture about firm picks unless they asked for jars.

Zone 7 people preserve because the season is short. We preserve because August is a glut. Same habit, different reason. Do not act like jars are a failure of fresh eating. Jars are how August does not become a dare.

If you never cook, you do not need this pick. You need the neck and a freezer bag. That is also a job. It is a cleaner one.

If I am making a small pot of jam, I still want some dessert figs in the mix or the jar tastes like a good idea and not like August. Firm for structure, ripe for flavor. That is a kitchen ratio, not a harvest religion. The harvest part is keeping the two kinds from lying in the same unlabeled bowl.

I will not tell you pint counts. I will tell you I have run out of lids in a week I should have frozen. Lids are a Tuesday problem. The tree does not care.

A pressure canner conversation is not this set. A pot on a stove is enough of a kitchen. If you want USDA jar times, you want a different page and a current book. I will not invent a time from memory and kill a neighbor.

I do not pick kitchen-firm because I am afraid of ripe fruit. I pick it because I have a pot and a clock. Fear is how you never taste the farm. A clock is how you keep the farm.

Two trays on the table. Two labels if you have a helper. “Eat” and “Cook.” I have mixed them. I have regretted the mix more than I have regretted a small tray.
