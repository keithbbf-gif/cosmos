If you have to yank, you are early or you are clumsy. I will train the clumsy. I will not train the early.

A ripe fig wants to leave. You lift, you twist, the stem comes with the fruit. Janet Carson said leave the stem attached. She is right. The stem is a little lid over a wound. A torn shoulder is a wet hole. Latex weeps. The tray next to it gets sticky. Tomorrow morning that fig is a bruise with a name.

Two hands when the fruit is large or the wood is brittle. One hand behind the fig so the neck does not peel the shoulder. One hand on the fruit. A quarter turn. If it fights, stop. Look at the neck again. Feel the give. If you are wrong, leave it. There will be another morning. There will not be another unbruised fig if you tear this one off to prove a point.

I do not “milk” a fig down the cane like I am stripping beans. Each fruit is a decision. The ones behind it are still working.

Thumbnails as knives are how you open a fig you should have left. If you cut, you have already admitted it was not ready, or you are doing the kitchen-firm pick on purpose. Cutting a ripe fig because you are impatient is how latex gets on the next six fruits.

Latex is the white sap. It is not milk. It will find a scratch on your wrist and remind you all afternoon. I will write that piece. Here the point is: a clean twist reduces the sap event. A rip sprays.

Pots at waist height are a gift. In-ground stools we keep low on purpose so you are not on a ladder with a bowl. If you trained a tree you cannot reach, that is a winter problem you planted. Do not stand on a five-gallon bucket in August clay. I have watched that movie.

The motion is small. People who pick apples want to pull down. Down is how you snap a young cane and take three unripe figs with the one you wanted. Twist in place. Let the neck tell you.

If the stem stays on the plant and the fruit comes off naked, you were late or the neck was already rotting. Eat that one standing up. Do not stack it.

I put the fig in the tray like an egg. Not a baseball toss. Not a pile. Stem orientation I care about later. Not tearing I care about now.

Kids yank. Show them once on a fruit you can afford. If they yank again, they get the job of carrying the tray in the shade, not the job of the necks.

A Saturday guest will pick with enthusiasm. Give them a pot. Do not give them the in-ground Celeste you have been watching for four mornings.

I have picked in rain. I do not like it. Wet fruit slips. Necks tear. The tray becomes a puddle. If I must, I pick fewer, I dry the tray, I get out of the weather. Rain fruit that was already ready still eats. Rain fruit you tore because you could not feel anything eats like a bandage.

A cane that is still soft at the tip will peel if you use it as a handle. Hold the older wood. I have stripped a shoulder and a leaf at once because I grabbed the wrong inch. The fig was ready. My hand was not.

The twist is not a trick. It is the plant’s own release. When you fight the plant, you are not harvesting. You are dismantling. Dismantling is for winter wood, not for August food.
