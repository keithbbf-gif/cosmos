A fig on the ground is not automatically trash and it is not automatically a gift. It is a decision you make with your nose and your fingers, not with a proverb.

If it fell because the neck was done and the clay is dry and it has been there ten minutes, I will eat it or I will put it in the now tray. If it fell into a puddle, or it has a hole, or it smells like a cider experiment, it is not coming in the house to be washed into honesty. Water does not unsour a fig.

Ants will vote. A clean drop with a few ants is still food if you are standing there. A drop that is a highway is compost.

I do not make a “ground fruit” bowl for guests. I make a ground fruit decision for me, at the plant, like a grown-up. Pride is how you serve grit.

Drought drop is a different object. Those are often still hard. They did not ripen on the way down. They fled. I do not ripen them on the windowsill. I do not. Jack will write why. I will say I have tried, and I have eaten disappointment with dust on it.

Storm drop can be almost-ripe fruit the wind stole. Those I will treat as bird-save: sort, counter if they already had color and give, kitchen if they are firm, compost if they are green rocks.

Chickens get the ones I will not argue with. Hens already interrupt this website. They can finish the argument.

I walk the ground before I walk the canopy so I am not stepping on the decision. Clay hides a dark fig. I have dyed a shoe.

Kids will grab every drop like it is treasure. Treasure gets a rinse at the hose and a grown-up look. If I say no, it is no. A sour fig is a good way to teach a child that figs can be awful, which I do not need help with.

A drop under a wasp plant stays on the ground. I am not fighting a shop for a dirty fig.

If the plant is dropping a lot of small hard fruit, that is water, or a young plant, or a pot that cooked. Harvest technique will not fix it. The hose might.

I do not leave drops to “draw the pests away from the tree.” They draw pests to the tree. Clean up is harvest. A bucket for the hole and a tray for the table. Two vessels. One walk.

Zone 9 people with clean dry dirt have a different ground story. Our ground is clay, ticks, and a smell after rain. Be choosier than a Mediterranean postcard.

I do not make salsa from a mystery drop I did not see fall. That is how you eat a fig that fermented in a hoof print. If I did not see it leave the neck, it has to pass a harder test: smell, skin, a cut. No cut, no kitchen. The hole is still open.

Dogs will eat a drop and then look at you like they invented dessert. I still sort. A dog is not a health department. If the drop was sour, the dog can have the lesson. I do not serve the lesson to people.

I carry a cheap kitchen fork in the row for drops I do not want to glove. Flip, look, smell, decide. The fork lives with the sap rag, not in the silverware. Cross-contamination is still a fancy word for a bad idea.

The fig that dropped already made a decision. Yours is the second one. Make it fast. The third decision belongs to the yellowjackets.
