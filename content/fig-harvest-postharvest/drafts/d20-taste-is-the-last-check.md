If you have not put one in your mouth, you are not harvesting. You are collecting props.

Neck, give, twist, then taste *one* from that plant that morning. If it is empty, leave the rest. If it is jam, continue. If it is “fine,” decide whether you are eating or processing. Fine is often kitchen. Jam is a bowl.

I do not taste ten. I am not building a flight. I am checking whether this plant, this week, this water, is telling the truth. A plant can lie after a drought. A name can lie if it is not the name. Your mouth is cheaper than a lab.

PapaFig already named the families: berry, honey, sugar, melon, peachy. Use those words after you have juice on your hand, not after you read a tag. A “berry fig” that tastes like wet cardboard is either early or a stranger.

We score fruit on FigRoots with numbers because we are running reviews. Harvest is not a review session unless you scheduled one. Do not stand in the row assigning 4.5 to a fig you are about to put in a bucket. Eat. Then pick.

A bad taste is information. Sour is discard. Bland-and-firm is wait. Bland-and-soft can be water, shade, or a name that is ordinary on a bad week. One bad fig is not a death sentence for the variety. A week of bad figs is a hose question or a tag question.

I spit sour. I do not swallow to be polite to a plant. Politeness is how you teach yourself that figs are “an acquired taste,” which is a sentence written by people who have not had a ripe one.

Kids get the good one first. If their first fig is a grocery clamshell or a hard backyard bronze, you may have lost them until they are twenty. Give them the Celeste that actually gave. Then let them see the neck so they know why.

Taste at the plant, not after the fruit sat in a hot car. A hot car will make a good fig taste like a fight.

I will not write flavor like a wine list. I will write: this one is honey and the seeds are polite; this one is berry and the skin is chewy; this one is nothing and I walked away. Enough.

If you are picking for someone else — a neighbor, a sale you should not be running out of a shop — taste still happens. You do not donate your doubt.

Zone 7 people will eat a fig that would not pass here because they are glad it ripened at all. I understand glad. I still want you to know the difference between glad and good. Zone 9 people will call our good figs ordinary. Let them. Their mouths have a longer menu.

A guest who says “I don’t like figs” has usually had a grocery rock or a sour backyard bronze. Give them one you already tasted. If they still do not like it, that is a person, not a project. Do not strip a plant to convert them.

I keep a knife in the row only for the fig I am unsure about, and I wipe it. A shared knife through a sour and then a keeper is how you invent a mystery. One cut, one decision, rinse if you have to.

If my mouth is tired from a long pick, I wait before I judge a new name. Fatigue makes everything “fine.” Fine is how a good berry gets a bad reputation on a hot afternoon.

The last check is not a caption. It is a swallow or a spit. Then the tray earns its keep.
