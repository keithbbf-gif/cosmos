Breba is a word that makes people buy a name. It is not a promise in South Arkansas.

The fruit that forms on last year’s wood, sits through winter, and tries to finish in late spring or early summer — that is breba. In a climate where the canes stay brown and alive to the tips, it is a real first crop. Janet Carson’s Arkansas version is blunter: those figs rarely persist and thrive over winter here. The crop we harvest is the one on current wood.

I still look. I am not a monk. A mild winter, a wall, a pot we rolled into the shop — you can get a handful in June. A handful is a bonus. A handful is not a plan for jam, and it is not a reason to tell a new grower their tree “failed” in June because the internet showed a loaded breba on a Brown Turkey in a comment.

The wood has to live. If January took the canes to the ground, there is no last-year wood with a swell. There is a stool and a flush. That flush will try to be the main crop. It will be later. It will not be breba no matter how badly you want the word.

Some names make breba like it is a job. Some names barely bother. Commerce will sell you both as “everbearing.” Everbearing in a catalog is often just “we hope you get two stories.” In 8a, the second story is the one that pays rent.

Jack will tell you not to prune last year’s fruiting wood off a name you are counting on for breba. Believe him, and then remember Celeste: a hard late-winter prune on Celeste is how you limit *this* year’s fruit, because Celeste is slow to forgive. The turkey pile will throw crop on new growth even after you butchered it. That is not a moral. That is how the plant is.

I have eaten June fruit off a pot that spent the cold months on the shop floor. It was good. It was also four figs and a story. The same plant in August filled a tray. If you only have room for one story in your head, keep August.

Zone 9 advice says protect the breba wood like it is the farm. Zone 7 advice says you will not see breba unless you wrap. We sit in the middle and get our feelings hurt by both. I wrap a young in-ground if the forecast is ugly. I do not wrap a mature stool and then expect a June market. I expect the plant to be alive in April.

A breba fig is often larger, a little blander, a little more likely to split if June decides to be a monsoon. People compare it to the main crop of the same name and decide the variety “isn’t that good.” They tasted the bonus and graded the plant. Wait for the crop that grew in this year’s heat.

I will pick a ripe breba the same way I pick a main-crop fig. Neck, give, twist, stem on. I will not pick a hard June marble because I am excited the word came true. Excitement is how you eat cardboard and tell the forum the variety is overrated.

If you are choosing names because you “want breba,” say it out loud and then look at your winter. If your 8a yard is a frost pocket and the canes die most years, buy a name that fruits on new wood and get over the romance. If you have a south wall and a pot you can walk inside, you can play for June. Play is the right verb.

We already asked the question on FigRoots: will you try to get breba? It is a question, not a feature list. The answer in this yard, most years, is: I will take it if the wood lived, and I will not rearrange the summer around it.

Write “bonus” on the tape if you have to. The main crop does not need a nickname. It needs August and a bowl.
