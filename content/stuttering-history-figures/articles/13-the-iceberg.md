This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Joseph G. Sheehan drew an iceberg. The year people remember is the 1953 approach-avoidance paper, and then the 1970 book. The drawing is older than most of the clinicians who still photocopy it. Above the water: repetitions, prolongations, blocks, the face that moves. Below: fear, shame, the word you did not say, the job you did not apply for, the name you changed in the coffee line. The drawing is a teaching object. It is not a validated test. It is not a protocol. It is a way to stop a student from thinking the disorder is only the tip.

Covert stuttering — a speaker who passes for fluent by avoiding, substituting, and staying out of the sentence — is the iceberg’s private case. The syllable count can look almost clean. The life does not. Later writers (some of them people who stutter, writing outside the academy) insisted that a clinic which only rewards a clean count can train a better hiders.

## What the decades thought they were solving

Fear. Sheehan had been a chemist who could not keep a job, then a clinical psychologist at UCLA who ran a Psychology Speech Clinic from 1949 until his death in 1983. Knight Dunlap’s old idea of negative practice sits in the background: do the thing on purpose and it loses its charge. Voluntary stuttering, already in Bryngelson’s Minnesota, becomes in Sheehan’s room a social act. Look at the listener. Stay. Say the name.

Van Riper’s desensitization is a cousin. Williams’s “what are you doing?” is a cousin. The iceberg is the picture that escaped the family and entered every intro slide.

## Two or three objects

The 1953 paper: stuttering as approach-avoidance conflict. Dollard and Miller’s psychology, applied to a mouth.

The iceberg figure as it appears in later textbooks. If you need the figure in WordPress, redraw it and credit Sheehan’s idea. Do not scan a still-copyrighted textbook page.

Vivian Sheehan’s continuation of the clinic after 1983, first at UCLA, later in Santa Monica. The iceberg had a second author in the room even when the spine said Joseph.

## Who was left out

Speakers whose shame is not only about stuttering — race, gender, a second language, a school that already hated their voice. An iceberg that is only about fluency can hide those weights. Children who do not yet have a vocabulary for shame and are asked to draw waterlines anyway. Fluent siblings used as a moral contrast.

Bloodstein’s continuity hypothesis — that stuttering grows out of ordinary disfluency — can be read as a friend of the iceberg or as a rival. Friend: everyone bumps; some people learn to fear the bump. Rival: if the speech behaviors are already special at onset (Yairi’s later claim), the underwater mass is not the first cause. Let the rivalry stand. Do not pick a winner for a brand.

## Residue

A 2026 evaluation that asks what the person does *instead* of stuttering — the avoided sounds, the skipped meetings — is living in Sheehan’s drawing. So is every social-media post that says “my stutter is the least of it.” The residue to refuse is a clinic that uses the iceberg as décor and then grades only the tip.

Self-help groups (next essay) took the underwater half public. That was not Sheehan’s invention. It was what the drawing made sayable in a church basement.
