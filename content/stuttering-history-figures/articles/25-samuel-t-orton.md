This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Samuel Torrey Orton is in the reading clinic first. Strephosymbolia, the Iowa years, Anna Gillingham’s later teaching methods, a society that still bears his name. He is in this pack because Lee Edward Travis and others borrowed a neurological imagination — two hemispheres, one that should lead — and offered it to stammering. Orton was not a speech-language pathologist. Do not make him one.

He was born in 1879 and died in 1948. He trained as a physician, worked in pathology and neurology, and spent a decisive stretch at Iowa in the 1920s, overlapping the campus that would become a stuttering factory. The overlap is geography and a shared wish for a brain story. It is not a joint practice.

## The loan

Mixed dominance as an explanation for a childhood disorder is an elegant diagram. For reading, it organized a generation of remediation. For stuttering, it organized handedness inventories, switched pens, and a literature Bryngelson could take to Minnesota. The later fluency data were not kind to the simple arrow. Twin tables and scanners found brains without restoring the schoolroom rule.

Orton’s responsibility is the diagram and the Iowa air. Travis’s responsibility is the speech textbook. A parent who still asks whether the left hand caused the stammer is asking Orton-through-Travis. Answer the parent with the handedness essay (08), not with a new rule about forks.

## What later people kept

In reading: a great deal, argued over and revised. In fluency: almost none of the schoolroom practice, and a caution about elegant brains. Imaging after 1996 will find hemispheric differences in adults who stutter. Those differences are not a warrant to cite Orton as if the 1930s had been right all along. Different instrument. Different claim.

He should remain in the reading histories as a founder. He should remain in fluency histories as a lender. Lenders do not get to own the debt collectors.

Iowa in the 1920s was small enough that a neurologist and a speech laboratory could share a sidewalk and a wish. The wish was respectability: a childhood problem with a cortex under it. Reading kept more of that wish, revised through Gillingham and a later society. Stuttering kept the sidewalk and lost the simple cortex. A profile that makes Orton a secret SLP is doing the sidewalk a disservice. He walked it for another disorder.
