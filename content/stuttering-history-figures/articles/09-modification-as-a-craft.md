This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Charles Van Riper opened a speech clinic at Western State Teachers College (later Western Michigan) in 1936. He stuttered. He had already been a patient of other people’s certainty. *Speech Correction: Principles and Methods* (1939) became the book American undergraduates wore out — ten editions — and the stuttering chapters taught a craft with a sequence later students reduced to a mnemonic. Identification. Desensitization. Modification. Stabilization. The mnemonic is not a protocol in this magazine. It is a memory of how a field tried to teach talking without promising a cured tongue.

The craft said: the stammer is going to be there; learn to see it, fear it less, change the shape of the moment, and keep the change when the room gets hard. Pull-outs, cancellations, preparatory sets — those nouns are Van Riper’s furniture. Later clinicians still argue about the furniture. They do not get a how-to from this page.

## What the decades thought they were solving

Not the cause. Van Riper was willing to live with a messy cause if the hour could be useful. That was a fight with Iowa. Johnson wanted the listener’s adjective. Travis wanted the hemisphere. Van Riper wanted a person who could stay in the sentence.

Joseph Sheehan, from a UCLA psychology clinic after 1949, supplied a different vocabulary for the same stubborn hour: approach and avoidance, an iceberg with a visible tip and a heavier underwater mass of shame. Dean Williams, back in Iowa in the 1950s after Indiana, asked a question that sounded simple and was not: what are you doing? The child who could describe the struggle could sometimes change it. Forward-moving speech, in Williams’s later teaching, was a direction, not a gadget.

Hugo Gregory, at Northwestern from 1962, would later try to stop the civil war between this craft and the fluency-shaping machines. That peace conference is the next essay’s leftover. This essay is the bench.

## Two or three books

Van Riper 1939, and then *The Nature of Stuttering* and *The Treatment of Stuttering* in the early 1970s, when he allowed himself a longer argument.

Sheehan’s 1953 paper in the *Journal of Psychology*, and the 1970 book *Stuttering: Research and Therapy*.

Williams’s papers and the later Stuttering Foundation pamphlets that carried his teacherly voice into school folders. The pamphlets are not reproduced here.

## Who was left out

People who wanted the stammer gone, not redesigned. Fluency-shaping will speak for them. Children too young for a craft that depends on talking about talking. Speakers whose language at home was not the clinic’s English. Black clients in Midwestern university clinics that did not say so in the prefaces.

Women who ran Van Riper’s rooms and Sheehan’s groups — Vivian Sheehan continued the UCLA work after 1983 — appear as helpers in too many sentences. This pack will at least name Vivian in the iceberg essay.

## Residue

A modern fluency specialist who still teaches a client to stay in a moment of stuttering is speaking Van Riper whether the mnemonic is said aloud or not. A specialist who refuses to do so is also answering him. The craft is a living argument, not a relic. The relic is the promise that the argument ends.

Western Michigan still has his name on things. The book spines still turn up in faculty offices. The residue is a permission to work with the stammer as it is. The abuse of the residue is a photocopied hierarchy handed to a parent as weekend homework.
