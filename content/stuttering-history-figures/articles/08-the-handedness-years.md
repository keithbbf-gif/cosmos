This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Samuel T. Orton (1879–1948) is remembered first for reading. The same neurological imagination — two hemispheres, one that should lead, a child whose lead is messy — was offered to stuttering. Lee Edward Travis took the offer into Iowa’s speech laboratory. Bryng Bryngelson carried laterality to Minnesota and published, in the 1930s, comparisons of stutterers’ handedness with everyone else’s.

The practical cousin of the theory was a cultural habit already in the schools: stop the left hand. If mixed dominance causes a stammer, a forced right hand might cause one too, and a restored lead might heal it. Parents were told to watch the fork, the pen, the throwing arm. Some children who stuttered were also children who had been switched. The correlation was irresistible. Irresistible is not the same as causal.

## What the years thought they were solving

A visible, countable, respectable cause. Not a wet tongue. Not a weak will. Not, yet, a listener’s adjective. A hemisphere. Physicians could draw it. Psychologists could test it with dichotic listening later, and with cruder sidedness inventories first. Bryngelson’s 1937 paper with Bernice Rutherford and his 1939 *Journal of Speech Disorders* article are typical objects: small groups, laterality scores, a conclusion leaning toward the theory that paid the clinic’s rent.

Travis’s 1931 textbook gave the story a hard cover. Students copied the diagram. Clinicians told left-handed children to pick a side and stay there. The advice felt like medicine.

## What the data did

Group differences in handedness, when they appeared, were modest and messy. Plenty of right-handed children stuttered. Plenty of left-handed children did not. Twin studies and later genetics would not point at a fork. Imaging in the 1990s would find hemispheric differences in adults who stutter — Fox, Ingham, and colleagues, 1996 — but those differences are not Orton’s schoolroom story, and they are not a warrant to switch a child’s pen.

Wingate, who liked a fight, treated the dominance years as a cautionary tale in his later historical writing: an elegant idea that outran its tables. Bloodstein’s handbook editions filed laterality under theories that had been loved.

## Who was left out

Left-handed children who did not stutter and therefore never entered the clinic counts. Children whose handedness was “fixed” and whose stammer stayed. Teachers who enforced the right hand for handwriting drills that had nothing to do with Iowa. The theory blamed a hemisphere and missed a pedagogy.

Women’s laterality data were often folded into mixed groups. Rutherford’s name on the 1937 paper is easy to skip if you only cite Bryngelson.

## Residue

A modern parent still sometimes asks whether the left hand caused the stammer. The question is 1935 arriving in a 2026 waiting room. The honest answer is historical: we thought so; we measured; we stopped switching hands for fluency’s sake. Handedness is real. So is stuttering. The simple arrow between them was a decade’s wish.

Keep Orton in the reading clinic. Keep Travis in the Iowa hallway. Keep Bryngelson’s Minnesota laterality papers on the shelf as a record of what a young American field looked like when it wanted a brain story and did not yet have a scanner.

A separate laterality leftover sits in popular neurology: the idea that a “crossed” writer is a crossed person. Fluency clinicians who still meet that idea in a parent’s first sentence are not meeting a 2026 discovery. They are meeting a 1935 inventory that escaped the journal and entered the kitchen. The kind answer is historical and short. We counted. We stopped switching hands for this reason. We did not stop being interested in brains.
