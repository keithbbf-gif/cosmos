This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In the 1950s a delayed auditory feedback loop — your voice in your ear, late — was a laboratory toy that made fluent speakers stumble and, oddly, made some stuttering speakers go smooth. The paradox was irresistible. If a machine can buy fluency, maybe fluency is a timing trick, not a neurosis and not a hemisphere. Israel Goldiamond, in a 1965 chapter, treated stuttering and fluency as operant classes you could shape. Other people built boxes. Hotel ballrooms rented the boxes. University clinics argued about the boxes.

Ronald L. Webster’s Precision Fluency Shaping Program, associated with Hollins College (now Hollins University) and the Hollins Communications Research Institute, is the cleanest academic cousin of this mood. Targets for breath, voicing, and articulation. Slowed speech that is then sped up. A program, not a vibe. This page will not list the targets. It will say that a program existed, was taught, was exported, and was loved by people who wanted the stammer gone rather than befriended.

## What the decades thought they were solving

The visible tip. If the mouth can be made to run without blocks, the underwater half of Sheehan’s iceberg can wait. For some speakers, in some rooms, the mouth did run. Recordings from intensive programs are impressive. The second year is quieter in the brochures.

William H. Perkins, at USC, spent years on phonation, respiration, and the coordination among them. His 1976 *JSHR* paper with Rudas, Johnson, and Bell is a laboratory object: voiced, whispered, silent articulation, and a claim about complexity. Later Perkins turned on his own field’s outcomes in essays that read like a man who had watched too many impressive first weeks.

Martin F. Schwartz’s 1976 book *Stuttering Solved* is the commercial cousin. The title is a claim this article will not repeat as a fact. Airflow as a product sat in the same decade as airflow as a research variable. Readers deserve the difference.

## Two or three rooms

The DAF booth.

Hollins and its imitators: residential, intensive, a new way of talking taken home on tape.

The hotel seminar: a weekend, a device, a testimonial. Dieffenbach’s structure in polyester.

Einer Boberg and Deborah Kully’s Comprehensive Stuttering Program (1985) tried to keep the prolonged-speech engine and add maintenance, self-help, and a Canadian institutional address (ISTAR, 1986). That is the serious version of the machine years: measure, follow, admit relapse.

## Who was left out

People who became fluent in a whisper and could not stand the new voice. People who could not pay for a month away. Children who were run through adult targets. Speakers of languages the English targets were not built for. The spouse who now lived with a person who sounded like a training tape.

Modification clinicians were left out of the brochures, then caricatured as people who “taught stuttering.” The caricature was useful for sales.

## Residue

A modern device in the ear — delayed or frequency-altered feedback as a daily aid — is a descendant. So is every prolonged-speech protocol that still has a university address. So is every ad that uses the word *solved*. The residue worth keeping is Boberg’s maintenance question: what happens in month fourteen? The residue worth refusing is the title that ends the argument.

Gregory’s Northwestern workshops tried to marry the machine to the craft. Guitar’s later textbook would present an “integrated” approach as if the civil war had a peace treaty. Treaties are documents. Speakers are not.
