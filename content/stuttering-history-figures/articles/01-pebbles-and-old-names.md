This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The oldest Western story about a difficult mouth is not a case note. It is a politician on a beach. Plutarch, writing centuries after Demosthenes of Athens (c. 384–322 BCE), says the orator filled his mouth with pebbles, shouted against the surf, and ran uphill reciting. Later schoolmasters loved the picture. It is a usable myth and a bad clinic. We do not know what Demosthenes’s speech actually did. We know what later writers needed him to have done: a famous man who *fixed it* by effort.

That hunger — a famous mouth, a simple drill, a before-and-after — is older than speech-language pathology. It will return as a knife in 1841, as a chewing exercise in Vienna, as a commercial paperback that claims the problem is solved. The pebble is the first prop.

## What the old words were

Greek and Latin medical writers did not say *stuttering*. They said *ischnophonia*, *psellismos*, *balbuties*, a thick tongue, a wet brain, a timid chest. Aristotle’s tradition links hesitant speech with a cold or moist temperament. Hippocratic notes notice sudden speechlessness after a stroke — which is a different failure — and also notice children who do not speak cleanly. The drawer is mixed. *Aphonia* points at the larynx. *Balbuties* points at a stumble. A magazine that collapses them into “they didn’t have a word yet” is being kind to the present.

Medieval Arabic and Latin physicians kept the humoral frame. Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna) and the writers who excerpted him treat hesitant speech among other voice faults: dryness, moisture, a short uvula, a habit learned in fear. The interesting pages are the ones that refuse a moral insult. A child who repeats a syllable is not automatically a fool or a liar. Most pages do not refuse. Caregivers did the daily work without a profession attached. The physician wrote a line and left. The mother, the nurse, the grammar master — they lived with the block. Early texts almost never name those people.

## Three rooms that are not clinics

An Athenian assembly is not a therapy room. A medieval schoolroom is not a fluency program. A Renaissance elocution closet is not Van Riper’s Western Michigan clinic. What they share is a public penalty. Speech that stops is heard as character. The orator who cannot finish a sentence loses the vote. The choirboy who blocks loses the part. The later American academy will spend fifty years arguing whether the trouble lives in the mouth, the ear, the hemisphere, or the listener’s adjective. The older world already had an answer: it lives in the person, and the person should try harder.

Jewish, Christian, and Islamic commentators sometimes read Moses’s “slow of speech” (Exodus 4:10) as a stammer. That reading is a homily, not an onset study. Keep it in the sermon. Do not put it in a prevalence table.

## What this era did not do

It did not count. There is no thousand-family survey, no sex ratio, no persistence curve. Andrews and Harris are 1964. Yairi and Ambrose are later still.

It did not localize a fluency network. Broca’s jar is 1861 and it is about words that will not come after a lesion, not about a child who knows the word and cannot start it. Do not file Demosthenes under aphasia.

It did not hire a speech correctionist. That job is still rhetoric, pulpit, and the schoolroom.

It did, sometimes, notice that fear makes speech worse and that a practiced cadence makes it easier. Those two observations will be rediscovered with new instruments for two thousand years. The pebble is a cadence toy. So is Colombat’s later metronome habit. So is a delayed auditory feedback machine. The toy changes. The hunger does not.

A modern first visit that begins by asking what the person can still do, and whether the person is ashamed of the miss, is closer to the better old pages than to Plutarch’s beach. The beach is useful. It is also late literature. The old names were already a medical and moral object. They had not yet become a laboratory one.
