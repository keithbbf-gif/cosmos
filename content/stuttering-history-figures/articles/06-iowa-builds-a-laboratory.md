This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Lee Edward Travis came to the University of Iowa in the 1920s and treated stuttering as if it were a problem a psychology department could own. *Speech Pathology* (1931) is an early American textbook that does not apologize for the laboratory. Handedness, hemisphere dominance, the nervous child, the recording device — Iowa stacked them on a Midwestern campus and invited graduate students to take a piece.

Wendell Johnson arrived in 1926 as an undergraduate who stuttered and stayed until he died in the same town in 1965. The sibling profession pack has him as an ASHA president and a semantics teacher. This essay keeps him as the man who made Iowa synonymous with a theory of stuttering: the trouble is not in the mouth; it is in the diagnosis. That theory will get its own essay, because in 1939 it walked into an orphanage. This essay is the building around that door.

## What the campus thought it was solving

Iowa thought it was replacing masters and surgeons with measurement. A stammerer could be timed, wired, asked about parents, sorted by handedness. Bryng Bryngelson took the laterality piece north to Minnesota after an Iowa master’s (1926) and a 1931 doctorate. Samuel Orton, more famous for reading, supplied a neurological story about mixed dominance that Travis could use. The story was elegant. The later data were not kind to it. Elegance is not a defense.

The clinic and the laboratory shared hallways. Students who stuttered became the field. That is Iowa’s glory and its conflict of interest. A science staffed by its subjects can hear things a detached surgery cannot. It can also protect a theory because the theorist’s own mouth depends on it.

## Two or three objects

Travis’s 1931 book is one object.

The Iowa speech clinic as an address is another. Visitors came. ASHA’s early membership — still the American Academy of Speech Correction in the 1920s — is thick with Iowa names. Robert West, Smiley Blanton, and the Wisconsin line are a rival geography. Do not flatten them. Wisconsin is psychiatry and the first university clinic (1914). Iowa is the experiment.

A third object is a master’s thesis spine, 1939, Mary Tudor. It does not belong to this essay’s argument except as a warning label on the building. The next essay opens that spine.

## Who was left out

Farm children who never reached Iowa City. Black stammerers in a university that was not waiting for them. Women who ran the daily clinic hours and then typed the men’s papers. The orphanage children next door in Davenport, who were available *because* they had no parents to refuse a labeling experiment.

Iowa’s published photographs — the ones later archives still hold — are of labs and white graduate students. That is not a complete census of American stuttering in 1935. It is a complete census of who got to be the science.

## Residue

Ask an older American clinician where stuttering research lived and many will still say Iowa, the way aphasia people say Boston. The residue is a permission and a scar. Permission: fluency is a university subject. Scar: a university subject can be done to people who cannot leave.

Van Riper built a different room in Kalamazoo: less theory of cause, more craft of talking. Bloodstein, at Brooklyn, would later try to read everybody and refuse a single cause. Both rooms are answers to Iowa, even when they are polite.

This series will not copy the sibling pack’s Iowa sentences. The campus is the same. The question here is narrower. What did a laboratory think a stammer *was*, before the newspaper found the orphanage?
