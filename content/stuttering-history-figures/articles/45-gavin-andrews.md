This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Gavin Andrews’s fluency fame is a 1964 cloth book. *The Syndrome of Stuttering*, published by the Spastics Society Medical Education and Information Unit with Heinemann, came out of the departments of psychological medicine and child health at the University of Newcastle upon Tyne. Mary Harris, a speech pathologist and therapist, is on the title page. So are D. W. K. Kay, R. F. Garside, and a list of testers under Donald Court and Martin Roth. The Thousand Family Study weather is in the room. A magazine that prints only Andrews as a lone epidemiologist is stealing a committee.

Wikidata and public biographical notes give a birth date of 6 August 1931 (John Gavin Andrews). UNSW Sydney lists him as Emeritus Professor of Psychiatry from 2010, with a later career in depression, classification, cost-effectiveness, and internet treatment of common mental disorders — hundreds of papers, a 2018 Australian Mental Health Prize. He is living in the institutional present this pack can see. Stuttering is the early work. Do not drag the later psychiatry into a fluency protocol. Do not ignore it as if the man stopped in 1964.

## The 1964 object

Prevalence by age and sex. Intelligence, laterality, social class — the chapter heads of a mid-century syndrome book. Onset and the “severe syndrome” as a developmental story. The numbers were used for decades, sometimes past their climate. Later Illinois work (Yairi, Ambrose) and later reviews revised prevalence and recovery. That is how epidemiology is supposed to behave. Wingate’s definition paper, the same year, is a different 1964. Keep them in the same semester and out of each other’s footnotes.

Andrews also wrote later on stuttering treatment and epidemiology from Sydney. The Newcastle book is why he is in a *history* roster. The Sydney vita is why the figure_dates field says “b. 1931” and not a closed interval.

## What later people kept

A sex-ratio sentence every intro lecture still borrows. A permission to count in a population, not only in a clinic. What they discarded: some of the 1964 certainty about class and laterality. What they still owe: Mary Harris’s name on the same title page.

Living subject. Published work only. No private biography. No generated face. No how-to.

A psychiatrist who begins in Newcastle stammering and spends fifty later years on anxiety and depression is not a lost fluency man. He is a reminder that the 1964 book sat in psychological medicine, not in an American speech department. The later internet-treatment work is someone else’s series. The 1964 cloth is this series: a population, a sex ratio, a committee title page, a number every lecture still borrows and every later review has the right to revise. Harris’s name stays on the borrow.
