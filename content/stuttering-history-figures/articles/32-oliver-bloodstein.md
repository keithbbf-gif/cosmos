This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Oliver Bloodstein’s *A Handbook on Stuttering* (first edition 1969) is the book a doctoral student was handed when the professor did not want a brand. Editions stacked up; Nan Bernstein Ratner joined later ones; a sixth edition appeared in 2008. The tone is the method: read everybody, refuse a single cause, keep the tables honest. Brooklyn College of the City University of New York was the address for more than fifty years.

He was born in 1920, took a doctorate at Iowa, served in the U.S. Army in the South Pacific, joined Brooklyn in 1948, directed the Speech and Hearing Center late in the career, and died 4 July 2010 in Albany, New York, age eighty-nine, formerly of Brooklyn (Riverview Funeral Home notice). ASHA Fellow; Honors of the Association; Malcolm Fraser Award, 1999. The honors are real. The handbook is why he is here.

## The hypothesis and the shelf

The continuity hypothesis — that stuttering develops out of ordinary disfluency — is Bloodstein’s most argued idea. It is a friend of Johnson’s listener and a rival of Yairi’s early-onset descriptions. Friend: everyone bumps; some people learn to struggle. Rival: if the first days already look special, continuity is a story about the wrong starting line. Bloodstein knew it was a hypothesis. Later handbook editions kept the argument on the page instead of declaring a win.

*Stuttering: The Search for a Cause and Cure* (1993) is the slimmer, more public book. The word *cure* in the subtitle is a historian’s irony, not a hotel promise. He did not sell a solved mouth.

## What later people kept

A habit of the literature review as an ethical act. A suspicion of schools that only cite themselves. What they discarded, or revised: continuity as a full theory of onset. What they still use: the handbook as a map of who said what, in which year, with how many subjects.

He is a speech-language pathologist and a researcher. The Iowa doctorate is the warrant for Dr. Bloodstein. The Brooklyn classroom is the residue. A used copy of the 1969 boards still turns up in faculty offices with underlining that stops at laterality and starts again at learning. CUNY’s doctoral faculty line meant the handbook was not only a Brooklyn undergraduate object. Students from across the city could take the refusal of a single cause as a method. That refusal is the late-century gift. It is also why a 2026 syllabus that assigns one chapter from one school is doing less than he did.
