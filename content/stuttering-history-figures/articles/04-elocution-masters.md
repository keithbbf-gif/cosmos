This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

James Hunt’s *Stammering and Stuttering, Their Nature and Treatment* appeared in London in 1861. The date is easy to remember because it is also Broca’s year, and the two books have nothing kind to say to each other. Broca is looking at a brain in a jar. Hunt is looking at a paying pupil in a room. Hunt’s father, Thomas, had already run a stammering practice. The son inherited a trade and wrote it up as science.

The book is specific about drills and about famous patients in the way a fashionable practice is always specific. Charles Kingsley sat in this world. So did other Victorian names that later pamphlets like to list. A magazine should not turn those sittings into testimonials. What matters is the structure: a private master, a course of lessons, a promise that delivery can be taught, a fee.

## What the decades thought they were solving

Elocution said the problem was a bad habit of the speaking body — breath, jaw, the attack on a consonant — and that a gentleman (the pupil is usually a gentleman) could be restyled. Alexander Melville Bell’s Visible Speech, published in 1867, belongs on this shelf even though its larger life is deaf education and a family argument about oralism. The Bells taught speech as something you could see. A stammerer in that room was another mouth to be redrawn.

American cities imported the trade. Newspaper advertisements for “speech correction” before 1914 are often elocution in a medical hat. Edward Wheeler Scripture will later try to drag the same mouths into a Yale laboratory. The master and the laboratory will fight for the rest of the century.

Hunt died in 1869, age thirty-six. The same vita includes the Anthropological Society of London and a polygenist racial science that later biology discarded and later ethics condemned. A fluency history that prints only the 1861 title page is doing public relations for a drawing-room. Keep the society in the same paragraph as the stammering book. The man who sold a corrected mouth also sold a ranked human species.

## Two or three houses

Hunt’s London practice is one house.

The Bell teaching rooms — Edinburgh, then North America — are another. Visible Speech charts will show up in the sibling profession pack. Here they matter as a fluency fantasy: if you can see the sound, you can command it.

A third house is the cheaper school for stammering children, the kind Charles Bluemel later remembered from his English boyhood: improvement on the grounds, relapse on the train home. That house rarely leaves a title page. Bluemel’s 1913 book will be one of the first to treat the relapse as data.

## Who was left out

Working-class stammerers do not buy Hunt. Girls are admitted as exceptions. Jewish and Irish patients appear in English notes as types, not as authors. The colonial subject appears in Hunt’s other books as a specimen. The drawing-room method has a zip code.

Teachers in ordinary schools already managed blocked children with silence, mockery, or a command to start over. That management is the mass history. The master’s book is the surviving history.

## Residue

Van Riper’s first edition of *Speech Correction* (1939) still has to argue with this furniture: the idea that a better recitation is a better life. Some of the furniture was useful. Breath is real. A feared sound is real. A practiced easier onset is a real clinical object later people will argue about. The master’s error was the product. He sold a finished gentleman.

A modern reader who meets “elocution” on a community-college poster is meeting a thin descendant. The thick history is a fee, a famous pupil, and a racial science in the back catalog.
