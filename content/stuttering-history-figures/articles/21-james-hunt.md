This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

James Hunt’s *Stammering and Stuttering, Their Nature and Treatment* (London, 1861) is a practitioner’s book with scientific manners. The manners matter. Mid-Victorian London would buy a corrected mouth if the master sounded like a man of science. Hunt’s father, Thomas Hunt, had already sold that mouth. The son inherited rooms and wrote them up.

He was born in 1833 and died in 1869, thirty-six years old. The stammering practice is only half the vita. The other half is the Anthropological Society of London, which he helped to found, and a polygenist racial science that ranked human beings as if they were breeds. Later biology discarded the science. Later ethics condemned the ranking. A fluency profile that prints only Kingsley in the waiting room is a drawing-room brochure.

## The practice

Hunt treated stammering as a teachable disorder of delivery: breath, articulators, the attack on a feared sound. He published cases the way a fashionable doctor publishes cases. Famous literary and political names attach to the Hunt rooms in later retellings. This pack will not run a testimonial list. The structure is the fact: a fee, a course, a promise that a gentleman can be restyled.

1861 is also Broca’s year. Hunt is not looking at a jar. He is looking at a pupil. The two 1861s should stay in the same century and out of each other’s sentences.

## The society

The Anthropological Society was not a hobby. Hunt used it to argue that human groups were separately originated and unequally endowed. Stammering, in that climate, could be read as a degeneration or a type. Even when Hunt’s speech book stays practical, the author’s other books are in the shop. Readers of SLPWOW deserve the shop.

He died too young to become a grand old man. The speech trade went on without him — elocutionists, then Scripture, then the American academy. The racial society’s afterlife is a different, longer embarrassment.

## What later people kept

A usable description of feared sounds and of the social penalty of a block. A warning, if they will take it, that a fluency expert can be a racial scientist before lunch. The drills themselves belong in a locked historical cabinet, not on a parent blog.

British *stammer* is Hunt’s word. American *stutter* is the later house word of this series. He used both in a title. That is why the title is still handy.
