This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Charles Sidney Bluemel (Sidney in some catalogs, Sydney in Duchan) was a psychiatrist who never stopped writing about his own mouth. Born 17 July 1884 in London, he stammered and wheezed through an English boyhood, spent time at a school for stammerers, improved on the grounds, and relapsed on the way back to ordinary school. That relapse — later a cliché of intensive programs — is already in his early life. He emigrated, took an M.D. at the University of Colorado in 1916, practiced in Denver, and owned Mount Airy Sanitarium from 1927 to 1953. He died in Denver on 17 December 1960.

## The books

*Stammering and Cognate Defects of Speech* (1913) is early, thick with history, and already trying to separate a simple stammer from a complicated one. Later papers and *Mental Aspects of Stammering* (1930), *Stammering and Allied Disorders* (1935), and *The Riddle of Stuttering* (1957) move the cause around: auditory imagery, a break in consciousness, inhibition versus the wish to speak, then a more general disorganization under stress. Duchan’s summary of that drift is fair. This pack will not pretend the theory was stable. The stable thing is the riddle in the 1957 title.

Wendell Johnson reviewed *The Riddle* in *Pediatrics* in 1957. The Iowa man and the Denver man did not share a cause. They shared a subject.

Primary and secondary stammering — Bluemel’s most borrowed distinction — gave American clinicians a way to talk about a child who is still easy in the bump and a speaker who has learned to fight, hide, and fear. Van Riper’s later furniture sits on that distinction whether the footnote is printed or not. So does every warning that a preschooler is not a miniature adult.

## What later people kept

The primary/secondary split, revised and renamed. The historical chapters, which later popular writers raided. What they discarded: some of the psychiatry, the sanitarium as a fluency address, the hope for a mental “cure” of a severe stammer. Mount Airy was a psychiatric hospital. Stuttering was one of its owner’s problems, not its only census.

He is a physician. Call him Dr. Bluemel. Do not call him an SLP. Do not turn “think the words” into a 2026 homework meme. Denver is not Iowa and not Vienna. It is a sanitarium owner with a London boyhood and a 1913 title page, writing the riddle until 1957 because the riddle had not agreed to end.
