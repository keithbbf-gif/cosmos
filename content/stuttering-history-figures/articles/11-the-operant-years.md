This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

B. F. Skinner did not write a stuttering manual. His students’ neighbors did. If a behavior has a rate, the rate can be changed by what follows it. Fluency can be reinforced. Stuttering can be placed on extinction, or punished, or — more often in polite clinics — answered with a pause and a request to try again. The vocabulary is dry on purpose. Dry vocabulary can still be used on a child.

Israel Goldiamond’s 1965 chapter is a landmark because it said the quiet part: fluency and stuttering are manipulable classes. Bruce Ryan’s Gradual Increase in Length and Complexity of Utterance (GILCU) is a later, teachable structure for that idea. Roger J. Ingham, first in Australia and then in California, spent decades insisting that if you claim a treatment works you must count, follow, and publish the ugly months. His fights with colleagues were about measurement as much as about kindness.

## What the years thought they were solving

Mystique. Modification looked, to a behaviorist, like talk about talk. Diagnosogenic theory looked like a story. A token and a stopwatch looked like science. For adult intensive programs, the look was often earned: graphs moved. For small children, the same look could become a house where every disfluency had a consequence and every parent became a technician.

Richard Curlee, trained at USC with Perkins and later at Arizona, tried to make treatment research design a subject rather than a slogan. The later phrase “evidence-based practice” is a 1990s import from medicine. Curlee’s 1960s–70s papers are a prologue, as his Arizona memorialists (including Ingham and Conture) said after 2008.

## Two or three objects

Goldiamond 1965.

A GILCU protocol on a clinic clipboard — described, not reproduced.

Ingham’s outcome papers and the correspondence they provoked in *JFD* and *JSHR*. The correspondence is part of the science. Fluency is a field that argues in public.

Lidcombe, in the next essay, is an operant cousin that put the parent in the chair and a verbal contingency on a preschooler’s speech. It is not GILCU. It is not Goldiamond in a cardigan. The family resemblance is real: consequences, counts, a claim that the environment can move the rate.

## Who was left out

The speaker who fluent-for-tokens and stammered-for-life. The child whose “correct” speech was a whisper of fear. Clinicians who refused punishment and were told they were soft. People who stuttered and wanted to talk about shame while the graph only wanted syllables.

Self-help groups in the 1970s (next essay) were, in part, a refusal of the technician. A person is not a rate.

## Residue

Count what you claim. Follow past the last session. Do not call a first-week graph a life. Those are operant gifts, and they are still gifts. The residue to refuse is the idea that a contingency is innocent because it is written in a methods section.

A modern preschool program that uses praise and occasional correction is walking near this history whether it cites Skinner or not. Cite the history. Do not photocopy a 1974 token menu into a parent folder from a brand blog.

Punishment — time-out, a shock, a loud “no” — had a brief, ugly laboratory life in the wider behavior literature. Fluency clinics that stayed with reinforcement and a pause were not therefore innocent. A pause is still a consequence. The ethical question is not whether the graph moved. The question is who owned the graph and whether the speaker could leave the room. 1939 is the extreme of that question. 1974 is the polite version. Keep them on the same shelf without pretending they are the same study.
