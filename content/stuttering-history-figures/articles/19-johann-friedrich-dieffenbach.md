This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

The face in the lithographs is a surgeon’s face: dark coat, the composure of a man who rebuilt noses for a living. Johann Friedrich Dieffenbach was born 1 February 1792 in Königsberg and died 11 November 1847 in Berlin. Plastic surgery histories claim him with reason. Fluency histories claim him with embarrassment. Both claims are about the same hands.

He studied in Königsberg and elsewhere, fought in the Napoleonic endgame as a volunteer, and made his name in Berlin on reconstructive operations. Eyelids, lips, noses, grafts. He was not a speech master. In 1841 he decided stuttering was a surgical object and said so in public.

## The pamphlet

*Die Heilung des Stotterns durch eine neue chirurgische Operation* (Berlin: Albert Förstner, 1841), with a letter to the Institut de France the same year. The tongue, he argued, could be cut so that the stammer would end. Other surgeons copied the idea because his name was already a European instrument. Patients volunteered. Later writers disagree on the numbers and the deaths. This profile will not invent a ledger. It will say the fashion was brief, the infections ordinary, the relapses obvious, and the embarrassment lasting.

A great reconstructive surgeon can still be wrong about a timing disorder. The skill that repairs a destroyed face is not a fluency theory. 1841 is what happens when a famous technique looks for a new indication.

## What later people kept

Almost nothing of the operation. A great deal of the structure: a mechanism, a before-and-after, a forceful name. Hotel-ballroom devices and “solved” paperbacks are Dieffenbach without the blood. That sentence is an analogy, not a libel against any living clinician who uses prolonged speech in a university clinic.

Medical students still meet him as a father of plastic surgery. They should also meet the 1841 title page. A vita that prints only the noses is a brochure.

Berlin in the 1840s was a city that believed a famous professor could end an old misery in a morning. Patients traveled. Medical weeklies took notes. The same weeklies later took notes on the infections and the returned blocks. A reconstructive genius is still a man in a fashion. The fashion is the fluency fact. The noses are someone else’s series.
