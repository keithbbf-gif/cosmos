This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Ronald L. Webster’s Precision Fluency Shaping Program, associated with Hollins College (now Hollins University) in Virginia and the Hollins Communications Research Institute, is the version of the machine years that wore a campus address. Breath, voicing, articulation as targets. Slowed speech that is then sped up. An intensive structure. People who wanted the stammer gone rather than redesigned knew the name. This page will not list the targets or the daily schedule. Those are clinical property. History’s job is to say that a named program existed, was taught, was exported, and was argued with.

Living scholar. Published descriptions, institute pages, and the secondary literature that cites Hollins. No private biography. No hotel-seminar conflation: Webster’s shop is not Schwartz’s 1976 paperback, even if both decades loved airflow. Keep the letterheads apart.

## The job that mattered

A fluency that could be shaped, recorded, and taken home. Goldiamond had said the classes were manipulable. Webster built a curriculum. Boberg and Kully later kept prolonged speech and added maintenance in Edmonton. Gregory tried to marry shaping to modification in Evanston. Guitar put both in a table of contents. Webster is the shaping pole those later peace conferences had to name.

Relapse is the quiet file, here as everywhere. A first-week tape is not a life. Perkins, who had believed in a mechanism, said so from USC. The saying does not erase the people who preferred the new voice to the old block. Preference is data. So is month fourteen.

## What later people kept

A respectable intensive model. A vocabulary of targets. What they discarded, or should watch: the implication that the other bench “taught stuttering.” What they still use: devices and prolonged-speech cousins that do not always print Hollins on the consent form.

Living subject. No guessed dates. No generated face. No protocol.

Hollins is a Virginia campus that most fluency undergraduates never visit and many intensive-program alumni can name. That split is the historical fact: a specialized address, a shaped voice, a network of people who preferred the new talking to the old block. University clinics that later taught “easy onsets” without printing Hollins on the consent form still owe the decade a citation. Owe is not the same as copy. Copy, from a brand blog, is how a target list becomes a home program. This article refuses that list and keeps the letterhead.
