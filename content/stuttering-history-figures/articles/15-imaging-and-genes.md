This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Pauline Howie’s 1981 twin paper in *JSHR* gave the old family story a number. Monozygotic pairs agreed on stuttering more than dizygotic pairs. The number is not a gene. It is a permission to stop saying the parents did it with their eyebrows. Johnson’s listener is not erased by a concordance. The concordance does constrain him.

Ehud Yairi, at the University of Illinois from 1977, with Nicoline Ambrose and colleagues, followed young children from near onset. Persistence versus recovery. Early speech behaviors that were not just “normal bumps plus a nervous mother.” A 1992 longitudinal paper; a 1996 genetics paper with Ambrose and Nancy Cox; the 2005 book *Early Childhood Stuttering*. The Illinois project is why a 2026 clinician can say, without folklore, that many preschoolers recover and some do not, and that the two groups do not look identical on day one.

## 1996 in a scanner

Peter T. Fox, Roger J. Ingham, Janis C. Ingham, and colleagues published in *Nature* in 1996 a PET study of the neural systems of stuttering. The paper is famous because *Nature* is famous. What it actually did was show, in adults, activation patterns that differed with fluency and with treatment-altered speech. It did not find Orton’s schoolroom hemisphere. It did not license a new surgery. It did not tell a parent what to say at dinner.

Later fMRI and diffusion work thickened the map and the arguments: auditory-motor timing, basal ganglia, white-matter tracts, whether a finding is cause or a life of stuttering written into a brain. This article will not referee a 2026 review article. It will say that the scanner years ended the last respectable version of “it’s only a habit” without restoring Dieffenbach.

## What the decades thought they were solving

Respectability. A disorder with a PET figure and a twin table is harder to mock. Funding follows mockery’s end. So does a new reductionism: if it is in the brain, the hour of therapy is a courtesy. The serious labs did not say that. The internet did.

Dennis Drayna’s later genetic work (not a profile here) and other family studies sit on Howie’s shelf. Names of candidate genes change. The family pattern does not. A magazine that prints a 2010 gene name as destiny will look foolish in 2028.

## Who was left out

Children, who cannot lie still in the first scanners. Women, underrepresented in some early imaging samples. Speakers outside the university’s language. People whose stuttering began after a stroke or a head injury — acquired stuttering — filed in a different drawer and then forgotten at the popular lecture.

Illinois’s families were families who could come to Champaign and stay in a study. That is a better sample than Hunt’s drawing-room and still not the world.

## Residue

A modern parent who arrives with a printout about “the stuttering gene” is living in this era. So is a student who thinks the 1996 *Nature* paper ended theory. There was not a mythic stuttering cover in the sense later slides invent; do not invent one. The residue worth keeping: count onset, count recovery, do not blame a mother’s adjective for a concordance. The residue worth refusing: a scan as a personality.

Janis Ingham’s name on the 1996 author line is easy to slide past if you only say “Fox et al.” Do not slide past it. Imaging labs, like Iowa clinics, had a habit of filing women under “et al.” The scanner does not correct that habit. The caption can.
