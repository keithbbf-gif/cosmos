This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1939 Mary Tudor, a graduate student at the University of Iowa, wrote a master’s thesis with a dry title: “An Experimental Study of the Effect of Evaluative Labeling upon Speech Fluency.” The work was done at the Soldiers and Sailors Orphans’ Home in Davenport. Twenty-two children. Some already spoke with noticeable disfluency. Some did not. Half received praise for their speech. Half received the kind of remarks a diagnosogenic theory predicts will make a child begin to struggle: you are a stutterer; watch your mouth; that was a stutter.

Wendell Johnson advised the thesis. The theory was already his: stuttering begins when a listener applies a bad name to ordinary bumps. The orphanage was a convenience. The children were available. They did not have parents at the door.

## What the study thought it was solving

Johnson wanted a cause that lived in language, not in a weak hemisphere. If you can create or worsen stuttering by labeling, the mouth is not the first culprit. The thesis is an attempt to make that claim experimental. Later readers have argued about how strong the speech-change data were. They have not argued, once the newspaper arrived, about the design. You do not tell institutionalized children they are defective speakers in order to test a seminar idea.

The children are already in the public scientific and legal record. This series names the study and the setting. It does not reprint a roster of their names as a feature. It does not invent their nights. The hallway is the fact. The inner life is theirs.

## 2001 and 2007

The thesis sat. Iowa did not advertise it. In 2001 the *San Jose Mercury News* published Jim Dyer’s series and the study acquired a tabloid name, the “Monster Study.” The name is not Tudor’s title. It is what a headline does. Johnson was dead (1965). Tudor was alive and spoke. The University of Iowa apologized. Lawsuits followed. In 2007 a settlement paid surviving plaintiffs. Those are public facts. They are not a clinic lesson in how to do Lidcombe.

A later popular retelling likes to say Johnson “caused stuttering.” That sentence is too clean. Some children were already disfluent. The harm documented in interviews decades later is also shame, self-watchfulness, a lifelong suspicion of one’s own mouth. Diagnosogenic theory predicted watchfulness. It did not have the right to install it.

## Who was left out

Orphanage staff who had to live with the aftermath. The children who were in the “good” labeling group, used as controls. Other Iowa studies that used students who could quit. The 1939 design chose the group that could not quit.

Women in this story are Tudor, who carried the clipboard, and the matrons of the home. The theory is filed under a man’s name. That filing is accurate about power and incomplete about labor.

## Residue

Every later caution about calling a toddler a stutterer sits in the shadow of Davenport, whether the caution is wise or overlearned. Later Illinois data (Yairi, Ambrose) showed that many children who begin to stutter recover, and that early speech behaviors are not just parental hallucination. That evidence argues with Johnson’s cause. It does not excuse 1939.

A modern evaluation that uses careful language, asks the family’s words, and refuses a parlor experiment is not being fashionable. It is refusing this porch. The thesis is in the library. The settlement is in the record. The hunger to prove a theory on a captive group is the part that does not stay in 1939.
