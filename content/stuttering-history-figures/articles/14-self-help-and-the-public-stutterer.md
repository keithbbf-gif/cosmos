This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1947 Malcolm Fraser of Memphis, a businessman who stuttered, talked with Charles Van Riper about a charitable foundation that would print usable information and not wait for a university catalog. The Stuttering Foundation of America — later the Stuttering Foundation — is that object. Pamphlets, then books, then films. Jane Fraser later ran it. The address was a house on Henry Street before it was an institution. ASHA eventually gave Fraser a distinguished service award. The Foundation’s taste has always been closer to the clinic than to the picket line. That is a fact, not an insult.

In January 1977, in California, Bob Goldman and Michael Sugarman incorporated the National Stuttering Project. The first office was a den. Sugarman slept on a couch. A *San Francisco Chronicle* piece helped them find a board. Local groups, a newsletter (*Letting Go*), a style that said people who stutter are a public, not a caseload. The Project became the National Stuttering Association. The institutional history on the NSA’s own site is the warrant for the 1977 date. Do not move it to 1976 because a blog did.

## What these efforts thought they were solving

Isolation. A person can leave a university intensive program fluent on Tuesday and have no one to eat lunch with on Wednesday. Fraser’s answer was paper: a parent could hold a pamphlet. Goldman and Sugarman’s answer was a room: a person could say the feared word in company that would not flinch.

International cousins followed: the International Stuttering Association, national groups in Britain, India, Japan. The International Fluency Association (Boberg was its first president in 1991) is a professional society with a consumer committee, not the same thing as a self-help group. Keep the letterheads apart.

## Two or three objects

A 1947 conversation in Van Riper’s orbit, and the first Foundation printings.

A 1977 California incorporation paper and a church-basement circle of chairs.

A later NSA convention hotel ballroom — the same architectural joke as the commercial “solved” seminar, used for the opposite purpose.

## Who was left out

People who cannot travel or pay a convention fee. Children, at first; family programs came later and unevenly. Speakers whose first language is not the group’s English. Black and brown stammerers in groups that were, for years, photographically white. People who do not want a stuttering identity at all and experience the movement as another label.

Clinicians were sometimes left out on purpose. That refusal was political and, at times, earned. A history that sides only with the academy will miss why the den was necessary.

## Residue

A 2026 client who arrives already having read a Foundation book and already having a group is a different client from Van Riper’s 1938 undergraduate. The public stutterer — on a podcast, in a workplace film, in a presidential news cycle — is a late fact. This article will not use living politicians as décor. The fact is enough: the disorder left the clinic without asking permission.

SLPWOW is a profession site, not an NSA chapter. These essays should link a reader toward history, not toward a household clinic’s intake form.

British stammering self-help, Indian stammering associations, and Japanese groups wrote their own origin stories and do not owe California a royalty. The 1977 den is an American fact, not a world monopoly. A magazine that treats NSA as the only public is doing what Iowa did to the map: one campus, one letterhead, a quiet everywhere else. The International Stuttering Association’s later network is a reminder that the basement idea traveled and changed languages.
