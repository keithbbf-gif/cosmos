This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1830 Marc Colombat de l’Isère opened, in Paris, something he called an Institut orthophonique. The name is doing a lot of work. *Orthophonie* is not yet the Belgian and French profession of the twentieth century. It is a private house for “vices of speech,” especially *bégaiement*, run by a physician who also wrote on women’s surgery and ventriloquism. He published *Du bégaiement et de tous les autres vices de la parole* the same year. A second edition in 1831 carried a report to the Académie de médecine signed by men whose names still sit in other histories: Itard, Esquirol, Hervez de Chégoin.

That report is the hinge of this decade. A stammer is now a thing a learned society will sit still for. It is not yet a thing a university speech clinic will measure. Between those two rooms lies a knife.

## What the decade thought it was solving

The leftover humoral story said the tongue was too wet, too weak, too short, or too tied. Surgeons already knew the frenulum. They had been clipping children’s tongues for other reasons. Physicians who did not want to cut offered rhythm, breathing, and a practiced attack on the feared consonant. Colombat’s method — later nicknamed around the *muta-consonne* and a measured cadence — belongs in that second group. Later advertisements inflated an 1833 prize. The books are real. The prize-as-miracle is sales.

Jean-Marc Gaspard Itard (1774–1838) is in this era because Paris asked him to look, not because he became a fluency specialist. His larger vita is the wild boy of Aveyron and the medicalization of deaf education. The stuttering pages are a slice: a physician who believed speech faults could be described without immediately becoming a moral. That permission matters. It is also not a lineage. There is no “Itard school of stammering.”

German and Austrian writers in the same years used *Stottern* and *Stammeln* and argued about spasm, habit, and the nerves. The argument is medical. The patients are still mostly paying adults and the children of paying adults. Poor children who blocked in a parish school do not enter the treatise.

## Two or three objects, not twelve

Colombat’s 1830/1831 book, with its folded plates, is one object. A later 1840 *Traité de tous les vices de la parole* is the same man thickening the claim. Dieffenbach’s 1841 Berlin pamphlet is the next object, and it belongs to the following essay. Keep them in order. The institute is a house. The pamphlet is a dare.

A third object is quieter: the Académie report. Learned medicine agrees that *bégaiement* is discussable. Once a thing is discussable, a surgeon can offer to end it.

## Who was left out

Women who stammered appear as wives and as rare case headings, not as authors. Jewish and provincial patients appear when they pay. The enslaved and the colonial subject do not appear at all in these Paris books. The daily listener — the parent who finishes the word — is an absence the later Iowa school will fill with a theory, and then with an orphanage.

Elocution masters in London and the American cities ran a parallel trade: less Latin, more drawing-room. They will get their own essay. Do not promote Colombat into that trade. He wanted a medical institute, not a parlor.

## Residue

A modern reader who meets “orthophonie” on a Belgian clinic door is meeting a later profession that borrowed a word. Colombat’s house is not that door. What survived is the habit of treating fluency as a medical object with a private address. What had to be put down is the idea that a vice of speech is a vice of character — and, within a decade, the idea that the tongue itself was the vice.

Paris made stammering sit for a portrait. Berlin, in 1841, decided the portrait needed surgery.
