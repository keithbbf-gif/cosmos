Jack’s least-favorite chore, on the record, is fertilizing that many pots. Mine is walking dead-outs. Neither of those jobs puts a fig on a plate in August.

We have lived both jobs in the same week. A collection of a few hundred trees and a few hundred names, most in **3- and 5-gallon** cans. A much smaller in-ground set. The [Fig Jam interview](https://www.thefigjam.co/p/interview-with-a-fig-grower-keith) is where we said that out loud. None of that is a pantry.

A pantry is two or six trees that finish in your climate, tight enough for August, picked daily, fruit you will eat standing up. Celeste and a Turkey type are pantry candidates even if I am not a fan of them so far. A berry type that has proven here is pantry. A row of unfruited celebrities is a library.

[Pick The Right Fig](https://figroots.com/2025/07/02/pick-the-right-fig-for-you/) already asked flavor, GDD, tight eye, pots, and birds as one checklist. This page is the job behind the checklist. Are you feeding a household this year, or are you keeping names alive until fruit tells the truth?

## The jobs fight

The library wants space, mix, a winter shop, labels, patience, money in sticks. The pantry wants water, morning walks, a net if birds are heavy, and a variety you already believe.

If you spend July shuffling #3s, you will miss the one in-ground tree that is actually ripe. I have done it. Birds do not miss it.

If you only plant pantry trees, you will not try the fig that converts a person who “doesn’t like figs.” I have also done that, years ago, before I knew berry types existed. [An Introduction to Figs](https://figroots.com/an-introduction-to-figs/) is still the right warning on trusted wood and a year or three to prove type. The library is how you run that clock. It is not how you pack a freezer.

Do both if you must. Do not lie about which job a pot is. A #3 of an unfruited name is not “almost a pantry tree.” It is a trial. Treat it like a trial: label, water, wait. Do not plant the whole library in the only good hole.

## Fridge pick-list: pantry trees first

This is the morning I actually run in a ripe week.

There is a card on the fridge. Not a variety encyclopedia. A pick order. Pantry trees first — the in-ground keepers, the two or six names that have already fed us, the pots by the door that are softening this week. Then the library. Not the other way around.

If the library is in a hoop and the pantry is at the house, walk the house first. That sounds dumb until you count pecks. UAEX already named birds, squirrels, and raccoons as the competition. They do not wait for you to finish a pot census.

I have walked wilt on the pot block at 7 a.m. and found the yard tree already opened by something with a beak. The fix is not a better fertilizer. The fix is the order on the card. Pantry. Then library. Then, if there is time, the celebrity that might color tomorrow.

A sour or split fig still comes off on that walk. The split-versus-sour draft is the two-bucket version. This page is which tree you walk toward when you only have twenty minutes.

## Two shopping lists

Pantry: a tool fig that lives here, one honey or gold if you like that, one berry from a person who has eaten it in the South, a hose that reaches, a net. In-ground if the hole drains. Pots if nematodes or a hard freeze is the life.

Library: labels, mix, shop space, patience, cuttings you will actually stick. Not twenty names this week.

If you mix the lists at the checkout, you will come home with twelve names and no net. The birds will explain the rest. A pantry tree without a net in a bird year is a library of pecks. Buy the net for the trees you eat. Let the library take its chances, or roll those pots to the door the week they ripen.

Cuttings are cheap compared to two years of a wrong tree. They are not free if you count pots, mix, shop space, and July water. A collection has a bill. A pantry has a smaller bill and a bowl.

Jack ranks fruit he has eaten. Syrian Dark #2, Jack Lilly, the ones he will actually talk about. That list is pantry research. It is not a shopping list for twenty more pots this week. I am still not a fan of Brown Turkey and Celeste as dessert. I still understand why they exist as tools. The Celeste-and-Turkey draft in this set is that argument. Start there if you want food before you want a personality.

## Count bowls, not names

At the end of July I would rather know what we ate than how many tags we own.

A guest who asks “how many varieties?” and blinks at the number is standing in the library. Offer them a fig from the pantry tree, not a tour of cups. The tour does not convert anyone. The fruit does.

Count bowls. The number that matters is what you ate. The other number is a hobby. I will not invent a harvest total for us. I will say: if the bowls are empty and the tags are many, you are running a library that stole the pantry’s morning.

True-to-type still takes a year or three. That clock is honest. It is also how a collection justifies itself. Just do not confuse the clock with dinner.

## Dead-outs still sting

Inventory still stings. New names especially. I told [The Fig Jam](https://www.thefigjam.co/p/interview-with-a-fig-grower-keith) that, and it is still true. I try to look at what lived. The successes outweigh if you let them. It is also still a sting.

A pantry tree that dies is a different sting. You lost food. Rebuild with a tool name first — something UAEX would recognize in Arkansas, a Celeste or a Turkey type that rebounds — even if I do not love the plate yet. Then put the rare name back in a pot until you trust the hole.

A pantry does not need an inventory of ghosts. A collection does. Write the number. Then go pick something ripe if it is summer. That is the correction. Do not make dead-outs the whole story, and do not skip the count so you can keep bragging on a number that is no longer true.

Do not ship a name you have not fruited as confirmed. Do not keep identity in your head. The labels draft is the pen. The library dies of honesty when you stop writing “the brown one.”

## Three eaters before twenty names

How I would start a house that wants fruit: one tool tree that lives here. One honey or gold if you like that. One berry from a person who has eaten it in the South. In-ground if the hole drains. Pots if nematodes or a hard freeze is the life.

Then stop buying names until you have eaten those three.

That is the sentence I want on the shed next to the wood-versus-fruit card. What do you want on the table in August? If the answer is fruit, plant fewer names and pick them. If the answer is names, keep the shop open and buy jam at the store without guilt.

We want both. The ground is how the pantry wins a little more every year. The pots are how the library stays alive. Call them by their jobs. The week will make more sense. If you only have time for one job this year, pick the pantry. The library will wait. The ripe fig will not. A name in a notebook does not split. A fig on the tree does.

A library can become a pantry one hole at a time. That is the only conversion I trust. Not a shopping cart.
