You knock a tired fig out of a pot and the roots look like they swallowed BBs. Fertilizer will not talk those galls into a fibrous mat. That plant is not hungry. It is occupied.

[Pick The Right Fig](https://figroots.com/2025/07/02/pick-the-right-fig-for-you/) said it in a bullet: if you have nematodes, grow in pots. [An Introduction to Figs](https://figroots.com/an-introduction-to-figs/) said do not root in mystery local soil. I am not writing a fumigation manual. I am writing the collector’s rule: **a rare stick goes in mix you trust.**

## Galls, sand, and a tired top

Texas A&M’s disease notes list root-knot as a common fig problem. Galls. Swellings. The top looks tired no matter how you feed. The 2015 figs PDF is blunt that **sandy ground** is where root-knot gets ugly. Clay is not innocent, but sand is the usual story. NC State’s fig-disease page puts cold and nematodes as the two limits in their climate, and root-knot as a major problem on sand.

You knock a plant out of a pot, or you lift a sad in-ground tree, and the roots have beads and knots. Not the even, fibrous mat you want. Pale fibrous roots are the picture you want. The bad ones look like the root swallowed BBs. The plant is short, yellow-leaning, wilted when the pot is still heavy. A lot of “I fertilized and nothing happened” is this. The roots cannot work.

I will not diagnose your photo from here. If you see galls, treat the plant as infested. Do not spread that soil to a clean pot. A shovel that just dug a knotted plant can move dirt to a clean hole. Knock off what you can. Do not plant the next rare name in the same spoonful.

A sad plant may also be wet feet or winter. Dig before you write a novel. The knots, if they are there, will not be subtle. Once you have seen them, you will not unsee them. That is useful. It stops you from buying a third bag of fertilizer for a root that cannot work.

Do not knock a rare name in July to satisfy curiosity. Wait for pot-up or dormancy if the plant is otherwise living. Knock a tool plant you can spare if you need a picture of a healthy root first.

## What the universities actually said — and what they did not

TAMU: plant **nematode-free stock** into soil that is as clean as you can get, and understand sand is the ugly case. NC State: there is **no chemical control for established plants**. Their optional hole talk is a 3–4 foot wide hole with the soil replaced **18–24 inches** deep, plus fallow, as a site reset — not a spray. LSU AgCenter Pub. 1802 adds a crop-history warning: avoid old **tomato, okra, or tobacco** ground. Those crops share the pest.

I will not name a fumigant recipe. TAMU’s older handbook talk about site treatment before planting is exactly the kind of thing that goes stale, and it was never meant for a living pot. Rules and products change. **[VERIFY] with your county agent** before anyone sells you a chemical story for a tree that is already in the ground. Louisiana label law is not Arkansas label law. I will not copy a PDF onto your hose.

A well-established root system lives with pressure better than a baby. I would not start the baby in the infested sand. If a tool tree is in the ground and living, I might leave it and keep it strong with water and mulch. I would not plant the collection around it in the same sand.

Grafting a dessert name onto a knotted root system is a way to keep a hole. It is not a way to escape the nematode. The top can be wonderful. The root is still the root.

## A rare stick in mix you trust

Root it in coir, Promix, DE, perlite — the media we already talk about. Not a coffee can of garden dirt “to save money.” [Outdoor](https://figroots.com/outdoor/) uses yard dirt for **bulk** starts of wood we can replace. That is free rootstock logic. That is not a rare-name logic.

If the plant has to live in a county with a nematode reputation, it can stay in a pot for life. Water, afternoon shade, mix that drains, the winter shuffle. The pot-versus-ground draft in this set is the rest of that life. The Introduction flagged Florida dirt for a reason. If you are on sand and you want a collection, pots are the default, not a temporary nursery. In-ground is a tool tree you are willing to share with the worms.

If you want it in the ground, you need a hole you believe is clean, or a raised bed of imported mix, and you still accept that TAMU says the fig will likely meet nematodes eventually. A $40 cutting into sand is a donation. I would not make it.

Box-store soil crossing state lines is already an Introduction warning for other reasons. Nematodes are one more. I would not “save money” on a rare stick by filling a cup from the tomato row. LSU 1802 already named that ground. The savings are a gall.

## Wood travels. Galls stay in the trash.

The wood is not the gall. A dormant stick, washed, stuck in clean mix, is a new root system. That is one reason cuttings are how figs travel. Do not pot the old knotted roots into the new cup “to help it.” Leave the galls in the trash.

If you air-layer, you also get new roots in clean wrap. Same idea. Do not dunk the layer in the old hole’s sand.

I would not take cuttings from an infested plant and stick them in clean mix *with dirty roots attached*. A cutting is wood. I would not sell or trade a potted fig I knew was knotted without saying it. That is how the mess travels. A potted fig can carry galls in the mix. If I trade a plant, I would knock it and look. If I see beads, I say so or I do not trade. A cutting is cleaner.

## A knock-out morning

This is the morning I would actually run if a tool plant looks tired on sand and I still want the name.

I wait until I am already potting or the plant is dormant. I knock it. Fibrous and pale: I put it back and I look at water and shade next, not a novel. Beads and knots: the top is now a wood source, not a hole resident. I take **6–8 inch, three-node** sticks from live wood, wash them, and stick them in mix I trust. The knotted roots and the dirt they came in go in the trash, not into the next #3.

The hole can stay empty, or it can take a tool tree I am willing to share with the worms. It does not get the rare name. If I ever reset a hole, NC State’s 3–4 foot, 18–24 inch replacement is the conversation I would have with the county agent — **[VERIFY]** — not a product I will name here.

See galls: pots, clean mix, do not share the dirt. No galls, clay that drains, a variety you have eaten: ground if you want. Sand and a rare cutting: pot.

Pots keep the stories separate. That is the point of the plastic. I would rather water a #3 for the rest of a name’s life than donate it to a hole I cannot lift. The hole can wait. The stick cannot survive a gall it never should have met.
