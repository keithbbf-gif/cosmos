Pick The Right Fig said it in a bullet. If you have nematodes, grow in pots. The Intro page said do not root in local soil if you are in a root-knot county — Florida talk shows up for a reason.

Texas A&M’s disease notes: root-knot is one of the common fig problems. Galls. Swellings. The top looks tired no matter how you feed. Sandy ground is where it gets ugly. Clay is not innocent, but sand is the usual story.

I am not writing a fumigation manual. I am writing the collector’s rule: **a rare stick goes in mix you trust.**

## What it looks like

You knock a plant out of a pot or you lift a sad in-ground tree and the roots have beads and knots. Not the even, fibrous mat you want. The plant is short, yellow-leaning, wilted when the pot is still heavy.

A lot of “I fertilized and nothing happened” is this. The roots cannot work.

I will not diagnose your photo from here. If you see galls, treat the plant as infested. Do not spread that soil to a clean pot.

## What I would do with a named cutting

Root it in coir, Promix, DE, perlite — the media we already talk about. Not a coffee can of garden dirt “to save money.” The Outdoor page uses yard dirt for **bulk** starts of wood we can replace. That is free rootstock logic. That is not Black Madeira logic.

If the plant has to live in a county with a nematode reputation, it can stay in a pot for life. The pot draft is the rest of that life: water, shade, shuffle, mix that drains.

If you want it in the ground, you need a hole you believe is clean, or a raised bed of imported mix, and you still accept that TAMU says the fig will likely meet nematodes eventually. A well-established root system lives with pressure better than a baby. I would not start the baby in the infested sand.

## What I would not do

I would not dump a chemical into a live pot because a forum said so. TAMU’s old handbook talks site treatment *before* planting and warns against using that material around living plants. Rules and products change. [VERIFY] with your county agent. I will not name a recipe.

I would not take cuttings from an infested plant and stick them in clean mix *with dirty roots attached*. A cutting is wood. Leave the galls in the trash.

I would not sell or trade a potted fig I knew was knotted without saying it. That is how the mess travels.

## In-ground keepers

If a tool tree (Celeste, a Turkey type) is in the ground and living, I might leave it and feed and water like TAMU says — keep the plant strong. I would not plant the collection around it in the same sand.

Grafting a dessert name onto a knotted root system is a way to keep a hole. It is not a way to escape the nematode. The top can be wonderful. The root is still the root.

## The short version

## Cuttings off an infested plant

The wood is not the gall. A dormant stick, washed, stuck in clean mix, is a new root system. That is one reason cuttings are how figs travel. Do not pot the old knotted roots into the new cup “to help it.”

If you air-layer, you also get new roots in clean wrap. Same idea. Do not dunk the layer in the old hole’s sand.

## Sharing plants

A potted fig can carry galls in the mix. If I trade a plant, I would knock it and look. If I see beads, I say so or I do not trade. A cutting is cleaner.

Box-store soil crossing state lines is already an Intro warning for other reasons. Nematodes are one more.

## Florida and sand talk

The Intro flagged Florida dirt for a reason. If you are on sand and you want a collection, pots are the default, not a temporary nursery. In-ground is a tool tree you are willing to share with the worms.

See galls: pots, clean mix, do not share the dirt. No galls, clay that drains, a variety you have eaten: ground if you want. Sand and a $40 cutting: pot.

Patience is the other pest. A tired plant may also be wet feet or winter. Dig before you write a novel. The knots, if they are there, will not be subtle. Once you have seen them, you will not unsee them. That is useful. It stops you from buying a third bag of fertilizer for a root that cannot work.

## What a healthy root looks like

Fibrous. Pale. No beads. You will know the bad ones because they look like the root swallowed BBs. If you have never knocked a pot, knock a tool plant you can spare so you have a picture. Then knock the sad one.

Do not knock a rare name in July to satisfy curiosity. Wait for pot-up or dormancy if the plant is otherwise living.

A sad in-ground fig on sand is a candidate for a pot rescue if the top still has a stick worth cutting. The hole can stay empty. The name can live in mix.

## Do not share the shovel story

A shovel that just dug a knotted plant can move dirt to a clean hole. I am not running a hospital. I am saying: knock off what you can, do not plant the next rare name in the same spoonful. Pots keep the stories separate. That is the point of the plastic.
