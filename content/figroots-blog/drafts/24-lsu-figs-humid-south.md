If you live in steam, a fig released by LSU is not magic. It is a clue. Those plants were selected where summers are wet and rust is not a rumor.

I will not invent a tasting score. I will not tell you we ranked LSU Purple against **Negra d’Agde**. Jack’s plate from [The Fig Jam](https://www.thefigjam.co/p/interview-with-a-fig-grower-keith) is fruit we ate — Red Sicilian, Syrian Dark #2, Jack Lilly, NSDC, Negra d’Agde. I am still not a fan of Celeste or Brown Turkey so far. An LSU tag does not skip that wait.

## The real releases

LSU AgCenter’s 2015 note, Charlie Johnson talking, is the list I will stand on.

**LSU Purple**, 1991. **LSU Gold**, 1995. Then in **2007**: **Tiger**, **Champagne**, and **O’Rourke**. Those are the named public releases. They are **common figs**. No wasp. That is the type we grow.

TAMU’s 2015 trial table put LSU Purple in the “suggested for trial” column: medium to dark purple skin, strawberry flesh, mild and sweet in *their* description, closed eye, relatively cold hardy, widely adapted across the Gulf. That is Texas ink, not our plate. Look at **your** ostiole. Photo it.

Johnson on the others, still LSU’s words: Tiger fruit 30 to 50 percent larger than Celeste, matures about seven days earlier, longer fruiting period on a relatively small tree. Champagne is yellow, good cold resistance in their talk, a brown streak when ripe. O’Rourke they call an “improved Celeste” — larger fruit, more productivity. I will print the release notes. I will not print a FigRoots score.

Gold is a yellow fig with light red to pink pulp in AgCenter descriptions I have read. Same rule. If you want a gold fig in humidity, an LSU name is a better first bet than a thin-skinned Mediterranean postcard. Then taste. Then decide.

I would not start with a rare LSU numbered seedling I cannot trace. Johnson mentioned lines still under evaluation — a black fig numbered in their 2015 note, later than the others. Evaluation is not a release. A Facebook “LSU Tiger” from a stranger is the true-to-type problem with a prettier acronym.

## O’Rourke’s program, then a pause, then a second look

Ed O’Rourke joined LSU horticulture in the mid-1950s. Crosses over about ten years, mid-1950s into the mid-1960s. Johnson: caprifig from California as the male parent, Celeste from Louisiana and Hunt from Georgia as female parents. The program was interrupted in the mid-1960s. Trees survived at Hammond, on the Baton Rouge campus, at other stations.

In the 1990s Johnson and Jimmy Boudreaux worked with O’Rourke to collect and identify those plants, then compared them over about five years before release. Fruit size, cold-hardiness, disease resistance. They wanted trees of the same age in the same soil. That is why I treat an LSU name as humidity homework, not as a personality.

Historically those releases went out public — no plant patent in that 2015 telling — so any nursery can hold them, and any shrug can type the letters. The letters are not the fruit. Buy from a source that has fruited it, or a nursery that actually holds the release. Box-store “LSU Purple” can be a shrug. Wait for fruit. Year one is a rumor. Year two is a conversation.

## Celeste is still the Louisiana yard standard

Johnson, 2015: the most popular fig variety grown in Louisiana is **Celeste**, around for more than a hundred years. Elsewhere in the Southeast, Brown Turkey enjoys greater popularity. Figs are pass-along plants. The same clone wears a pile of names.

That is why I keep a tool tree even though I am not a fan of Celeste so far. LSU did not rip Celeste out to make room for Purple. They used Celeste as a parent. A county-agent Celeste in a Searcy yard is still the plant UAEX can actually name. The Celeste-and-Turkey draft is the tool job. An LSU name is the next ring: still a climate-aware fig, a little more interest on the plate *if* the fruit is good.

I will not tell you to rip out a good berry type because it does not say LSU on the tag. I will not tell you every LSU fig is tight-eyed and rust-proof. Releases differ. Years differ. A wet August still cracks fruit.

TAMU’s older closed-eye list was Celeste, Texas Everbearing, Alma. LSU Purple often gets tossed into humid-South closed-eye talk. Look at the fruit. Tight is a door. Physics can still pop a balloon.

## Gulf timing is not Ozark timing

“June is time for figs in Louisiana” is a Baton Rouge sentence. I would not copy it onto an Ozark ridge. GDD and nights. A late freeze after budbreak. Water. We have drafts for those.

Purple shows up in grower talk as a plant that will fruit younger than some. AgCenter has said Purple can throw a small crop a year or two after planting, and a heavy July main crop with a later tail. “Into December” is a Gulf sentence. I would not count on December figs in central Arkansas. **[VERIFY]** the tail in your yard, your year.

Champagne’s yellow fruit is a bird problem in Johnson’s own mouth: birds seem to see yellow figs better. That is the birds draft, not a flavor note. Green-ripe hiding is not the same as yellow-ripe lighting up.

Plant an LSU name where you would plant any humid-South fig: morning sun, afternoon relief, water even, pick daily in the flush. Tight-eye habits still apply. The LSU letters do not buy a skip.

## Rust-resistant trees hold leaves later

LSU’s 2015 news is the tradeoff I want on the same page as the names.

Fig rust is the primary disease. Spores live on diseased leaves on the ground, blow into new leaves, cycle. In a wet summer that defoliation-and-regrowth can happen three or four times. New fig varieties tolerate rust better and **hold their leaves longer**.

Trees with rust resistance, Johnson said, present a drawback. Without defoliation they stay green longer into the fall and are **more susceptible to winter cold damage**. Trees that rust lose leaves earlier, harden off more quickly, and that makes them more cold tolerant in his telling.

That is not a reason to hope for rust. It is why a “rust-proof” sales sentence is not a winter sentence. The rust draft is the fungus. This page is why an LSU leaf that stays late is a gift in August and a question in November.

I will not pretend a Baton Rouge finish date is a Little Rock finish date. I will not pretend foliage that stays green is free.

## Tag unverified until fruit

I would rather have one fruited LSU Purple from a known yard than three unfruited “LSU” tags from a swap. The release is a clue. The plate is the proof.

If it sours, you now know that clone in your week. Relabel your expectations. If it holds and you like the taste, it can go in the ground. If you cannot find a true release, do not buy a shrug with LSU in the title.

How I would plant one this spring: morning sun, afternoon relief, mix or hole that drains, label the pot with the **source**, not just LSU. Pick daily in the flush. Write the eye when the first fruit opens. If it holds in a wet week and I like the taste, it can take a hole next spring. If it sours, it stays a pot or it becomes rootstock. That is the same rule as any other name.

LSU did the humid homework. Use it. Then taste. Then decide. A release paper is not a ripe fig. I will not invent the score. Wait for the plate.
