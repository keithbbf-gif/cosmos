University of Arkansas Extension’s comfort line: even if figs freeze to the soil, they often resprout from the roots and may bear that year, because they fruit on **current-season wood**. Texas A&M’s 2015 figs PDF gives the picture: a crowd of shoots, then you keep **five or six** as new trunks, and you thin over **two or three weeks** so you do not strip the leaf factory in one afternoon.

That is the Southern fig after a bad winter. It is a bush that you turn back into a bush on purpose. Zone **8a** South Arkansas still pays this tax. It is not a funeral if the roots lived.

This page is the winter kill to the ground. The late-freeze draft in this set is a different night — buds already moving, then a shot in the 20s. Do not run those two jobs as one panic.

## First: are the roots alive?

Scratch wood. Wait for budbreak. Live cambium is green under the bark. Brown and dry all the way down is a stick.

A **pot** that froze solid may be dead below as well as above. A #3 is a thin can. Roots in plastic see every degree. If nothing moves and the mix smells sour, you have a corpse. Cuttings from that plant should have been taken *before* the freeze if the wood was good. Too late now. Take the label off so you do not water a ghost.

In-ground roots in clay that did not heave have a better chance. Wait. I have watched people dig up a “dead” fig in February that would have moved in April. UAEX is blunt that winter injury is worse in the northern counties. We still wait. North of us waits longer.

A black nursery pot does not get the courtesy of mature wood. The winter-protection page already said pots above about **40°F**, barely moist, not a heated den. If you skipped that and the can froze through, this chapter may be a burial, not a thicket. TAMU’s **17°F** and UAEX’s older **15°F** clip are conversation numbers for wood, not a lab result for your clone in a thin can.

## Wait until budbreak to saw

Dead and live are easier when the buds move. Do not sculpt in January because you are angry at the forecast.

Celeste still does not want a nervous hat-rack if any old wood lived. UAEX: a hard late-winter prune on Celeste greatly limits that season’s fruit. A Turkey type — Brown Turkey / Texas Everbearing — is more forgiving and still tends to bear after freeze or a severe prune. Read the tool-tree draft. If you treat a live Celeste like a dead Turkey, you will delete a crop that might have been there.

If everything above ground is meat, you are in TAMU’s resprout program. Let the shoots come. Do not fertilize the pale spears the week they appear. Those shoots are using stored food. Water. Shade if they are in a pot. Feed when they are a plant, not a spear. The fertigation draft is the same sentence. TAMU said no fertilizer at planting. A freeze year is a kind of replanting. I listen.

I would not strip a thicket in one Saturday to feel useful. You will cook the remaining shoots and then blame July.

## Five or six trunks, not twenty

This is the week I actually run, once the shoots are about **two feet**.

TAMU’s number: keep **five or six** trunks. Thin over **two or three weeks**. I have kept fewer on a tight hole. I have kept a thicket when I was lazy, and then I paid for it with a spray of weak wood and rust in a still interior.

Pick the strong shoots with decent spacing around the hole. Remove the rest in passes. Leave enough leaf that the plant can feed the roots it still has. New trunks have a small effective root system relative to the leaf they will make. July will cook them if you turn a thicket into five bald sticks in one afternoon.

A thicket you never thin becomes a pile of weak stems. Twenty trunks is a hedge you will hate in July. Multi-trunk is how we live with 8a. Own the shape.

You still want **6–8 hours** of sun if you want fruit on those new shoots. A recovered bush in a north cave is still a shade plant. Afternoon shade on a black pot of a survivor is a different sentence. The pot cooked last winter. Do not cook it in July to celebrate.

Mulch the in-ground ring. Pulled off the trunks. Water. UAEX: dry trees drop fruit. Shallow roots did not get wiser because the top died.

## Celeste and Turkey after a kill

If the variety was a tool tree, rebuild. The hole is already paid for.

Celeste: hardier wood going into the freeze, stingier crop if you also chopped what lived, or if the resprout is all you have and the season is late. I am still not a fan of the plate so far. I still rebuild a Celeste that lived in the roots. It is a climate tool.

Brown Turkey / Texas Everbearing: this is the job it was hired for. UAEX said it rebounds and still tends to bear after winter damage. NC State said Brown Turkey and Brunswick make fair-to-good crops on suckers the season after freeze injury. An early tool fig has a better shot at fruit the same year than a late berry name that just lost its head start. Do not rip a late name out in September of a freeze year and call it a variety verdict. Write the year down.

If the variety was a mislabeled dud, this is graft-over season once you have a shoot as thick as a **pencil**. If it was a name you never liked, same. Long trial first — we said that on The Fig Jam. A freeze is not a mood. If the roots are knotted or the hole is a bathtub, do not rebuild. Move. The nematode draft and the clay draft are those holes.

## Year one and year two

**Year one after the kill.** You are growing wood. Main crop on new shoots needs time and heat. Maybe fruit. Maybe leaves. A late freeze plus a late start plus a late variety is a growing-degree-day miss. The GDD draft is the math. This year the clock started when the shoots actually moved, not when the calendar said spring. An early Celeste or Turkey type has a better shot. A late celebrity may give you a canopy. Eat what shows. Do not assume the tree is established because it gave you six figs on a resprout. Water like a first-year hole. The first-two-summers draft in this set is that hose.

**Year two.** The trunks you kept will want to be a thicket. Thin. Do not let twenty weak stems share one hole forever. Spring passes are kinder than a July rage-cut. Those trunks are next year’s prune decision. Celeste still does not want a hat-rack if you wanted fruit. A Turkey type will forgive more. Protect a young recovered trunk the next winter the way you would protect any teenager. Cage, leaves, a wrap you will actually install. North counties especially.

Do not plant a recovered fig in a hole you will not walk to with a hose. If the hose does not reach, the thicket is a wish.

You can take wood when it lignifies — **6–8 inches**, three nodes. You will delay the tree if you strip it for trays. I would rebuild first on a one-tree yard. Frozen-back wood that is still brown and dry is not a cutting. Green suckers are not fridge wood.

## Pots that froze, and labels on a graft

A #3 that sat out can be dead in the roots while the stick still looks brown. Wait for bud. Smell the mix. If it is sour and nothing moves by the time the rest of the block is leafing, you are done. If it moves from the base, pot-up rules still apply — **3-gallon before June** if it is still a cup that lived. Fragile. Shade. Do not celebrate with fertilizer.

Every sucker is the same variety if the tree was one clone. If you had a **graft**, suckers are the **old name**. A freeze year is when people forget and then they have a mixed bush. Paint the kept trunks if you need a reminder. Paint pen. The label draft is the rest. If you graft the resprout later, relabel the day it takes. Suckers below the union are the rootstock trying to take the tree back. Cut them.

A freeze is a reset, not a funeral, if the roots lived. Pick five or six shoots. Grow this year’s wood. Eat what the season allows. The ceremony is a saw in January when you cannot tell live from dead. Skip it. April will show you the green. Then you earn the right to cut.
