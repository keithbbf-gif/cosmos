I have walked a row in late September and pinched fruit that should have been jam. Skin still tight. Neck still stiff. The tree was fine. Leaves were fine. The variety was the problem.

People ask for “the best fig.” That question skips the heat budget. [Pick The Right Fig](https://figroots.com/2025/07/02/pick-the-right-fig-for-you/) already said it in one line: you can have a wonderful fig and not have the growing degree days to finish it. This is that line, stretched out so you can use it.

## What a growing degree day actually is

A growing degree day is not a calendar day. It is a heat unit.

The version most US growers punch into a calculator is **GDD50**: take the day’s high and low, average them, subtract 50°F, and throw out anything below zero. A day that runs 86 / 64 is (86+64)/2 − 50 = 25. A day that stays 48 / 40 is zero. Add those numbers from the date you care about (budbreak, or a spring date you picked) until the fruit is actually ripe.

Some calculators also cap the high at 86°F so a 105° afternoon does not count as magic extra ripening. That cap is a field-crop habit, not a fig law. [VERIFY] whether your favorite calculator is capping, and write it down so you do not compare two different math piles.

Figs do not file a USDA GDD chart the way corn does. Hobby tools such as the [iGrowFigs GDD calculator](https://apps.igrowfigs.com/gdd/) pull station weather and let you set the base. Grower writeups on OurFigs often talk GDD50 and toss around rough bins — breba on the low end, late main crop much higher. Treat those bins as **conversation numbers**, not a lab result. [VERIFY] any specific “this variety needs 2,400” claim against fruit you have actually ripened at your place.

## Why the calendar lies

July 15 in South Arkansas is not July 15 in western Washington. Same date. Different pile of heat.

A late fig can look perfect in a California photo and still be a hard green marble here if you started it late, grew it in a pot that stalled, or the variety is just slow. The inverse happens too. We have heat. Zone **8a** South Arkansas will cook a tray of cuttings if you forget the thermostat. That does not mean every late berry fig will finish after a cool, wet August.

Heat units also reset in a stupid year. A warm February does not help if March freezes the push back to wood. Some GDD tools let you zero the count after a hard freeze. That is closer to how a tree lives than adding January warmth you already lost.

## How we use it (without pretending we invented a model)

I do not keep a lab notebook of degree-days on every pot. I do three practical things.

**1. I ask how late the variety is before I fall in love with the name.**  
If the only notes you can find say “late” or “needs a long season,” that is a warning in the upper South, not a dare. Early and mid-season figs earn their keep here. Late figs go in the trial row, not in the “feed the family” row, until they prove they finish.

**2. I watch the last six weeks, not just the summer average.**  
A fig can sit there looking like a green marble through the quiet middle of the season, then swell and color when the heat and water line up. That quiet stretch is normal. What is not normal is a variety that is still marble-hard when nights are already falling into the 50s.

**3. I do not confuse heat with light.**  
Pick The Right Fig said this too: you can have the GDDs and still get poor fruit in too much shade. In the North, full sun is the default. In the South, we still want morning sun. We just do not fry a black pot on a gravel pad at 2 p.m. and call that “more GDD.” See the shade-and-water draft in this set.

University work on fig phenology uses different bases and °C totals. One Japanese study on ‘Houraishi’ put roughly **2,100 °C-days** on the fruit from budbreak to harvest, independent of node, in that climate. A Turkish study on Bursa Siyahı reported a different effective-temperature stack. Those papers prove temperature sums matter. They do not hand you a plug-and-play number for ‘Red Sicilian’ in Saline County. [VERIFY] before you quote a °C total as if it were your backyard.

## Arkansas / Southeast, specifically

We are not short on summer. We are short on **clean finish**.

August rain plus heat is when fruit sours and splits. A slow variety that wants another four weeks of dry warmth is asking for weather we do not reliably get. University of Arkansas Extension has been blunt for years: common figs only (no wasp types), fruit on current-season wood after winter bite, and souring when water gets in the eye. That is the climate you are buying a variety *into*.

North Arkansas will also steal wood. A late fig that needed last year’s growth for a breba, or needed an early start, will look like a failure when the real issue was dieback. Count heat after the tree is actually growing, not after a date on a seed packet.

Gulf Coast growers have a longer tail. That is a different math problem. Do not copy a south Louisiana finish date onto a ridge in the Ozarks and call it the same fig.

## A simple way to run the number this year

Pick one variety you already know. Write down the date it first softened last year, or this year if you are in season. Run your zip through a GDD50 calculator from a spring start date you will reuse (last frost, or the week you saw budbreak). That total is *your* local number for *that* tree, that year.

Next year, when someone offers you a “must have” late fig, you have a stick to measure with. If their ripening notes sit weeks past your known finisher, you need a reason: a warmer microclimate, a greenhouse, a pot you can shuffle, or you are collecting, not harvesting.

I would rather eat an early fig that is honest than babysit a celebrity variety that dies green. We still trial the celebrities. We just do not pretend the calendar will save them.

## What GDD will not fix

Water swings. A tree that dropped fruit in a dry week did not fail because you miscounted degree-days. UAEX lists drought, storms, and a weak tree as drop causes. Tight eyes and smaller fruit still matter in humidity. Birds still hit the dark ones.

GDD is the heat question. Ask it first, then ask the rest.

If you want the short version we already published: more GDDs means a longer season, and some figs list a number. Use the number as a filter. Prove it on the tree.
