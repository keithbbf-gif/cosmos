I have watched a person wrap the only trunk on a one-tree yard because an air layer “feels safer” than a stick in a cup. Safer is the wrong word. If that wrap sours, you do not have a failed cup. You have a stump.

We already posted the hands: the [air-layer how-to](https://figroots.com/air-layering/) and a [fall timing page for zones 6–9](https://figroots.com/2025/09/18/setting-fall-airlayers-in-zones-6-9/). I am not restaging foil, girdle, or the zone table. This page is the job. When I would peel a ring, and when I would fill a bag with sticks.

## A cutting is a tray. A layer is a plant you can already picture.

The industry cutting we already defined is **6–8 inches and three nodes**. Lignified if you can get it. Fridge if it is dormant. That stick is cheap, mail-able, and on its own from the first minute. Failures have names: rot, callus-only, a dry stick. I wrote those up. The cutting does not get a mother to drink from while it thinks.

I cut when I want volume. Twenty plants. A tray of names. Wood to mail. A mother with more laterals than she needs. A variety I am willing to wait a year to see as a small tree. [Lets Talk About Fig Cuttings](https://figroots.com/2025/12/23/lets-talk-about-fig-cuttings/) is the stick. The prune-day draft in this set is why I do not strip a dessert tree for a grocery bag that will mold in the shop.

I do not cut when the only wood on the plant is the leader I still need as a tree. Then I wait, or I take one side stick and live with a smaller plant.

A cutting is also how figs travel. You can wash it, bag it, and send it. You cannot mail a foil balloon with a live branch still attached. If the job is “get this name into six cups,” the layer is the wrong tool. If the job is “this spare limb should be a #5 by next summer,” the stick is the slow road.

## When I wrap instead

An air layer keeps the branch on the mother. Water and food still come up. You scrape bark and cambium, pack **coco or long-fiber sphagnum at field capacity**, foil it, and you check it. Roots form in the wrap. You cut below the ball and pot it **without shaking the ball to death**. The live how-to is the hands for that. I will not copy the photos here.

Field capacity is the same word as a [Fig Pop](https://figroots.com/fig-pops/): squeeze until it clumps and does not drip. If it drips, you packed a swamp on a branch. I would not pack a layer with a lot of DE or perlite. It dries. Dad likes coco for layers. Sphagnum is second — cost, and those roots can be brittle. The timing page already said that. I pack what I have that holds together wet. I do not pack dry peat dust and hope.

You get a bigger plant faster if it works. You risk a branch. You risk mold in a wet wrap late in the year. That drink from the mother is why layers feel safer. It is also why a bad wrap can still cost wood you wanted.

**I want twenty plants:** cuttings.

**I want this one spare branch to be a tree this year:** layer.

**The wood is green and I am in July:** a layer can still work. A green cutting is a now drill. I would not fridge either. The fridge is for dormant wood. We already said that.

**I am late in the fall on an in-ground tree:** read the zone table we already posted. Maybe do not start. A half-rooted wrap in a freeze is a sad balloon. I would rather take dormant cuttings in December than start a late layer on an in-ground tree in October in a zone that is already cooling. People start layers when they get inspired. Inspiration is a bad calendar.

Pots you can walk into a shop have a longer root window if the space is warm. In-ground trees need an earlier start and an earlier take-off. That is the whole argument of the fall page. I will not rewrite the rows.

## Size of the wrap versus the branch

The live how-to said do not pick a branch bigger than the root ball you will make. A four-foot limb with a tennis-ball wrap is a sail with a postage stamp of roots. I would layer something I can pot in a **#3 or #5** without a crane.

Pencil-thick laterals make cuttings. A wrap is for a plant you can already picture sitting in a #5 next year. If the branch is thicker than the wrap you can actually pack and keep moist, you picked the wrong job. Cut it into 6–8 inch sticks instead.

Do not layer the only trunk. I will say it twice because people do it after watching a video of a mature orchard tree. A one-tree yard should keep that leader. Take a side stick. Live with a smaller plant. A dead leader is a harder story than a failed cup. The mother has to stay a tree.

A spare branch is the test. If you cannot name a branch you would be willing to lose, you are not ready to layer. You are ready to wait, or to take one cutting from wood that is actually extra.

## One Sunday: one wrap, six sticks

This is how I would actually spend the morning. Not a personality. Not a “both methods” speech.

I walk the mother first, in South Arkansas, on a day I can still get my hands around the wood. I am looking for a spare branch — not the leader, not the only scaffold that will hold next year’s crop, something I can picture in a #3. If I cannot find that branch, I put the foil away. The tree stays a tree.

I pack coco to field capacity. Squeeze. No drip. Foil. I write the name on the foil with a paint pen, because a wrap with no name is how a layer becomes “the brown one” in October. Then I leave it. The weekly check is later. Today I am only allowed to start what I will actually tend.

Then I take **six** cuttings: **6–8 inches, three nodes**, lignified if the wood is there. Bag. Date. Source. Six is a tray I will actually stick. A grocery bag of laterals is how you get a Damaged Cuttings photo and a mother that looks hat-racked. If I only need four pops, I take four plus two. The tree keeps the rest.

I check the shop before I quit: do I have a pot and mix ready for the day I cut the layer off? If the answer is no, I am not allowed to be surprised in July. A layer on a tailgate while I look for a bag of mix is how you cook a root ball you spent weeks making. The pot sits empty in the greenhouse with a paint-pen name already on it. That is the other half of the Sunday.

Weekly after that. If it dried, I spray. If it soured, I waited too long or I packed it wet. A wrap you never open is a surprise, and surprises on a mother tree are expensive. I would rather open foil on a Saturday and look foolish than find a black balloon in November.

If the wrap is late and the nights are swinging, I do what the timing page said: keep it attached or overwinter it protected. I do not panic-plant a half-rooted ball into a hole in October because I am tired of looking at foil.

## Potting the ball

Same as a pop, only the ball is bigger and people get rough. Do not strip the sphagnum off to “see roots.” Shade. Water. No hero fertilizer. If you shake the ball, you spent the wrap for a handful of broken white threads.

Have the pot filled before you cut. Slide the ball in. Do not tamp like you are setting a fence post. The first week is shade and a drink, not a tour of the gravel pad at 2 p.m. Black plastic on gravel will cook a new root system as fast as it cooks a #3. Afternoon shade is not optional in 8a.

Both methods clone the tree. Both fail wet. Both fail dry. The layer has a mother for backup until you cut. The cutting is on its own from minute one. I wash cuttings. I still check a layer for mold. Different rituals. Same plant if the name was true.

Use the mother’s drink for a spare branch. Do not spend it on the only trunk you have. I would layer that spare and cut six sticks from the rest. I would not layer a leader to feel advanced. The tree that is still a tree in March is the one I wanted.
