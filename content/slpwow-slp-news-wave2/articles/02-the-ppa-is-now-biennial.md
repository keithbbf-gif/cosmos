# ASHA’s Public Policy Agenda is now a two-year document

*Educational news brief for SLPWOW. Not legal advice, not billing advice, and not a substitute for reading the primary source or for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

The news is almost boring, which is why it is easy to miss. In 2025 the Government Affairs and Public Policy Board voted to write the Public Policy Agenda every other year instead of every year. The 2025–2026 agenda is meant to cover the 119th Congress. ASHA says so on the advocacy-priorities page, in plain bureaucratic English.

That is a calendar change. It is not a new Medicare benefit. It is not a compact privilege. It is not a reason to rewrite a clinic’s prior-authorization script.

## What the agenda is for

ASHA describes the Public Policy Agenda as the list of what members said matters most, and as a framework for staff to write actual objectives. The published 2025–2026 text is short on statutory citations and long on verbs: protect payment, expand and permanently cover telehealth, keep the full professional scope in the room, and operationalize the interstate compact.

Those verbs are instructions to lobbyists and to members who send messages to Congress. They are not instructions to a MAC. CMS still publishes the Physician Fee Schedule. State boards still publish compact onboarding dates. A school district still writes its own Medicaid claiming manual.

## Why the two-year cycle matters

Congress already works on a two-year clock. An annual PPA that reset every January was fighting the House and Senate calendar. A biennial PPA can stay on one set of bills — telehealth permanence, payment updates, compact implementation — without pretending that a new PDF in January invented a new profession.

The cost of the change is freshness. A 2025–2026 document will still be the official agenda in late 2026 even if a conversion factor, a compact map, or a telehealth sunset has moved. That is why this desk treats the PPA as a **priority list**, then sends you to the later CMS and compact briefs for the numbers.

## How to read the 2025–2026 PDF without getting sold

Open the priorities page, then the PDF. Notice three buckets that keep returning: government payers (Medicare, Medicaid, Tricare) and private insurers; tools for billing and coding; and federal and state lobbying. The second bucket is the quiet one. ASHA is telling members it will keep writing fee-schedule booklets and coding FAQs. Those booklets are readings. They are useful. They are not the Federal Register.

Notice also what the agenda does not do. It does not set the KX threshold. It does not list which four jurisdictions were issuing compact privileges in September 2026. It does not diagnose late talkers. If a slide deck treats the PPA as if it were all of those things, the slide deck is the problem.

## What this is not

This is not a scorecard of wins. Congress extending Medicare telehealth authority through 31 December 2027 (see the later brief) is a result that sits *beside* the agenda, not inside it. The agenda asked for permanence. The statute gave a two-year extension. Those are different sentences. Keep them in different boxes.

If you need to brief a staff meeting, say: ASHA will lobby the same three themes for two years; check CMS and the compact site for what actually changed this month.
