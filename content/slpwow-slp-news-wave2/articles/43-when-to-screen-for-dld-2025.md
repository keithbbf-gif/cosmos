# A 2025 review put the earliest decent DLD screen around age two-and-a-half

*Educational news brief for SLPWOW. Not legal advice, not billing advice, and not a substitute for reading the primary source or for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

Sansavini and colleagues’ 2025 review in *Frontiers in Pediatrics* asked a scheduling question: when do screening tools for developmental language disorder actually work? The paper uses CATALISE’s DLD, even when the older studies said SLI. It reminds readers that prevalence estimates bounce because cutoffs bounce, and it cites the familiar ballpark of about **7.6 percent** of 4- to 5-year-olds in one often-quoted sample.

The age story is the news. At and below **2 years**, sensitivity is not good enough to trust a screen as a true-impairment finder. Around **2.5 years**, some tools start to show predictive accuracy in the 70–80 percent sensitivity-and-specificity neighborhood; the authors like multidomain instruments (they mention ESSENCE-Q) more than a single vocabulary tally. At **3**, concurrent validity sometimes looks fine and predictive validity is still thin. By **4**, persistent DLD has usually declared itself enough that a diagnosis can be confirmed with less shame.

The authors’ plain sentence: **2.5 years is the earliest age at which DLD screening tools in their review showed adequate predictive power.**

Caregiver concern at 18 months is still a reason to look, to check hearing, to watch gesture and comprehension. CATALISE already said severe problems should not wait for a pretty ROC curve. A failed 2.5-year screen is not a diagnosis. A passed one is not a promise.

CDC’s surveillance checklists are doing a different job at different well-child visits. Do not average them with this review and call it a protocol.

## How a clinic might change a flow without becoming a factory

If your “language screen” is a 24-month vocabulary list you treat as destiny, this paper is a rebuke. If you have no structured look until kindergarten, it is a different rebuke. The middle path is: surveillance always, a better screen near 30 months when worry or risk is present, an evaluation when the screen or the life says so.

This brief will not name a commercial kit as the winner. The review is about timing and about how messy cutoffs remain.

Public-health people will want a universal 30-month language screen because 2.5 sounded like a number. The paper is more modest than that. It says tools start to have a chance then, not that every kindergarten should be redesigned around one questionnaire. Pair it with hearing surveillance and with CATALISE’s warning that severe cases should not wait for a pretty curve.

If your pediatric office still uses a 24-month word-count as a pass/fail gate, retire the gate. Keep the word-count as a conversation starter. That is the whole operational change this review can honestly support without becoming a product.
