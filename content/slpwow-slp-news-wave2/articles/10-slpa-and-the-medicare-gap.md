# Assistants can be licensed at home and still be invisible to Medicare

*Educational news brief for SLPWOW. Not legal advice, not billing advice, and not a substitute for reading the primary source or for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

Physical therapist assistants and occupational therapy assistants have a Medicare story: recognized personnel, a payment differential, a supervision argument that never ends. Speech-language pathology assistants have a colder sentence. CMS’s Benefit Policy Manual, in the transmittal ASHA keeps pointing at, says services of speech-language pathology assistants are **not recognized for Medicare coverage**. If they are billed as therapy, they are treated as unskilled and denied as not reasonable and necessary — even when the assistant is licensed in the state. Aides are not therapy services either.

ASHA’s Medicare FAQ for SLPs repeats that paragraph almost as a courtesy, then adds the private-plan coda: check the payer; many plans copy Medicare.

That is the news that refuses to get old. Every hiring season someone announces that “Medicare finally covers SLPAs.” Wave 2 checked the public FAQ and the manual language again in September 2026. The refusal was still there.

## Why the PTA analogy fails

The 85 percent assistant differential that clinics quote is a PTA/OTA rule. It is not a hidden SLPA rule. There is no parallel modifier that lets you bill 92507 at a reduced rate for an assistant’s minute. The correct operational reading is harsher: do not put an SLPA on a Medicare claim as the skilled provider. If an assistant helps with unskilled tasks, those minutes are not the therapy service.

State practice acts can still create SLPAs, set supervision ratios, and let schools or private clinics use them. Medicaid and commercial plans can choose to recognize them. Those are other payers. Do not launder a state license into a Medicare benefit.

## What ASHA is and is not doing

ASHA trains and affiliates assistants. ASHA’s PPA talks about workforce and access. None of that rewrites 42 CFR 410.62 or the manual paragraph on support personnel. If ASHA later wins a coverage change, that will be a CMS or statutory brief of its own. Until a transmittal says otherwise, the FAQ is the public line.

Clinical fellows and other provisional licensees are a different fight — ASHA has warned that CMS’s qualified-personnel language can snag people who are not yet fully licensed. Do not collapse CF and SLPA into one “assistants” slide. One is a path to the SLP benefit. The other is outside it.

## How to write the hiring memo

“We may hire an SLPA where state law and non-Medicare payers allow. We will not bill Medicare for SLPA-furnished therapy. Supervision plans follow the state board, not a social-media ratio. Families of Medicare beneficiaries should expect the SLP on the skilled visit.”

If a consultant offers a workaround that “everyone uses,” ask for the manual citation. This desk did not find one.
