# A compact privilege is equivalent to a license. It is not the CCC.

*Educational news brief for SLPWOW. Not legal advice, not billing advice, and not a substitute for reading the primary source or for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

The Commission’s definition is short. A privilege to practice is granted through compact legislation, obtained in CompactConnect, based on a home-state license, and is the authorization to practice in another member state. The home page says that privilege is **equivalent to a license**.

ASHA’s CCC-SLP is a certificate issued by CFCC. It requires a degree, a CAA path, an exam, a fellowship, and maintenance hours. States often use it as a shortcut in their own license rules. Medicare and Medicaid personnel regulations often orbit it. It is still not a license, and it is not a privilege.

## Three cards, three jobs

1. **Home-state license** — the compact’s foundation. Active, unencumbered, in a member state that has actually onboarded.
2. **Privilege** — the remote state’s compact authorization, with that state’s fee and maybe that state’s jurisprudence exam.
3. **CCC** — the private credential that employers, payers, and some statutes still ask for.

Lose the home license and the privilege theory collapses. Lose the CCC and you may lose a job or a payer even if the privilege is intact. Hold only the CCC and you cannot compact-practice in Ohio.

## “Equivalent” does not mean “identical paperwork”

Equivalent-to-a-license means the remote state treats you as authorized to practice. It does not mean you skip that state’s jurisprudence quiz if the fee sheet says the quiz is required. Tennessee’s 2026 row required jurisprudence and listed a $150 state fee on top of the Commission’s $50. Ohio’s row said jurisprudence was not required and listed $25 plus $50. Read the row.

It also does not mean you may ignore the remote practice act. If Tennessee forbids a procedure your home state allows, you are in Tennessee when the patient is.

## How to explain it to a family

“I am licensed in my home state and I hold a privilege that lets me treat you in yours. I also hold a national professional certificate. Those are different cards.” If that sounds fussy, it is because the last decade of travel therapy marketed “compact” as a sticker.

Employers who require the CCC *and* a privilege are not being redundant. They are stacking a private quality signal on a public authorization. You can argue about whether the stack is fair. You cannot argue that they are the same card. Payers who still list “CCC or equivalent” in a manual are talking about personnel qualifications, not about CompactConnect.

If your CCC lapses and your privilege does not, you may still be legal in the remote state and unemployable in the building that wrote CCC into the contract. Fix the certificate or change the building. Do not yell at the Commission.
