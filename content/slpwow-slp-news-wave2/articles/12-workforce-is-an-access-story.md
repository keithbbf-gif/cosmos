# ASHA’s workforce line is an access story, not a caseload number

*Educational news brief for SLPWOW. Not legal advice, not billing advice, and not a substitute for reading the primary source or for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

The 2025–2026 Public Policy Agenda’s workforce paragraph is the one that sounds like a news lede. Services are valuable. Need is rising. Members are overburdened. Communities go without. Then the agenda does something more specific than a sigh. It asks ASHA and members to defend licensure as a public-protection tool across delivery methods, including telehealth; to hold education and training standards; and to **operationalize** the Audiology and Speech-Language Pathology Interstate Compact.

That last verb is the policy. The first sentences are the atmosphere.

## Shortage headlines are not a scope waiver

When a district or a home-health agency cannot hire, the temptation is to stretch supervision, to bill an assistant as if Medicare had blinked, or to let a compact rumor replace a license. The agenda’s own structure argues the other way. Licensure standards exist because the people in the chairs can be harmed. A compact privilege is supposed to be equivalent to a license in a member state, not a shortcut around competence.

This desk’s compact briefs will keep repeating the September 2026 fact: 37 jurisdictions enacted; four issued privileges. “Operationalize” still had a lot of map left. A workforce crisis that treats the compact as already national is lying about access.

## What ASHA did not publish in that paragraph

A national caseload cap. A school-to-medical FTE formula. A promise that SLPAs will be Medicare providers. A wage number. Those fights live in other ASHA pages, union contracts, and state education rules. If you need workload, go find the workload documents. Do not footnote the PPA as if it set 55 students.

## How to use the paragraph anyway

It is a civic sentence you can hand to a legislator without pretending it is data. Pair it with something countable: vacancy months, miles to the next hospital SLP, compact issuing status in your state, Medicaid school-based claiming that still cannot find a qualified provider under 42 CFR 440.110. The agenda gives you the frame. You still owe the count.

Families reading this magazine should hear a plainer version. There are not enough clinicians in some counties. That is why telehealth extensions and compact privileges exist as *attempts*. They are not a diagnosis of your child, and they are not permission for an unqualified person to take the slot.

## Three numbers that beat a vibe

If you have to brief a school board or a hospital C-suite, take three counts you can defend: unfilled FTE months, miles to the next outpatient SLP who takes the child’s payer, and whether your state is issuing compact privileges or only has a signed bill. Those three numbers will outlast any “workforce crisis” slide. They also keep you honest when the crisis is real in one county and invented as a reason to skip supervision in another.

ASHA can lobby. CMS can pay. The compact can open a door. None of those machines hires the person. The PPA paragraph is allowed to be atmospheric. Your memo is not.
