# Targeted manual medical review survived the 2026 rule

*Educational news brief for SLPWOW. Not legal advice, not billing advice, and not a substitute for reading the primary source or for evaluation by a licensed clinician.*

The KX modifier is the doorbell. Targeted manual medical review is the conversation that sometimes follows. ASHA’s 2026 fee-schedule analysis lists “targeted manual medical review” among the policies that did **not** go away when conversion factors split. CMS’s Therapy Services page still talks about review authority, including failures to use the KX modifier correctly.

This is not a new program. It is a reminder that 2026’s payment headlines did not repeal medical-necessity culture.

## What “targeted” is supposed to mean

After the hard cap died, Congress and CMS kept a way to look at high-spend therapy claims without pretending every dollar above $2,480 is fraud. Review is supposed to be aimed — high outliers, odd patterns, missing modifiers — not a blanket punishment for every stroke patient who still cannot swallow safely in month four. In practice, a clinic learns it is a target when additional documentation requests arrive. The defense is the chart you already should have written: skilled service, goals, why this intensity, why still now.

Do not confuse review with prior authorization. Medicare Advantage plans may want a PA. Traditional Part B targeted review is usually retrospective. Different beast, same need for a sentence a stranger can follow.

## How not to scare a family

Do not tell a beneficiary that “Medicare will investigate you if we keep going.” The review is of the claim and the record, not of the person’s right to have a swallow. Do tell them that coverage still depends on medical necessity, and that a plateau without a new skilled question is how therapy ends even when the KX is on the line.

## What this desk will not do

We will not publish a template progress note. We will not guess your MAC’s favorite phrases. We will say: if you add KX, you are asserting the record is ready. If you are not ready, fix the record before the modifier.

Review letters often ask for the plan of care, the certifying physician’s order if your setting needs one, and a stretch of notes that show skill rather than attendance. If you cannot find the order in a week, that is the finding. If every note is a clone, that is the finding. Software that autocompletes “patient continues to benefit” is not an ally here.

A clinic that has never been reviewed is not a clinic that is safe. It is a clinic that has not been drawn. Keep the chart as if the draw already happened. That is cheaper than a consultant after the letter.

If review finds a problem, fix the process, not just the one chart they named. Reviewers can count. A single pretty note in a stack of clones is not camouflage. It is a contrast study.
