I say that before I say a number, because people shop
thickness as a look. The eased edge casts a shadow. The
photograph wants 1.5 inches or 2.25 or a laminated 4-inch
apron that is lying about the panel. The structure does
not care about the photograph. A 1.5-inch hard-maple edge-
grain panel is a good residential lid. It will take a
modest seating fly if we help it. A 1.25-inch oak panel
from a home center is a lid that already used its margin
in the truck.

Shop numbers, labeled as shop numbers:

- 1.5 inch edge-grain maple: everyday Bradley spec for an
  oiled work top. Overhang 8 to 10 inches before I start
  talking about brackets or a thicker panel.
- 1.75 to 2 inch edge grain: happier at 10 to 12 inches
  of fly, still not a stone-style 15.
- 2.25 inch and up, especially end grain: the block
  conversation. Weight becomes a box conversation.
- Anything under 1.25 on an island: I treat it as
  furniture veneer over a substrate until proven otherwise.

Stone shops fly farther because the slab is stiff and
because they put steel in the fly. Wood is lighter and
it bends. Thickness is one way to buy stiffness. A
corbel, a steel angle, a second rail — those are others.
I will not sell a thin panel and a row of stools as a
package without one of those others.

Aprons confuse the issue. A 1-inch panel with a 3-inch
built-up edge looks like a block from the stool and
behaves like a 1-inch panel in the field. Fine for a
filmed hosting top. Dishonest for a cutting top that
will be planed someday. If we are going to resurface,
we need meat.

Bradley boxes can carry thick lids. Tell the box. A
surprise 2.5-inch walnut glue-up on clips meant for a
thinner maple panel is how you oval the screw holes and
then blame the wood for moving. The wood did move. The
spec was shy.
