Effingham, Illinois, 1887: a blacksmith’s sycamore cookie
becomes a meat-market tool, and a family name gets welded to
maple.

That is the lore John Boos & Company tells, and I tell it as
lore. Conrad Boos cut a cross-section of sycamore, put legs
under it for the smithy, and a butcher wanted the same object
for meat. The son copied the cookie. Adelbert Gravenhorst
bought in a few years later and put capital under the name.
By 1900 a block had gone to Paris for a fair. The early
product was industrial — butcher shops, mills, anywhere steel
met wood. The slogan they used was about that meeting, not
about a breakfast bar.

The material shifted as the century did. A single trunk cookie
cracks. It is also only as wide as the tree. Laminated northern
hard maple — sugar maple, *Acer saccharum* — became the shop
answer. It is hard enough (Forest Products Laboratory puts
side hardness around 1,450 lbf on the Janka test at 12 percent
moisture). It glues. It has little of the tannin and odor that
make oak and walnut more complicated next to raw meat. When
commercial listings later named maple for food-contact boards,
they were naming a habit the Midwest shops already had.

I am not writing an advertisement for Effingham. I am writing
why every serious American block conversation still starts
with maple. Michigan shops, Ohio shops, the same species, the
same reason. The South had gum and sycamore and oak, and used
them. The national word still meant maple because the national
shops shipped maple.

Plastic and stainless tried to take the grocery block after
the war. A lot of them did. Wood got accused of being dirty.
The shops that stayed in wood either added steel tables or
waited the accusation out. Residential gourmet stores — the
Williams-Sonoma story starts in the late 1950s — put small
maple boards in houses that had never seen a meat market.
The 1970s put the same glue-up on a counter run. The island
came later and borrowed the word again.

When a Bradley dealer says butcher block, I translate: do you
mean Midwest maple, shop thickness, oil — or do you mean a
pretty glue-up from whatever species the mill had? Both can
be good. Only one is the object the Midwest spent a century
making. Say which.
