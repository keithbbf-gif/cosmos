Hard maple became the shop wood because it glued, it cut
clean, and it did not argue with food.

*Acer saccharum* — sugar maple, hard maple, the “rock maple”
of the Midwest catalogs — is pale when it is fresh, tight
when it is quartersawn, and hard enough that a Janka ball
leaves a smaller dent than it will in walnut or cherry.
The Forest Products Laboratory puts that hardness near
1,450 lbf at 12 percent moisture. Shrinkage is not shy
(tangential movement is the one that cups a careless
panel), but the species is predictable if you dry it and
glue it like you mean it.

Shops liked the glue. Maple takes a glue line and keeps
it. Shops liked the smell, which is to say they liked the
absence of one. Oak tannin and walnut’s character are
lovely in a dining room and more of a conversation next
to raw protein. Maple does not perfume a chicken. That
sounds small until you have owned a board that does.

Maple stains if you let acid sit. Lemon, vinegar, a wet
tea bag — the pale field will take a shadow. Maple ambers
in the sun. Clients who want “white forever” should buy
painted boxes and a stone lid, not a maple top and a
speech about how it will stay gallery-white. It will not.

Soft maple is a different citizen. It is easier to dent,
easier to machine, cheaper on a quote, and a disappointment
if you paid for hard. I write hard maple on the spec. I
do not write maple and hope.

On a Bradley island, maple is the species I oil for work.
I film it only when the household has already admitted they
will use a board. A filmed maple top is a pale table. An
oiled maple top is the closest residential object to the
Midwest shop block. Both can sit on the same box. They
should not share a care card.

I keep a sample that has seen a year of kitchen light next
to a sample from the rack. I put both on the island during
the meeting. The conversation gets shorter.
