Kids will treat an oiled top like a desk, a studio, and
a drum. Plan for that or film it.

I like kids at an island. I also like telling the truth
about markers, glue, science fair salt, a dropped can
opener, and the drum solo on the seating fly. Oiled maple
will take a lot of that and sand back. It will also take
a permanent purple if the marker was the good kind. Film
will wipe the marker and show the gouge. Stone will wipe
both and wreck the science-fair vinegar if it is marble.

If the island is the homework station, I want a finish
that matches homework, or a mat that gets put down
without a fight. If the island is the baking station,
oil and a trivet and a kid who is allowed to make a
dent. Those are different houses. The same Bradley box
can serve both. The lid cannot pretend to be both
without a conversation.

Height matters. A 36-inch lid is a stretch for a small
person and an invitation to kneel on the stool. Knees
on wood are dents. I do not moralize. I spec thickness
and I spec a stool that does not become a ladder without
adults.

Food safety with kids is habit: wash after raw meat,
before cookies. A separate small board for chicken is
peace in a house that already has enough arguments. The
island can stay the cookie field.

Bradley sold family islands. The photographs had fruit,
not homework. This draft is the homework. If a dealer
is talking to a household with three kids under ten, I
want that fact on the intake, not as a surprise when
the purple marker calls. We can still sell oiled maple.
We sell it as a surface that will look used, which is
how you know the room is a kitchen, or we sell film
and a maple board in the drawer. Both are love. Only
one is a showroom at year five.

I also ask about pets. A wet dog chin on the seating
fly is a water ring with fur in it. Same wash, same
dry, same oil. The lid does not care whether the guest
had two legs or four. The care card can skip the joke
and keep the habit.
