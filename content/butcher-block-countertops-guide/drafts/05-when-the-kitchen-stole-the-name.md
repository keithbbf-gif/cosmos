Sometime after the war, the kitchen stole a shop word and put
it on a breakfast bar.

The theft was gradual, which is how thefts that become language
usually work. First the small board in the gourmet shop, maple,
oiled, honest. Then the laminated run along a wall in a 1970s
remodel, often oak, often already under a film, still called
butcher block because the glue lines showed. Then the island,
which needed a name that sounded like work while the household
wanted a place to sit and plug in a mixer.

By the time catalogs needed a thumbnail label, butcher block
meant “wood strips glued together.” Orientation dropped out.
Thickness dropped out. Finish dropped out. A face-grain walnut
table top and a three-inch maple end-grain block shared a
search term. That is convenient for a website. It is a mess
in a shop.

I do not blame homeowners. I blame the sentence that let a
stain schedule do the work of a spec. If the glue lines are
visible, the listing says butcher block. If the glue lines
are hidden under a cathedral grain, the listing says rustic
wood. Same mill, different photograph.

The island made the confusion expensive. A wall-run counter
can hide a cup against a backsplash. An island is a lid with
four weather sides and people leaning on two of them. A thin
oak “block” that would have survived over cabinets will dish
and split when it is a table, a cutting board, and a bar.
The name did not warn anyone.

Bradley sold into that language because every retailer we
worked with already used it. Hayneedle, Overstock, the big
boxes — they needed a filter. We shipped islands with wood
tops and let the filter say butcher block. This series is
the correction we owe the next drawing. Call the object by
the contract: oiled maple edge grain, or filmed walnut plank,
or end-grain block. Let the search term be a nickname, not
the spec.

If a designer sends a finish board labeled butcher block and
nothing else, I send it back. Not to be difficult. To keep
the word from bouncing a 1.25-inch oak panel into a job that
wanted a tool.
