If you want a working wood top in a house, you almost always
want edge grain.

Boards stand on edge. The long lines run the length of the
island. You see a field of stripes, not cathedrals, not a
checkerboard. Thickness in a decent shop is 1.25 to 2.5
inches. Alternate the growth rings when you glue so the
panel does not decide to become a trough. That last sentence
is shop talk the fabricator already knows. What you need to
know is that a cheap top glued in a hurry will cup, and that
cupping on an island is a lid that rocks glasses.

Edge grain is the default because it balances three jobs.
It is stable enough for a furniture box. It is hard enough
in maple and oak to take a knife if the finish allows. It
is handsome without looking like you ordered a trophy. End
grain is the trophy, and the tool. Face grain is the table.

I spec edge grain on most Bradley islands that want wood.
I write the strip width. Narrow strips look busier and can
hide more color variation. Wider strips look calmer and
show more of each board’s mood. Neither is more “authentic.”
Authenticity is a word I leave at the door with the sample
tags.

Overhang likes edge grain if the strips run the long way
and the fly is modest. The panel is a series of beams. It
will still bend if you ask a 1.5-inch top to fly 15 inches
without help. Thickness helps. Brackets help more. Pride
does not help.

Finish on edge grain can be oil or film. Oil if they will
cut. Film if they will host. The grain orientation does not
pick the finish. The household does.

When a drawing says wood top and the 3D view shows wide
planks with cathedrals, that is not edge grain. I mark the
drawing. Bradley purchase orders call the orientation by
name. “Butcher block” does not appear as a spec line. It
appears in this series as history and as a nickname. The
mill gets edge grain, 1.5 inch, hard maple, strips under
1.75 inch, oiled, eased edge. That is a top a shop can
build twice the same way.
