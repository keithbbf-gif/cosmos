Long tops want seams or they want a shop that can handle
an eight-foot glue-up without a banana.

A laminated panel has a length limit that is about the
beds, the clamps, and the truck, not about desire. Past
that limit you are asking a glue-up to stay flat across
a span that weather will work every season. I would
rather have a planned seam on a rail than a single field
that arrives already smiling.

Seams on wood are not stone seams. You cannot hope a
polyester knife will make them disappear and stay gone.
You can align strips, you can ease a tiny chamfer, you
can put the line over structure, and you can accept a
hairline in January. Clients who need an invisible eight
feet should buy stone or buy a shop that does architectural
veneer and then not cut on it.

I avoid a seam in the primary cutting zone. I avoid a
seam at a sink cut. I like a seam at a material change,
which is a hybrid wearing an honest face.

Transport is part of length. A top that barely fit in
the shop will not enjoy a stair, a turn, and a family
dog. Two shorter lids can be a better day. The island
can still read as one piece if the box and the edge
profile agree.

Bradley long islands — the dining-length pieces, the
ones people wanted to use as a table — are where this
draft earns its keep. We did not ship a banana and call
it rustic. We either had the clamp bed or we had a seam.
If a current mill says they can do ten feet in maple
edge grain, I want to see the last ten-foot they did,
in winter, after it lived in a house. Photographs in the
shop are not winter.

Write the length as a shop length, not a dream length.
The aisle and the stools will also vote, and they often
vote shorter.

If we must run long, I want stickers and a meter at
both ends of the panel before we clip. A banana that
shows up after the drive is a banana you do not force
flat with screws. You let it talk for a day in the
room, then you decide whether it is weather or a mill
problem. Forcing a long top is how you invent the
split this series already warned you about.
