End grain has a reputation it only half earned.

The true shop block stood the fibers up so a knife slipped
between them instead of severing them. That part is real. A
cleaver likes end grain. A honing steel likes the way the
surface gives and closes. When you resurface a tired end-grain
block you are taking a little off a thousand straws, not
slicing a long ribbon of earlywood and latewood the way you
do on a plank.

The mythology is that end grain is therefore the only honest
butcher block, and that a house which buys edge grain has
been sold a lie. Edge grain is how most residential tops
that will actually be used get built. It is stable enough,
handsome enough, and a fraction of the labor. End grain is
heavier, thirstier at the end-grain face, and priced like
the glue-up it is. I spec it when someone will break down
a chicken on the island and oil it like a tool. I do not
spec it because a mood board liked the checkerboard.

History here is useful. The cookie was end grain because the
tree was end grain. Lamination let shops keep the orientation
without the crack. It also let them flip the strips on edge
and make a different object that still showed glue lines.
Both objects went to market as blocks. Only one behaves like
the meat-market tool.

End grain drinks finish. That is why commercial blocks get
oiled on a schedule that looks obsessive to a residential
client. If you film an end-grain top, you have spent the
money and then forbidden the knife. I have seen it. It
photographs like a chessboard under glass.

On a Bradley island, end grain is a special order in the
honest sense: we are building a tool lid for a furniture
box. The box must take the weight. The fasteners must let
the panel move. The care card must say oil, not “wipe with
a damp cloth” as if it were quartz. If any of those three
is missing, I sell edge grain and keep the friendship.
