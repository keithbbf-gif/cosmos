Knives belong on oiled wood. Hot pans belong on a trivet.
Those two sentences prevent half the phone calls.

A knife on oiled maple or oak is the job the material
wanted. The edge lasts longer than it does on stone or
on glass. The top takes a scar and can be sanded. That is
the contract. A knife on filmed wood is a scratch in a
jacket. A knife on walnut is allowed if the household
likes dents; I still hand them a maple board if they
shave paper-thin onions every night.

Heat is the other contract. Wood does not want a 400-degree
skillet. It will brand. It will shock a finish. It can
open a glue line. Soapstone and many granites shrug.
Quartz is more nervous than its brochure. Wood needs a
pad, a trivet, or a stone insert if the island is where
the pans land. I do not care how “seasoned” the cast iron
is. The iron is hotter than the story.

People ask about a hot pot “just for a second.” A second
is how brands get their clean edges. If the household will
not keep a trivet on the island, I put a stone landing in
the design or I change the species conversation into a
stone conversation. I would rather lose the wood sale than
win it and inherit the brand.

Bradley mixed-use islands — wood where you cut, something
else where you set the pot — are the grown-up version of
this draft. A small marble or steel insert looks like a
fuss in the drawing and looks like wisdom on a Tuesday.
A cheap silicone trivet looks like nothing and works.

Sharpening on the island is a habit I discourage. A steel
is fine. A stone will dish a spot and leave slurry. Take
the stone to a board you do not love.

If a brand happens, let the top cool. Sand with the grain.
Oil. The scar will recede. It will not vanish on the first
pass. A household that needs vanish wants stone, and I
will say so without making them feel small. The trivet is
cheaper than the feeling.
