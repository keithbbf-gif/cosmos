Food-safe is a sentence about the finish you actually
maintain, not a halo printed on a label.

A clean, oiled maple top that is washed, dried, and not
used as a raw-chicken parking lot is a normal American
kitchen surface. A filthy filmed “block” with black sink
rims is not made safe by the word maple. Plastic boards
with a thousand wet scars are not made safe by being
plastic. Dean Cliver’s early-1990s work at UC Davis
pushed back on the panic that wood was inherently dirtier
than polymer. Bacteria inoculated on wood were often hard
to recover; knife-scarred plastic held on to them. That
work mattered. It did not issue a residential sacrament.

NSF/ANSI 2 listings are a commercial equipment story.
Hard maple shows up there because shops built boards to a
standard and paid for the listing. Your Bradley island is
not “NSF certified” because we used maple. I will not let
a dealer put that sentence on a hangtag. It is a lie that
gets repeated until it sounds like heritage.

What I tell a household:

- Wash with warm water and a little soap. Rinse. Dry now,
  not later.
- After raw meat, wash like you mean it. A dilute bleach
  rinse (the old shop line is about a teaspoon to a quart)
  or a vinegar wipe is a habit, not a personality.
- Do not soak. Do not dishwasher.
- Oil when pale.
- Use a separate small board for raw poultry if the
  household’s nerves need it. Pride is not a health code.

I do not sell antimicrobial as a feature. Some factory
oils and some studies talk about hydrophobic faces and
counts that drop over hours. Fine for a paper. The
kitchen still has to wash the onion off. If a product
needs a science paragraph to get through dinner, I would
rather sell the habit.

Bradley boards were sold next to islands for a reason:
the island can be furniture, the board can be the tool,
and food-safe becomes a drawer you can put through the
wash. That split is honest. A single oiled island that
the household will actually clean is also honest. A halo
on a hangtag is not.
