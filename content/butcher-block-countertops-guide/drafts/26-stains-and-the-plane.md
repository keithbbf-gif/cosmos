A stain is not a divorce. A plane, or 180 grit with the
grain, is usually the whole argument.

Oiled wood is repairable in a way stone is not. That is
the feature people forget when they are staring at a beet
ring. You are allowed to remove a little surface. You are
allowed to oil it back to the family of the rest of the
field. You are not allowed to belt-sand an island in the
room and then act shocked at the dust in the soft-close
drawers.

My order: wash, dry, decide whether the mark is oil-deep
or fiber-deep. Oil-deep gets oil and time. Fiber-deep gets
sanding by hand, with the grain, a progression if you need
it, then oil the spot and a wider halo. If the field has
gone patchy, sand the whole working zone so you are not
chasing islands of sheen.

A plane is the older tool. A sharp block plane will take
a whisper off a maple edge-grain top and leave a surface
that oil loves. It will also take a whisper off your
knuckle if you are rusty. I do not hand a plane to a
client who wanted quartz last week. I hand them a sanding
block and a phone number.

End grain sands differently. You are working the ends of
fibers. Be patient. Keep the block flat. A dish in a
checkerboard is a dish you will see in raking light for
a year.

Film finishes do not get the plane. They get a finisher,
or they get a living-with-it conversation. Do not plane a
conversion varnish. You will make a map of raw wood in a
plastic county.

Bradley tops that were specified as oiled should be
specified as repairable. That word belongs on the dealer
sheet. If the household lights up when I say repairable,
they are wood people. If they go still, we are in the
wrong finish or the wrong material. I would rather find
that out while I still have a pencil.
