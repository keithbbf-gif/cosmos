Winter heat in a tight house will pull a wood top faster
than a teenager with a wet glass.

The furnace runs. Indoor relative humidity drops into
numbers that make a guitar buzz and a maple panel think
about getting narrower. If the fasteners were screws in
round holes, the middle holds and the end splits, or a
glue line opens over the dishwasher because that edge
was already tired. If the underside was unfinished, the
panel cups. If the top sat under a vent, you get a pale
stripe that looks like a path.

What I do in January: look at the gaps before I look at
the glue. Measure indoor humidity if anyone has a meter.
Oil the field. Do not drive another screw. Do not fill
a seasonal hairline with epoxy unless you like a ridge
in July. A little wax in a stable cosmetic line is a
choice. A lot of filler in a moving line is a future
chip.

What I do in the spec, before winter: clips, finished
underside, acclimation, a species that matches the
destination, a conversation about humidifiers that is
not a lecture. Warren humidity is not Denver humidity.
I have said that already. I will say it every fall.

A split that is a real split — a run through a strip,
not a glue line — is a mill conversation. Sometimes a
butterfly is honest. Sometimes a replacement strip is
honest. Sometimes living with a tight hairline is
honest. A filler stick from the hardware store is
rarely honest on an island people will photograph.

Bradley lids that went north and west taught this draft.
The ones that stayed in the county had an easier winter
and a wetter August. The care card can mention both
seasons. Most cards mention “avoid moisture” and stop,
which is how a household over-dries a top with a space
heater and then calls it a defect.

If the split scares them, I show a picture of an old
shop block. Those cookies cracked and still worked. An
island is furniture, so we are kinder to the look. We
are not required to panic. Panic is how you get six
new screws and a worse split.
