A dishwasher is a steam cabinet. A soak is a warp machine.
Wood wants neither.

This is the shortest argument in the series and the one
that still gets a top killed. A household puts a small
board in the dishwasher “just this once,” or parks a
cutting lid in a full sink because the party was large.
The glue lines drink. The panel cups. The finish — oil or
film — lets go in patches. Then the internet says wood is
fussy.

Wash in the sink or on the island: warm water, a little
soap, a cloth or a soft brush, rinse, dry with a towel,
stand it or leave it in the airflow. Now. Not after the
show. Edges and the underside lip get a wipe if they got
wet. A standing island top cannot be stood on edge; it
can be dried like a table, thoroughly, including the
sink cut if there is one.

I do not use the dishwasher as a sanitizer for wood. The
heat and the detergent are a finish-stripping climate.
If the household needs a board that can go through the
machine, they need a polymer board for that job and a
wood board for the other job. Two boards is cheaper than
one ruined lid.

Steam from a dishwasher door that opens under a wood
overhang is a quieter version of the same attack. Every
night the same edge gets a climate it did not sign up
for. Detail the overhang, or film that edge, or move the
machine. I have seen a perfect maple field with one black
stripe over the dishwasher. The stripe was the schedule.

Bradley small boards — the 8 by 13 bread boards, the 13
by 16, the carving sizes — are the objects that get
soaked. Write it on the wrap: hand wash, dry, oil. The
island top gets the same three words in a larger font.
If a client laughs, I wait. If they still laugh, I sell
film or stone and keep the boards in the drawer where
the rule is easier to keep.
