I will sell end grain to a person who already owns a steel,
a boning knife, and a calendar that includes oil.

That is not a personality test. It is a use test. End grain
is a thousand drinking straws stood on end and glued. Knives
like it. Water likes it too, which is why you oil the face
and the edges and you never let a puddle commute overnight
from the sink. The surface will go pale in rectangles where
a cutting board used to live, if you stop using the whole
field. That pale is thirst, not death.

Construction is slower. You are gluing a mosaic, then
surfacing a mosaic, then finishing every open pore. Thickness
often wants 2.25 inches and up if the island is going to
take real work. Weight goes up with the cube of your
ambition. The Bradley box must be told. Rails, corner
blocks, the way the top clips on — all of it changes when
the lid is a block instead of a 1.5-inch panel.

I do not put a sink in end grain if I can help it. I do
not put a cooktop in it. I do not put a 14-inch seating
overhang on it without a support story that would embarrass
a stone shop. End grain is for the middle of the work, not
for the bar.

People ask about bacteria because they have read a paragraph
about maple and science. Dean Cliver’s early-1990s work made
wood look better than the plastic scare had claimed. Later
marketing turned that into a halo. I will say this once:
an oiled, cleaned end-grain block is a normal kitchen tool.
A wet, scarred, un-oiled end-grain block is a wet scarred
tool. The orientation does not forgive the habit.

On a Bradley island I treat end grain as a shop option, not
a default upgrade. The upgrade language is how you sell a
checkerboard to a household that wanted a photograph. If
they earn it, we build it, we write the oil on the card,
and we show them the pale test: when the top looks light
and dry, it is asking. When they stop seeing the ask, they
call us, and we sand and start the habit again.
