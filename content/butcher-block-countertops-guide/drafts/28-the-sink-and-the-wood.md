A sink in a wood top is a detailing problem, not a species
problem.

I have oiled sink rims for people I trust. I have also
refused a sink in walnut for a client who already told me
they travel three weeks a month. Standing water is the
enemy. The rim is where standing water lives. If the
cutout is proud, water runs onto the wood. If the cutout
is shy, water runs under the rim and stays. Either way
the glue line nearest the bowl is the first to speak.

Detail like you mean it: a rim that sheds, a finish that
can take a nightly wipe (often film at the rim even when
the field is oil — a hybrid that must be designed, not
improvised), a household that will dry the join before
bed. Undermount looks clean and is unforgiving. A slight
reveal can be kinder if the wood is finished through the
cut. Bare end grain in a sink hole is a straw in a glass
of water.

I still prefer the sink off the wood. Stone at the wet
end, wood at the work end, a seam we planned. That is
the grown-up island. A single maple field with a stainless
bowl in the middle is a pretty drawing and a care card
that has to be read every night.

Dishwashers, ice makers, and pot fillers are sink cousins.
They throw steam and splash at a height the old butcher
never designed for. If they live in the island, the top
material at that station should be the one that likes
water.

Bradley custom sheets have had sinks in wood. They also
had clients who wrote long spec notes. That correlation
is not an accident. If the dealer wants a sink in the
wood to hit a photograph, I want the household in the
meeting, not just the photograph. I will draw the hybrid.
I will also draw the refuse line: no sink in oiled walnut
for a house that does not wipe.

The care card, if we do it: dry the rim tonight. Not
when you think of it. Tonight.
