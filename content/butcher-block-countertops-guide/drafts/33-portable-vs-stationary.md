Casters turn an island into a cart. That is a feature until
someone parks it in the aisle and calls it a kitchen.

Bradley sold portable and stationary versions of the same
boxes. The SKU tails had a P. The weight did not change
as much as people hoped. A hardwood island with a 1.5-inch
top is still a piece you move on purpose, not a piece you
nudge with a hip while the soup is boiling. Casters that
lock, that can take the load, that do not mark the floor —
those are the hardware conversation. Pretty wheels are how
you get a drift toward the fridge.

A wood top on a portable island sees a different climate
than a wood top that never leaves its footprint. You roll
it to the back door for a party. You roll it under a vent.
You roll it into a patch of sun. The panel has to be
finished on all faces and fastened for movement, because
you have just invented a traveling humidity cabinet.

I like portable when the room is honestly too small for a
stationary island and the household will roll the box to
the wall on weekdays. I dislike portable when it is an
excuse to skip the aisle math. The tape still matters.
A cart in the work triangle is a cart.

The top spec does not change just because the box has
wheels. People try to go thinner “because it has to move.”
That is how you get a lid that cups and a cart that still
weighs too much. Keep the thickness. Keep the clips. Keep
the care. Add locks.

Electrical is the other tell. A portable island with a
cord is a tripping essay. A portable island with no power
is honest. If they need a mixer every day, we are back
to stationary and a circuit, or a wall outlet that the
cord can reach without crossing the aisle. I will not
invent a pop-up on a rolling box and call it clever.

Warren shipped both. The dealer sheet should say which,
and the care card should say lock the wheels before you
chop. A moving block is a cartoon. A locked cart can
still be a tool.
