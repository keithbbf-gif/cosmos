Walnut, cherry, oak, and ash will all sit on a Bradley
island. They will not all live the same life.

Walnut is the one people point at. Chocolate, expensive,
softer than maple — Forest Products Laboratory hardness is
in the neighborhood of 1,010 lbf — and gorgeous next to
painted doors. It dents. It is a seating-and-serving wood
more than a daily chopping wood, unless the household is
gentle or proud of dents. I like walnut under a film when
the island is furniture. I like it oiled when the client
already talks like a wood person and owns a plane or a
sanding block.

Cherry is a heartbreak wood. Beautiful, softer still (the
handbook’s Janka for black cherry sits under walnut), and
it will go red-brown in the sun while the underside of the
fruit bowl stays pale. I use it when the client wants that
story. I do not use it when they want control. An oiled
cherry island in a bay window is a tan line with drawers
under it.

White oak is the current friend of the modern kitchen. Ray
fleck if you quartersaw it, tough, a little more movement
than maple, tannin that will darken under some oils and
under wet iron. It pairs with the oak floors everyone
already has. It can look muddy if you stain it on top of
an already-warm floor. Samples in the room, not in the
showroom. Red oak is the coarser cousin; the old Bradley
board catalogs used it and warned that engraving would
look striated. That warning still holds.

Ash is the pale, ring-porous wood we paired with walnut
in the two-tone tops. It works. It takes a dent. Emerald
ash borer made supply a conversation; I do not pretend
the yard is as casual as it was in 2010. When we have
ash, I treat it as a working pale next to walnut’s dark,
not as a maple substitute for a cutting field.

Janka is a ball test, not a kitchen test. It tells you
about dents, not about water, heat, or a household. I
read it, then I ask the three questions from the woods
draft in the island guide and ask them again here: will
anyone cut without a board; is there a sink; do you want
year-one color or year-ten color. The species is the
answer to those questions, not the other way around.
