At Bradley we have sold wood tops that were blocks, and wood tops
that were furniture, and we have watched catalogs call both of
them butcher block.

A Bradley island is a piece of furniture with a working lid. The
box is full-access when we build it that way: drawers that actually
open, a rail that can take weight, a furniture back people will
lean on with jeans. The lid is a choice. Maple edge grain, oiled,
thick enough to plane. Walnut face grain under a film, because
the household hosts and will not oil. A two-tone of walnut and
ash that the old sheets called a conversation. A gum-wood glue-up
that a northern showroom would have stained and renamed.

What we do not mean: a printed laminate with a wood photograph.
A 3/4-inch pine butcher-block-look from a home center, already
cupping in the shrink wrap. A stained oak plank sealed in amber
plastic and sold as a cutting surface. Those objects exist. They
are not this guide, and they are not what we owe a dealer who
has to stand in the aisle with a tape.

The shop sits in Warren, in Bradley County, because the timber
was here. River names went on the boxes — Moro, Caney Creek,
Cossatot, Saline, Little Red, Shiloh — the way other factories
put numbers on SKUs. Some of those islands wore wood tops you
could cut on. Some wore wood tops you should not. The name on
the box did not settle the contract. The grain, the thickness,
and the finish did.

I will use butcher block in this series the way a shop uses it:
laminated hardwood, usually maple, edge or end grain, finished
so a knife is allowed. When I mean a furniture top, I will say
wood countertop, or filmed walnut, or plank. Words still mean
things, even in a showroom that has been selling “butcher block
islands” since the catalogs learned the phrase.

If you are quoting a client, write the top as six facts: species,
grain orientation, thickness, finish, how it is fastened, and
whether a sink or a cooktop lives in it. “Wood top” is how you
get a fight when the photographs go around the family chat.
“Oiled 1.5-inch hard-maple edge grain, Z-clipped, no sink” is
how you sleep.
