Commercial habits — scrape, salt, oil, don’t soak — still
work in a house that wants a tool.

The meat-market block was not maintained with a lifestyle
blog. It was scraped, sometimes salted, washed, dried,
oiled. The scrape is a bench scraper, not a steel wool
hobby. You pull the wet work off the field. You do not
carve a new valley. Salt is an old drying and scrubbing
trick; I use it on a small board more than on an island,
because a salty island is a salty island.

Houses are not shops. We do not break down quarters of
beef on a Bradley lid. We do cut a roast, smash garlic,
and park a hot pan we were not supposed to park. The
habits scale. Scrape. Wash. Dry. Oil. Trivet. Board for
the job that scares you.

What does not scale: the industrial sanitizer, the nightly
belt sander, the idea that a residential top should look
like a grocery block in 1952. That block was ugly in a
way a kitchen island is not allowed to be. We can take
the discipline and leave the gore.

I steal one more shop habit: the same cloth does not do
the floor and the lid. The same sponge does not do the
raw chicken plate and the cookie field unless it has been
through a wash that you trust. That is not precious. That
is how you keep the halo-talk out of the series and still
eat.

Bradley’s commercial cousins — the Midwest maple shops,
the NSF-listed boards — published care pages because they
got the same phone calls we did. Read them. Do not copy
them onto a hangtag as if our island were their listed
board. Steal the habits. Leave the certification.

If a household wants to live like a shop, I will spec
end grain and a thick lid and a scraper in the drawer.
If they want to live like a house, I will spec edge
grain and a board. Both are allowed. The hybrid of shop
mythology and house laziness is the one that fails.
