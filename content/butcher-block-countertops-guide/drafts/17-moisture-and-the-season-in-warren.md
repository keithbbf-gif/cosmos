Wood in Warren in August is not the same wood you ship to
Denver in January.

That is the whole moisture lesson, and most top failures
are a refusal to believe it. A panel glued at a shop
moisture content that matched a humid southern summer will
shrink when it meets a tight house with a furnace and a
humidifier that nobody filled. The field wants to get
narrower. The fasteners, if they were screws into slotted
nothing, hold the middle and split the end. Or the glue
lines open because the mill was the last place the wood
was allowed to move.

The Forest Products Laboratory handbook is blunt about
this: wood moves most in the tangential direction, less
radially, almost not at all along the fiber. An edge-grain
top is a stack of those directions. You cannot stop it. You
can dry the stock to a range that matches the destination,
you can finish all faces, and you can fasten it so the
field can slide.

I ask where the island will live before I lock a thickness
and a finish. A coastal house with windows open is one
climate. A high-altitude winter house with the heat on 70
and the indoor relative humidity in the twenties is another.
Warren in August is a third. We have shipped into all
three. The care card changes. The fastener story changes.
Sometimes the species changes, because a thirsty gum panel
in a dry house is a meaner citizen than a hard-maple panel
that was dried for that house.

Acclimation is not a myth and it is not a week on a porch.
The top wants to sit in the room, on stickers, away from
the vent, until a meter and a calendar agree. Then we clip
it. Clients who want the island “done by Saturday” get a
conversation about Saturday versus February.

Oil does not waterproof a top. It slows the exchange and
feeds the pale. A film slows it more and forbids the knife.
Neither wins against a puddle or a 20-point swing in
relative humidity. The household’s thermostat is part of
the spec. I write it down when they will tell me. When they
will not, I assume winter heat and fasten accordingly.
