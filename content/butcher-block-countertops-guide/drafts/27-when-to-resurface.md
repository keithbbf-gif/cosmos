Resurface when the hollow bothers the knife, not when the
top stops looking like the day it left Warren.

A shop block was turned down for decades until it was too
thin to trust. A residential island top has less meat. A
1.5-inch edge-grain panel can give you a few honest
resurfings if you are taking thousandths, not eighths. A
built-up apron with a thin field cannot. That is why I
argued about thickness as structure. The plane needs
something to eat.

Signs I trust: the knife rocks in a dish; water runs to
a low spot and sits; a glue line is proud because the
wood around it wore. Signs I ignore: it is not showroom
pale; it has knife marks; it looks like a kitchen. Knife
marks on an oiled top are the point.

Resurfacing in the house is possible on a small board and
miserable on an island that cannot go to the shop. Dust,
the clip pattern, the walls, the stools — all of it is in
the way. I would rather pull the lid, send it to the mill
that glued it, and clip it back. That is another reason
the fasteners have to be serviceable.

After a real resurfacing, the top is thirsty. Oil it like
year one. The household always forgets that part and then
calls about pale patches. The patches are new wood.

I do not resurface a filmed top to “turn it into oil.”
You can, if you sand through the jacket on every face and
accept that you are starting over. Most people want a
spot repair and an oil rag. That is how you get a bald
spot. Commit to the new contract or keep the old one.

Bradley lids that were built as tools should have a
sentence on the card: when the hollow bothers the work,
call the shop, do not belt-sand the kitchen. The farm
table that never left was allowed to go thin. The island
in the middle of a room still has to look like furniture
from the stool side. Leave some meat.
