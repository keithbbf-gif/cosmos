Face grain is a table pretending it got a job in the kitchen.

You see the wide face of the board, the cathedrals, the
knots if the grade allows them. It is the look people mean
when they say farm table and then point at the island. It
is prettier than edge grain to a lot of eyes. It is also
softer in the wear path, more willing to move, and a worse
place to put a knife. Earlywood valleys dish. A film helps
the wipe and forbids the cleaver. Oil will keep it alive
and still not make it a block.

I will spec face grain on a Bradley island that is furniture:
walnut under a conversion varnish, cherry that the client
already knows will tan, oak that matches a table they
inherited. I will not spec it as butcher block. If the
dealer needs a filter word for the website, that is their
problem. The purchase order says plank, species, thickness,
finish.

Movement is the quiet issue. A wide face-grain panel is a
row of boards trying to become wedges as the seasons turn.
Breadboard ends look like the answer and often become the
next problem if they are glued tight. I prefer a well-made
panel, a finish that slows the exchange, and fasteners that
let the field slide. I prefer not to run face grain eight
feet without a shop that has already done eight feet.

Cutting on face grain is how you get a valley in year three
that collects onion juice. If the household will cut, we
are in the wrong orientation or we are handing them a board
from the drawer and admitting the island is a table. Both
are fine. Pretending is not.

Bradley custom work has used plank looks — a walnut leaf
on a gum or oak box, a two-tone that reads as furniture.
Those tops earned their keep as lids people sat at. They
did not earn a cleaver. I would rather say that in the
showroom than in a warranty argument about a scar that was
always invited.
