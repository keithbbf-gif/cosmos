Oil is not a finish you apply once. It is a habit you keep
or you don’t.

Food-grade mineral oil — the USP kind, not the salad
dressing, not the can of “lemon oil” that is mostly solvent
and perfume — goes into the fibers, slows the pale, and
does not go rancid on the shelf the way a walnut oil or a
homemade walnut-and-beeswax experiment will. Commercial
block shops have sold their own oils for a century because
households will otherwise use whatever is near the stove.

The schedule is not a religion. Year one drinks. A new
top in a dry house may want oil every few weeks until it
stops looking thirsty the day after you wipe it. Then
monthly is a common shop line. Then, when the household
is using it and wiping it, you oil when the color goes
light and the water no longer beads for a moment. The top
will tell you if you look. If you do not look, the top
will tell the guest who sets a wet glass down and leaves
a ring that does not buff out.

How: warm room, clean dry top, cloth, thin coat, all faces
you can reach, including the edges and the underside lip
if you can get there. Let it sit. Wipe the puddles. Do
not make a sticky lake and call it extra protection. Extra
is just dust glue.

I do not oil a filmed top. I do not oil a top that still
has standing water. I do not oil over chicken fat and call
it conditioning. Wash, dry, then oil.

Bradley oiled boards left the shop with a first coat and
a sentence. The island top should leave with the same
sentence, written, taped inside the trash pull: the name
of the oil, the pale test, the refusal of cooking oil.
If the dealer wants to sell a branded cream, fine. If the
household wants a pharmacy mineral oil, fine. If they want
olive oil because it is in the house, I will say no until
they are tired of hearing it. Rancid is a smell you cannot
market away.
