Wax is the coat you put on after the oil has gone in, not
instead of it.

Beeswax mixed into mineral oil — the board creams the
commercial shops sell, or a simple shop mix — sits nearer
the surface. It slows a splash. It gives a soft sheen. It
is not a varnish and it is not a raincoat. People who wax
instead of oiling a thirsty top get a smeary pale that
still drinks through the holidays.

Order of operations: clean, dry, oil until the face looks
fed, wipe, then a thin cream if you want the extra skin.
Let it sit. Buff. If your cloth loads up with paste, you
used too much. The island is not a car.

I like wax on an oiled Bradley top that sees Saturday
baking and a fair amount of wet fruit. I skip it on a top
that is about to be cut on all afternoon, because the wax
will just become the first thing the knife moves. I skip
it on a filmed top, where it is a smear on a jacket.

Wax will show fingerprints and it will show a bad buff.
That is not a defect in the wood. That is the cream telling
you to use less. A household that hates that conversation
wants film, or stone, or a harder oil they will not admit
is a finish.

Do not put furniture paste wax full of solvents on a
cutting lid because it was under the sink. Do not put
candle wax on a scratch. Do not put crayon on a kid dent
and call it a folk repair unless you like explaining
crayon to a dinner guest.

The care card can say: oil when pale, cream if you want,
never cooking oil, never the garage can. That is enough
wax theology for a kitchen. The rest is catalog poetry.

One more shop tell: wax on the underside lip is wasted
motion, and wax on a raw end-grain sink cut is a smear
over a straw. Put the cream on the working face and the
eased edge people touch. Leave the hidden faces to oil,
which still feeds. If a household wants a single product,
mineral oil alone will keep a Bradley lid alive. Cream
is the extra skin for the people who already oil.
