Year one drinks oil. Year three sips. If you skip year
one, year three looks like a neglected porch.

A new top has just been surfaced. The fibers are open.
The house is a new climate. Forced air, a sunny bay, a
holiday party with wet glasses — the panel is learning
the room and losing moisture at the same time. Oil in
that year is not vanity. It is food for a lid that is
still settling its mind.

I tell households to look weekly for the first two
months, then monthly. Pale and dry means now. A dark,
sated field that still beads a drop can wait. There is
no prize for a sticky surplus. There is a prize for
not forgetting until Thanksgiving, when the stains and
the thirst arrive together.

Winter is the exam. If the top opens a hairline in
January, oil will not close a movement gap, but it will
keep the new wood in the gap from going further pale.
Humidify the house if you can do it without growing a
science project on the windows. Do not park the island
under a blast of heat.

By the end of year one the schedule usually calms down.
The household either has the habit or they have decided
wood was a personality they do not have. That is useful
information. We can film a top later if they want to
change contracts. We cannot un-hate a year of neglect
with one Saturday and a blog.

Bradley boards in a drawer follow a kinder year-one
because they can be oiled on a towel and stood up. The
island cannot. Build the habit into the room: a cloth
and a bottle in the same pull as the card. If the oil
lives in the garage, the island will live like a garage
project.

I do not sell year one as a honeymoon. I sell it as
apprenticeship. After that the lid is just part of the
kitchen, which is the whole point of putting wood in
the middle of the room.
