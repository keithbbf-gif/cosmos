Delivery day is when you teach the household the contract,
or they invent one.

I do this standing at the island, not in an email they
will lose. I put a hand on the lid. I say what it is —
oiled maple edge grain, or filmed walnut, or a hybrid —
in one sentence. I show the pale test if it is oil: a
drop of water, a moment of bead, the color when it is
thirsty. I show one clip so they know the lid is allowed
to move. I show the rim if there is a sink. I put the
card in the trash pull while they watch, because a card
in a drawer of manuals is a card that dies.

I say the three refusals: no dishwasher, no soak, no
cooking oil. I say the trivet sentence. I say the lemon
sentence if the wood is pale. I do not say enjoy your
new butcher block. I say this is the lid. This is how
it lives.

If the top acclimated in the room, I say so. If it needs
another day before the final clip, I say so and I do not
let the party start on a floating panel. If a corner
took a ding in the stair, we mark it now, oil it or
fill it now, and do not discover it in a review.

Kids and partners who were not in the design meeting
need the short version. I wait until they are in the
room if I can. The person who wipes the top on Thursday
is the spec.

Bradley deliveries used to include a board in the same
order — a gum bread board, a walnut-ash, a carving size.
I like that still. The island can be the furniture. The
board can take the chicken. On delivery I say which
object is which. If they only bought the island, I still
say where a board should live.

I leave a date. Year-one oil is a calendar, not a vibe.
If they want a call in October, they can have a call in
October. I would rather a call than a pale top and a
bad review that thinks wood failed. Wood did not fail.
The contract was never spoken out loud.
