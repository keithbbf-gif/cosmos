The can that says tung, or Danish, or “food safe once
cured,” is often a wiping varnish wearing work clothes.

Real tung oil exists. It polymerizes. It is slow. It is
not what most hardware-store “tung oil finishes” are. Those
are varnish, thinner, and a story. Danish oil is a family
of wiping varnishes. Some are honest about the resin. Some
are not. Hardwax oils are a newer shop citizen: oil in the
wood, wax and a binder on the face. They can be good. They
are still a product, not a folklore.

Why this matters on a Bradley top: the care card has to
match the chemistry. If we oiled with mineral oil, a later
coat of wiping varnish will sit on a greasy face and make
a smear, or it will stick in patches and look like a
disease. If we filmed the top at the shop, a later coat of
mineral oil is a dust magnet that does not feed the wood.
The contracts do not stack.

Boiled linseed oil is the other lie. The boiling is a
story; the metallic driers are the point. I do not put
that on a cutting surface and then say food-safe with a
straight face. Raw linseed is slower and still not my
residential recommendation next to a salad. Mineral oil
is boring. Boring is a virtue on a lid people eat from.

“Food safe once cured” is a sentence about a film that
has finished off-gassing, not a blessing you can apply to
any can. I want the product name on the spec. I want the
household to buy that product again, or to call us before
they improvise.

If a client loves a hardwax oil and will maintain it, I
will spec it on an oak or maple mixed-use island and write
the recoat window. If a client wants a can from the garage
because their uncle used it on a gunstock, I will walk
them back to mineral oil or to a film we choose on purpose.

The Midwest commercial shops have their branded oils and
creams. Use them if you like the shop. Use pharmacy mineral
oil if you like the pharmacy. Do not mix a mystery can
with a mystery top and then tell me the species failed.
The species did what the can told it to do.
