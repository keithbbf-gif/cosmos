An island top is a lid on a piece of furniture. A counter
run is a field glued to a wall.

That difference is why a lot of “butcher block counters”
fail when they become islands. A run has a backsplash to
hide a cup. It has cabinets under most of its area. It
has two short weather sides. An island has four weather
sides, people leaning on two of them, a dishwasher steam
on one, a seating fly on another, and a box that is
trying to be furniture. The same 1.25-inch oak glue-up
that lived over a dishwasher run will dish and split when
it is asked to be a table, a bar, and a cutting board.

I spec island lids thicker, or better supported, or both.
I finish the underside and the edges like they will be
seen, because they will. I fasten for movement in two
directions, not one. I think about the aisle before I
think about the overhang, because an island that wins the
photograph and loses the aisle is a piece of furniture
in the way.

Bradley boxes are the furniture. The lid should look like
it belongs on that furniture, not like a remnant from a
wall run. Matching a perimeter quartz run with a wood
island is a good split: stone where the wet work already
lives, wood where the island can be the tool or the
table. Matching a thin wood run with a thin wood island
is how you get two problems.

Seams are more honest on an island if you planned them.
A run can hide a seam at a sink. An island seam is a
feature. Put it on a rail. Do not put it in the cutting
field unless the mill can make the line boring.

When a designer copies a wall-run spec onto a Bradley
island, I send it back with this draft’s first sentence
at the top. Not to be precious. To keep a lid from being
asked to do a wall’s job in the middle of a room.
