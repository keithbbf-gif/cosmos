The night you leave a lemon cut-side down is the night the
maple learns a new color.

Acid stains pale woods. It is not a moral failure and it
is not a species failure. It is chemistry plus time plus
a household that went to bed. Wine, tomato, beet, a wet
tea bag, a florist’s packet leaking on the hosting side —
same story, different pigment. Walnut hides more. Cherry
will confuse you because it was already changing color in
the sun. Maple tells the truth in a circle.

What to do in the morning: wash, dry, look. If the mark
is in the oil, a fresh oil and a little time may even it.
If the mark is in the fiber, sand with the grain — 180 is
a common start — and oil the spot plus a wider field so
you do not leave a pale patch that looks like a repair.
If the mark is under a film, you are in refinisher
territory. Do not sand through a conversion varnish on a
Saturday and then oil the raw crater. You have made a
bald spot in a jacket.

Prevention is a board, or a habit of not using the island
as a drying rack for citrus. I say that without heat. I
have left the lemon. I have sanded the circle. The care
card can mention acid because the first stain is when
people decide wood was a mistake. It was not a mistake.
It was a night.

Bleach will lighten some stains and will also lighten the
wood around them. Use it as a wash, not as a bleach-the-
island project. Oxalic acid is a furniture-restorer’s
trick for iron and water marks; it is not a Tuesday
product. If you do not already own it, do not discover it
on a Bradley top without calling someone who has.

The hosting side of a mixed island is where the lemon
lives. That is an argument for stone on that side, or for
film, or for a household that owns coasters and uses them.
I would rather move the citrus than move the species.
Maple that has been stained and sanded and oiled still
looks like a kitchen. Maple that has been nagged into
regret looks like a showroom that lost.
