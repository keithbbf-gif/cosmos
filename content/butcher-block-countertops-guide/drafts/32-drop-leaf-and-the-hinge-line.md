A drop leaf is a hinge line, a support story, and a place
people will sit even when you told them not to.

Bradley built leaves. The old custom sheet for a 48 by 24
oak island with a 10-inch leaf is still in the files: 3-inch
radius on the outer corners, walnut and heart gum on the
field, Cossatot-style flush face, towel racks on the ends.
That leaf was a table when it was up and a thinner island
when it was down. The hinge line had to be straight. The
support had to take a guest. The wood had to match across
a joint that opens and closes.

I treat a leaf as a second top. Same species if you want
the match. Same finish. Fasteners and hinges that can be
serviced. A support that is not a wish — a swing arm, a
gateleg, a pull-out rail that actually locks. A leaf that
droops is a shrug in furniture form.

People will sit at a leaf. If the leaf is 10 inches, they
will sit badly. If we promised seating, we size the fly
and the support for seating, not for a place to park
mail. If we did not promise seating, we say so on the
card so the next owner does not add stools and a lawsuit
of the social kind.

Grain across a hinge is a tell. Edge-grain strips that
do not align when the leaf is up will bother the person
who paid for wood. Face-grain cathedrals that break at
the hinge look like two tables. I dry-fit. I mark. I do
not hope.

A leaf on an oiled top needs oil on the underside and on
the edge that kisses the hinge. That edge collects crumbs
and gets skipped. Then it looks like a tan line. The care
card can mention the hinge. Most cards do not. Mine does.

If a dealer wants a leaf because the photograph of Sandy
Creek or Pebble Creek had one, I want the support in the
quote. A leaf without a support line is a price that will
come back.
