Glue failure on a laminated top is not a mystery. It is a
shop that rushed the clamp, or a house that flooded the rim.

A butcher-block panel is a lot of glue lines doing the job
of a single board that never existed. The glue has to wet
the wood, stay under pressure, and then live with water,
heat, and a knife. Modern PVA and the better waterproof
formulas will do that if the mill lets them. Starved joints,
cold shops, and “it looked closed” are how you get a line
that opens in year two and collects onion juice.

I look at the lines on a sample. They should be hairlines,
not glue beads, not gaps you can catch a fingernail in.
A checkerboard end-grain top has more line than an edge-
grain top. That is more chance and more labor. I pay for
the labor or I do not buy the checkerboard.

Water is the house’s contribution to failure. A slow drip
from a florist vase, a sink rim that never gets dried, a
dishwasher steam that hits the same edge every night — the
glue line opens and then the argument starts about warranty.
Warranty language is not in this series. Habit is. Dry the
rim. Do not park a wet bag of ice on the same rectangle for
a week.

Heat is the other contribution. A hot pan left to “just
rest” will not always leave a brand, but it can shock a
finish and a glue line at the same time. Trivets are not
décor.

On a Bradley island I want a mill that will still answer
in year three. That sentence is the whole vendor test. A
top that was the cheapest glue-up on a container is a top
I will not clip to a box we built. We can make a lid in
Warren or we can buy a lid from a shop I can call. What I
will not do is mix a furniture box with an anonymous panel
and then write a care card that pretends they were born
together.

If a line opens, stop oiling it like a face and start
treating it like a joint: dry, assess, call the shop that
glued it. More oil will not close a starved glue line. It
will make a shiny crack.
