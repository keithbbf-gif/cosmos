Screw a wood top down like a slab of stone and you will
teach it to split.

Stone wants to sit. Wood wants to walk a little, every
season, for the life of the house. The fastener’s job is
to keep the lid from sliding off the box when someone leans
on the breakfast side, and to let the field get wider and
narrower without asking the screws to be the winter. Figure-
eights, Z-clips, wood buttons in slotted holes, a tight
fastener at the centerline and slop toward the ends — those
are the usual citizens. A grid of long screws through the
rails into the top is the usual mistake.

I pick the hardware for the grain and the span. A 1.5-inch
edge-grain maple panel on a Bradley box can live on Z-clips
around the perimeter and a couple of locating screws in
the middle third. A heavy end-grain block wants more
locations and a box that was built for the weight. A face-
grain walnut plank wants slop in the direction the boards
will move, which is across their width.

Do not glue the top to the box “for stiffness.” You have
just made the box responsible for a movement it cannot
do. The exception is a small, filmed furniture top on a
base that was designed as one piece, and even then I want
a shop that has already done that piece.

Access matters. Clips you can reach after the island is
in the room are clips you can service. Clips buried behind
a finished furniture back are a future floor pull. Full-
access Bradley boxes make this easier. A cheap box with a
false back makes it a curse.

On delivery I show the homeowner one clip. I say this lid
is allowed to move. If a gap appears at a breadboard or
at an end in January, we look at humidity before we look
at glue. If a screw head buries itself and a split starts,
we stop using that hole. The top is not a deck. It does
not want another screw next to the failed one.

Write the fastener on the spec sheet next to the species.
“Wood top, attached” is how you get a stone installer with
a impact driver and a warranty conversation I already lost.
