I have stood in the Warren shop and watched a top get
scraped that a catalog would have called rustic and
thrown away.

That is the note I want in this series. Bradley Brand
Furniture is a hardwood shop in a lumber county. The
address on the old invoices is 501 Pennington. The
phones were the 226 exchange. The work was islands and
boards and the occasional bench, sold through people
who stood on other people’s floors. Some of those people
were national websites. Some were a store in De Queen
with a truck.

The shop sense that matters for a butcher-block lid is
not the romance of 1903. It is whether the glue-up was
watched, whether the species was the species on the
ticket, whether a two-tone was a pair of woods that
move alike, whether a leaf was supported. Catalogs lie
in the direction of glow. Shops lie less when you are
in the room.

We engraved boards. We sold unfinished islands because
a household wanted to stain them next to a door they
already had. We sold mocha and onyx and alpine white
on boxes that then wore a natural wood lid, which is
a good split if you say it. A painted box and an oiled
maple top tells a guest where the work is.

I will not write a factory tour as a sales page. I will
write this: if you want a Bradley wood top, you want a
shop that still thinks like Warren — measure twice, name
the wood, fasten for February, hand over a card. If the
current build is in another building with another crew,
the thinking can travel. The thinking is the product.
The river name is the nickname.

When a dealer asks for “the Bradley look,” I ask which
year and which lid. The Moro was a small island. The
Caney was a different box. The Cossatot liked a two-tone
top. Those are memories. A new lid should be specified
as if the memories were a museum, not a warehouse.
