The southern board — sycamore, gum, the shop’s “Arkansas
mahogany” — is how Warren talked when northern maple was a
freight charge.

Sycamore is in the origin lore of the Midwest cookie, and
it is in our board list for a different reason: fine texture,
takes a laser, pale enough that engraving shows. It is not
as hard as sugar maple. The handbook puts American sycamore
well below maple on the Janka ball. I like it for a bread
board and for a gift board. I am slower to make it the
whole island lid unless the household is kind.

Sweetgum — heart gum, the red-brown heart, the sapwood that
does not match — is the wood a lot of southern kitchens
already lived with without naming it. It machines. It
moves. The heart is pretty next to walnut, which is why
the custom sheets said walnut/heart gum when a client
wanted a two-tone that was not a catalog cliché. I oil
gum if we are making a board. I think twice before I oil
a large gum island in a dry winter house.

Arkansas mahogany, in our old four-fold, was described as
strong, relatively light, tight and fine, similar to maple
with more color, good to engrave. I will not pin a Latin
name on a shop nickname in a staged draft and then have to
unwrite it. It was the local pale-brown that took a knife
and a laser and did not need to come from Wisconsin. Treat
it as a named grade from that shop, confirm the current
yard, and sample it in the room.

These woods are why a Bradley butcher-block conversation is
not only a maple conversation. The Midwest shop taught the
country a word. The Arkansas shop taught a county a set of
boards. When I spec an island top today I still reach for
hard maple first if the job is cutting. I reach for these
when the job is a board, a gift, a two-tone, or a match to
a piece already in the house.

Do not let a retailer rename gum as mahogany and maple as
butcher block in the same sentence. You will have two lies
and one top. Write the species. Write the orientation.
Write the finish. Let the nicknames live in the history
drafts where they belong.
