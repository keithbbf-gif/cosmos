There is no furniture row to walk as a working row. There is a museum, a journal, a school name, a 1912 table, a court opinion, a tornado path, a High Point showroom that still says 1946, a folding shop on North 3rd if the listing still holds, a lectern shop on a mountain if the listing still holds, a hospitality shop in Monticello if the listing still holds, mattresses in a reused appliance bay if the 2019 paper still holds, and a set of small hardwood shops in counties that used to sell stock.

That list will be wrong in places by the time an editor publishes it. Good. A present-tense factory sentence that cannot go stale is a lie. This essay is a check, not a crown.

## What “still” is allowed to mean

Still means: objects that are furniture or furniture’s cousins, made in Arkansas, leaving on a truck, in the decade this essay was written. It does not mean a hub. It does not mean one of three. It does not mean Ballman’s cooperative. It does not mean ten thousand wagons. Those were other stills.

Harris and Maxwell’s 15,373,000 board feet are not coming back as a civic program. Red gum and white oak were ninety-five percent of that table. 85.44 percent grown in Arkansas. Jebaraj’s 31,000 manufacturing jobs were not dressers and they thinned anyway — 6,500 gone by 2007, 7,500 more after 2008. What comes back, when it comes back, is a niche: a kitchen that wants a named woods and a work height, a church that wants a pew repaired, a hotel that wants a dresser on a short lead, a rental hall that wants a stack of chairs.

Girard’s two dozen doors are a memory. Higgins’s peak window is a memory. The *Journal*’s ranking of furniture as the city’s largest late-nineteenth-century industry is a memory. Memory is allowed. Memory that pretends to be a present-tense skyline is not.

## Fort Smith’s remaining cube

If McCourt is still stacking chairs at 1001 North 3rd, Fort Smith still understands a cube. Some directories say 1983. Confirm the year. The product class is older than the door: Ballman’s 1902 folding-bed house, the 1966 plywood trademark that later associated with Samsonite, then a later shop that is neither. Three folding stories. One habit.

If Riverside is still a distribution door — the 2019 paper said the company had manufactured until a few years earlier and was by then a distribution point — Fort Smith still understands a carton. The 1946 letterhead remains a selling year in High Point. Making thinned. The carton stayed.

If FFO is still making mattresses in a tall bay of the old Whirlpool building, Fort Smith still understands sleep as a manufactured object. Couch and Bedding, 1904; Riverside’s upholstery plant; a mattress line in a refrigerator shed. The buildings change owners. The need does not.

None of that is Riverfront Drive. All of it is the afterlife without a cello.

The Museum can hold a stencil. The Historical Society, formed in 1977, can hold a *Journal*. UAFS can hold a class. The chamber can hold Manufacturing Week. Those are civic stills. They are not sanders. Do not confuse them.

## The other counties

Harrison’s pew plants are gone. Turney opened in a garage in 1946, claimed a hemisphere in 1965, went to auction in 1968. Plant A still stands and does not manufacture. Plants B and C are gone. The oak, in some sanctuary, may still be taking a hymn.

St. Joe’s kiln is a Register page: Earl Martin, powder boxes, a Perfect Chair Kit, a Moore kiln finished in 1946, a 1974 sale of the property. Frames and kits are how furniture moves when money is short. The living room next to the plant is still the best solvent for founder myths.

Sound-Craft’s copy still describes a Morrilton shop with a 1947 claim and a Highway 64 door. Lecterns, racks, workstations. Confirm the door. Akin’s copy still describes Monticello, 1985, hospitality and senior living. Confirm the door. Drew County is not Fort Smith. A hotel dresser is still a factory object.

Warren still has timber weather and, in public listings, a furniture LLC on Pennington Street. Bradley Brand Furniture, LLC, 501 Pennington, organized 2008 in some directories, is not Bradley Lumber Company of Arkansas. It is not Fullerton. It is not Ballman-Cummings. It is not Riverside. It is not Turney. The postcard already split the job: furniture stock, flooring, pine millwork. A later shop that makes finished objects in the same county is a rhyme. It is not a will.

A 65-foot shop with glass on the long wall, if that is the shop an editor can photograph, should be captioned with a year and a county. It should not be captioned “the heir of Fort Smith furniture.” Heirs are legal. This is a craft class that survived a century of other people’s corporations.

## How a heritage page should end, if it must end

Not with a shop-our-collection. Not with a fake dynasty. With a fence and a freight bill. We make work tops and boards in a timber county. The factories that taught the state how to ship a room were mostly in Fort Smith, and they are mostly gone as factories. The 1912 table still tells you what they ate. The Pratt opinions still tell you how a name dies. The tornado still tells you where the brick was. The rest is a small shop and a truck.

If that is not enough search traffic, the search is asking for a lie. Better to be quiet and true than loud.

Pine still grows. Oak still grows. Gum still grows. The bulletin’s refusal — shortleaf almost absent from furniture — is a 1912 fact, not a 2026 law. A painted pine top in a Warren kitchen is allowed if the caption says pine. A hotel laminate in Monticello is allowed if the caption says laminate. Honesty about species is one of the few industrial habits that got cheaper as the shops got smaller.

## Against “last”

I will not write the last furniture factory in Arkansas. I will not write the last Fort Smith furniture factory. Last is a word that makes a good documentary title and a bad map. Someone is always making a cabinet in a pole barn the camera missed.

What I will write: the district ended. The named mid-century campus converted. The statewide shops that remain are smaller, more institutional or more kitchen, more honest about species or more laminate, and they live on freight the way the row lived on freight. The talent that lasted is the cube and the hardwood habit. The talent that did not last is the two-dozen-door skyline.

Re-check McCourt, Sound-Craft, Akin, FFO, Riverside’s distribution door, and any Warren shop in the year of publication. Occupancy is a fact that expires. The fence does not: no succession from Ballman, Riverside, Turney, or Bradley Lumber. Place continuity is a county and a truck. Corporate succession is a document nobody has.

Still frame: a bench now, a door now, a bill now. If Keith’s library has that now, pull it and write the date on the caption. If it has only analog Fort Smith brick, keep that brick in the earlier essays. This essay is the one that has to survive a fact-check next year. Next year, check the doors again. The shops are not a museum, not yet, not all of them. That “not yet” is the only optimistic clause I will allow, and it is not a promise. It is a status, like draft.
