Edward Ballman has a school. Herman Udouj has an obituary that can fill a column. The sander has a belt. I do not have the sander’s name. That is not a poetic emptiness I am proud of. It is a failure of the sources used here, and it should sit in the middle of the sequence like a missing tooth.

A furniture factory is a set of stations. Rough mill, glue, machine room, sanding, stain, rub, trim, pack. Founders pass through on tours. The stations keep the same shoulders. If Fort Smith was a hub for eighty years, that is eighty years of shoulders. Some of them are in city directories under “laborer” and “finisher.” Most of them are not in the *Journal*.

The Encyclopedia’s census is the only payroll we can count without inventing a name. Fort Smith had 3,099 people in 1880 and 11,311 in 1890. A city that triples in a decade is a city that imported labor. Some of it came from Indiana with Ott’s machinery. Some of it came from the county. Some of it came from the Territory. The sander is in that import whether we know his name or not. By 1950 the city had 47,942 people; by 1960, 52,991; by 1970, 62,802. Higgins’s furniture peak sits inside that later climb. The shoulders were still being added after the founders had schools named for them.

## What I will not invent

I will not give you a composite worker named Earl. I will not give you dialect. I will not give you a Black sanding crew or a white sanding crew as a fact, even though Fort Smith was a Jim Crow city with Howard Elementary and Lincoln High and a 1912 lynching the Encyclopedia does not skip — Sanford Lewis, March 23, 1912, a mob of almost a thousand, several police officers later tried and convicted for failing to stand against it. Race structured the jobs. I do not have the furniture plants’ job books. Assigning a race to a station without a book is a different kind of lie than the booster lie.

The later demography in the same entry is equally not a furniture roster. In 1975 Fort Chaffee was a federal resettlement center for Vietnamese refugees after the fall of Saigon; many stayed. A sizeable Laotian community settled beginning in the 1980s. Hispanic immigrants became residents in recent decades. Wat Buddha Samakitham and Vietnamese temples and churches are civic facts. They are not a sander’s badge. Do not assign those communities to a furniture plant without a source.

I will not give you a wage in 1910 dollars I pulled from a national average and dressed as local. I will not give you a finger lost in a jointer. Those things happened in this industry. They happened here if they happened here. A later oral-history project can name them. This essay can only refuse the composite.

Adam Carson’s 2013 thesis and 2018 labor essay sit in the bibliography as starting points for class culture. Read them before any civic-politics claim about who voted how on a furniture street. They are not a time book.

## What the public record still gives

Turney Wood Products in Harrison is the documented floor this town’s furniture houses mostly lack. The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* dates the company 1946–1968: laminated oak church furniture, more than 300 employees at the growth, more than a thousand churches a year by the mid-1960s, $3 million in sales in 1965. An August 1966 company newsletter noted four women recently begun as laborers in the finish department — news because most female factory employees then, the entry says, worked in administration. The same newsletter recorded a three-year contract with Carpenters Local 2746. In 1968 the plant went bankrupt; the Western District of Arkansas held that the bankrupt company did not have to honor the pre-bankruptcy agreement. That is a named union, a named finish room, a named fight. It is another Arkansas furniture town. It is also proof that floors *can* be written down when someone keeps a newsletter.

Riverside, 1996: fifty people in an 80,000-square-foot upholstery plant that a tornado took. The St. Louis Fed named the count, not the fifty. Still a count. The NWS path ran through an industrial strip after downtown. Sunday night luck, in that story, means the fifty were not in the building. The two children who died on the north side were not in a plant and they were not lucky.

Pratt: no workers. Capital arguing with capital. Eugene Rapley, a sales manager, is the closest the opinions come to a floor, and he was already at Riverside by the time he testified. Tom Condren is a merchandise manager. John Ayers is an officer. The sander is not in the caption.

Higgins: little shops and garages, a train of smaller industries. Labor as a city, not a hero. The 1917 telephone operators’ strike that became a nine-day general strike, dated in the Encyclopedia, is civic context — Schmidt’s thesis is in the bibliography — and not a furniture payroll. A city that can shut down for nine days is a city whose shoulders are organized enough to be counted when someone bothers. The furniture time books, if they survive, have not been bothered with here.

## Dust as a working condition

Sanding is the furniture job that does not photograph as skill and is skill. Grit, pressure, knowing when the stain will blotch because you burned the gum. Dust that is gum dust and oak dust and whatever the finish room vents did not catch. Mid-century plants got better at dust. Nineteenth-century plants got men who coughed. I will not write a medical novel. I will write that a hub’s air was a work product.

A present-tense shop with a collector is living after that sentence. It should not congratulate itself in this paragraph. It should keep the collector on.

## Why founders eat the page

Founders leave paper that archives want: charters, lawsuits, obituaries, school names. Workers leave time books that companies throw away and interviews that no one funds. A lazy documentary will use a still of a lathe and a voice-over about industry. A less lazy one will find a paycheck. This writing pass has not found the paycheck. The honest move is to say so and to point at the archives that might still have it: Fort Smith Museum of History, UAFS special collections, union remnants, church directories, the collector in a family attic who has a Riverside badge.

Jebaraj’s 31,000 manufacturing jobs in 1999 are a metro count, mixed, late, and still nameless at the belt. Higgins’s 34 manufacturers are a wider industrial city, still nameless at the belt. Girard’s two dozen riverfront houses are doors. Behind each door someone held a sander. I would like to know who. I do not.

## A shop in another county

A 65-foot shop in Warren with a handful of people is not a factory floor and should not steal this essay’s moral as “we are the workers now.” It is a different scale with names that can still be said in a room. Say them on a heritage page if they want to be said. Do not paste them onto 1910 Fort Smith.

Still frame: a belt, dust, no face. If a later editor gets a rights-cleared photograph of a named Fort Smith finisher, replace the hole. Until then, the hole is the most accurate picture this pack can print. Ballman can keep the school. The belt kept the town fed. I would like to know who held it. I do not. That sentence is the essay.
