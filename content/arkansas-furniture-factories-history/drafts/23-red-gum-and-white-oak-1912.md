The cleanest sentence in Arkansas furniture history is a table. In 1912, J. T. Harris and Hu Maxwell published *Wood-using industries and national forests of Arkansas* as Forest Service Bulletin 106. Table 8 is annual consumption of wood for furniture. Total: 15,373,000 board feet. Red gum: 9,061,000 feet, 58.94 percent, $20.29 a thousand at the factory. White oak: 5,586,000 feet, 36.34 percent, $32.45 a thousand. Together, they wrote, about ninety-five percent of the furniture wood in the state. Eighty-five percent of all of it grown in Arkansas.

I like this table the way I like a stencil. It does not care about kings. It cares about what the factories actually ate.

## What the prices say

White oak cost more. That is the face. Gum was cheaper and, they said, could be used inside and outside. White oak was “generally an outside wood” finished to look like itself. The price gap — roughly twelve dollars a thousand — is a factory decision you can still feel on a surviving suite: the parts you see, the parts you do not, the stain that tries to make the cheaper wood into the expensive one.

Red oak is a distant third, 346,000 feet, 2.25 percent. Cottonwood, elm, ash, and a scatter of other names fill the rest of the percent column like change. Mahogany appears as a rounding error of imported desire. Cherry, they said elsewhere in the bulletin, was too scarce in Arkansas to matter. Black walnut’s in-state cut was small. The folklore woods are not the factory woods.

Shortleaf pine’s “small showing” in furniture, they wrote, was remarkable given how much other Arkansas industry demanded it. Three-quarters of manufacturing wood in the state could be pine and the furniture rooms would still want hardwood. That sentence is the whole pine-belt essay in miniature. Warren’s mills could drown the country in yellow pine and still ship furniture *stock* rather than furniture. The Fort Smith finish rooms wanted gum and oak.

## Grown here

85.44 percent grown in Arkansas. 14.56 percent not. The white oak was 92 percent home-grown. The gum 82 percent. Even the face wood was a local ridge and a local bottom, not a Great Lakes leftover. A hub, if Fort Smith was one, was a hub because the forest was a hinterland, not because a port brought in mahogany.

Schaburg, the ghost name, lives inside that 85 percent without being named. So do a hundred unnamed landings. The table is statewide. Fort Smith is the city we attach it to because that is where the furniture factories clustered in the telling. Chair stock had its own table. Do not dump Table 10 into Table 8. Harris and Maxwell already knew the split.

## More kinds, still two kinds

They noted that furniture used more kinds of wood than any other industry in the survey — and that two kinds still made ninety-five percent. Diversity as a footnote, monopoly as a practice. A finish room that can stain gum to Circassian walnut, oak, cherry, mahogany is a finish room that does not need a diverse log deck. It needs a good stain and a customer who will not put a fingernail on the pore.

The woods pack in this repository already told the working properties. This essay’s job is the factory diet. If you want tyloses and movement, go there. If you want to know what a 1912 Fort Smith plant was buying, stay here.

## How to use the table without abusing it

Do not say Arkansas furniture is 59 percent gum in 2026. Do not say every Ballman-Cummings dresser is gum. Do not turn $20.29 into a present-tense price. Do say: the best statewide snapshot we have says the industry was a two-species machine with a stain department.

An antique dealer who wants “Arkansas oak” as a selling sentence should have to live with the gum majority. An SEO page that wants “native hardwood” can have it, because 85 percent home-grown is the table’s gift. The gift comes with a species name most marketing departments still swallow: sweetgum, red gum, *Liquidambar*. Say it.

## After 1912

Second-growth, the furniture crash, the import wave, the pine that finally got into furniture as paint-grade and construction-grade cousins — later weathers. The 1912 table is a lantern, not a law. It is also the only lantern with numbers this clean. Later employment figures (31,000 manufacturing jobs in 1999) are metro and mixed. Later company talk ($40 million, 3,000 dealers) is talk. Table 8 is a count.

A Warren shop buying white oak and walnut and sycamore for islands and boards is not living in Table 8. It is living in a later, smaller, more honest species list: the woods that a small shop will show without stain theater. That is a different industrial stage. The footnote can say so. The table stays 1912.
