Peaks are named by people who outlived them. Billy Higgins told the *Times Record* that Fort Smith’s furniture industry crested for several years in the late 1950s and early 1960s, then largely faded. He did not, in those interviews, give a single banner year. I am not going to invent one. I am going to treat 1958–1963 as a working window around his clause and keep the window labeled as working.

A peak, if it is real, is orders, overtime, a finish room that cannot hire fast enough, a siding that is never empty. It is also the last moment before a town’s civic story moves. Norge opened a refrigerator and freezer plant on February 1, 1962. That date sits inside the window. The furniture peak and the appliance arrival are not a sequence. They overlap. The town that Higgins called one of three furniture hubs was already becoming a different kind of factory town while the dressers were still leaving.

The Encyclopedia’s census is the civic floor under the ridge. Fort Smith had 47,942 people in 1950, 52,991 in 1960, 62,802 in 1970. Households were still being added after the furniture crest Higgins named. A peak in one industry can sit inside a city that is still growing in another. Do not invent a citywide furniture employment number for 1960. The later manufacturing peak Jebaraj named — 31,000 metro jobs in 1999 — is a mixed count after appliances, motors, cups, and trucking had already thickened the payroll. It is not Higgins’s furniture ridge pulled forward.

## What the window contains

Riverside and Twin Rivers are still independent enough, in the compiled sketch, to be about to merge. Arkansas Best has not yet bought them (1966). Ayers Furniture Industries has not yet been signed (1967). Ballman-Cummings still has a market identity the Pratt plaintiffs would later say was lost. The 1977 opinion would recite that Ballman-Cummings had shown a net operating profit in all years except one since the early 1950s — which means the peak window, if Higgins is right, is also a window in which the old hyphenated house was still a going factory, not yet a shell.

Girard’s two dozen names on the riverfront are more plausible as a remembered skyline in 1960 than in 1980. He later listed Ayers, Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey, and Fort Smith Chair among them. Those are memory names. They are still the best spoken list we have of the row while the ridge held.

Nationally, American case goods are in a suburban house boom. Formica and Scandinavian lines are fighting with colonial oak. Fort Smith’s material diet — gum and oak, stain that pretends — is well suited to colonial and to the cheaper suite a new ranch house will take. Harris and Maxwell’s 1912 table had already made the diet official: red gum about fifty-nine percent of Arkansas furniture wood, white oak about thirty-six, together about ninety-five. I do not have Riverside’s 1960 line book. The 1912 diet and the national weather are still the weather a Fort Smith sales manager woke up in.

Camp Chaffee’s economic gravity is fading as a civic excuse. The Encyclopedia dates the post’s activation to March 27, 1942, and notes the city spent the 1950s and 1960s trying to be less dependent on it. KFSM, the state’s second television station, went on the air in Fort Smith in 1953. Furniture was the old answer to independence. Appliances, motors, and trucking were the new ones. A peak can be a last successful version of the old answer.

## How a peak fades without a single villain

Imports will get their essay. The 1996 tornado will get its essay. Neither is the whole fade. Automation, dealer rivalry, High Point’s gravitational pull, a generation of managers who sold to conglomerates, a labor market that could make more money tightening compressor bolts — fade is a pile. Higgins’s “largely faded out” is a historian’s shrug that I will not dress up as a novel.

The 2018 *Times Record* piece says that by the time furniture faded, Dixie Cup and Whirlpool were already in town or coming. Higgins, in that interview, treated those later manufacturers as the next demand for spinoffs — the little shops and garages that had once boxed furniture would box something else. That is the important adjacency. Fort Smith did not become a ruin when the dressers slowed. It changed products. People who only write furniture history can miss that the parking lots stayed full. People who only write Whirlpool history can miss that the lots had been full before.

The Encyclopedia dates Whirlpool’s purchase of Norge to 1968 and, much later, a 2004 employment of 4,600 at that plant before the mid-2012 close. Those are appliance numbers. They are here only to keep the fade from looking like a city emptying. Jebaraj’s 31,000 manufacturing jobs in 1999 sit on the far side of the furniture fade and on the near side of the Whirlpool decline. Two different peaks. Do not stack them.

## Two peaks, again

The Fort Smith Historical Society *Journal*, as Higgins and the *Times Record* quoted it, called furniture the city’s largest industry and job provider in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. That is Peak One. Higgins’s late-1950s ridge is Peak Two. Between them sit a depression, a second war, glass, gas, and the slow conversion of the Territory into a state with its own stores. Naming only 1960 is a habit that thinks history starts when the photographs are sharper.

The Encyclopedia’s 1880s boom — population nearly tripling, Garrison Avenue as the wholesale center, rails in the 1870s — is the civic floor under Peak One. Natural gas in 1887 and the glass industry sit in the same entry as furniture becoming “increasingly central.” Peak Two is the one living memory can still argue about. Someone’s uncle ran a sander. Someone’s aunt sewed a couch. Riverside’s later thousand-plus employment, if the 2001 Fed story holds, is a descendant of this window, not the window itself. Do not drag 1,300 people back into 1960 without a source.

Norge’s February 1, 1962, opening is the overlap written as a date. A refrigerator plant does not cancel a dresser plant on the first Monday. It does hire from the same city. The peak window is short enough that a person could have stained gum in 1959 and hung doors on a freezer line in 1963. I do not have that person’s name. I have the two dates.

## What not to celebrate

A peak is not a festival. It is a strain on a kiln and a housing stock and a school system. It is also, in this town, a short ridge. By the 1970s the civic story has moved. By the 1990s the tornado will make the old story photogenic again, as ruins.

For a present-tense shop, the usable warning is ordinary: the best years are the years you should be starting the next object, the way Ballman started cabinets while beds still sold. Fort Smith’s furniture houses, in the peak window, were about to be bought, partnered, and sued. That is a kind of not starting the next object.

Still frame: a shift change, cars with fins if the year is right, no banner. If the library only has a present-tense lot in Warren, do not caption it 1960. The peak is Fort Smith’s, and it was over before a lot of the people who now want the phrase were born. Say the window. Leave the banner down.
