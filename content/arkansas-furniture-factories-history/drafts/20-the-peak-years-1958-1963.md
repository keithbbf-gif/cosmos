Peaks are named by people who outlived them. Billy Higgins told the *Times Record* that Fort Smith’s furniture industry crested for several years in the late 1950s and early 1960s, then largely faded. He did not, in those interviews, give a single banner year. I am not going to invent one. I am going to treat 1958–1963 as a working window around his clause and keep the window labeled as working.

A peak, if it is real, is orders, overtime, a finish room that cannot hire fast enough, a siding that is never empty. It is also the last moment before a town’s civic story moves. Norge opened a refrigerator and freezer plant on February 1, 1962. That date sits inside the window. The furniture peak and the appliance arrival are not a sequence. They overlap. The town that was “one of three hubs” was already becoming a different kind of factory town while the dressers were still leaving.

## What the window contains

Riverside and Twin Rivers are still independent enough, in the compiled sketch, to be about to merge. Arkansas Best has not yet bought them (1966). Ayers Furniture Industries has not yet been signed (1967). Ballman-Cummings still has a market identity the Pratt plaintiffs would later say was lost. Girard’s two dozen names on the riverfront are more plausible as a remembered skyline in 1960 than in 1980.

Nationally, American case goods are in a suburban house boom. Formica and Scandinavian lines are fighting with colonial oak. Fort Smith’s material diet — gum and oak, stain that pretends — is well suited to colonial and to the cheaper suite a new ranch house will take. I do not have Riverside’s 1960 line book. The national weather is still the weather a Fort Smith sales manager woke up in.

Camp Chaffee’s economic gravity is fading as a civic excuse; the Encyclopedia notes the city spent the 1950s and 1960s trying to be less dependent on the post. Furniture was the old answer. Appliances and motors and trucking were the new ones. A peak can be a last successful version of the old answer.

## How a peak fades without a single villain

Imports will get their essay. The 1996 tornado will get its essay. Neither is the whole fade. Automation, dealer rivalry, High Point’s gravitational pull, a generation of managers who sold to conglomerates, a labor market that could make more money tightening compressor bolts — fade is a pile. Higgins’s “largely faded out” is a historian’s shrug that I will not dress up as a novel.

The 2018 piece says that by the time furniture faded, Dixie Cup and Whirlpool were already in town or coming. That is the important adjacency. Fort Smith did not become a ruin when the dressers slowed. It changed products. People who only write furniture history can miss that the parking lots stayed full. People who only write Whirlpool history can miss that the lots had been full before.

## Two peaks, again

The Journal’s claim — furniture as the city’s largest industry in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — is Peak One. Higgins’s late-1950s ridge is Peak Two. Between them sit a depression, a second war, glass, gas, and the slow conversion of the Territory into a state with its own stores. A pack that names only 1960 is a pack that thinks history starts when the photographs are sharper.

Peak Two is the one living memory can still argue about. Someone’s uncle ran a sander. Someone’s aunt sewed a couch. Riverside’s later thousand-plus employment, if the 2001 Fed story holds, is a descendant of this window, not the window itself. Do not drag 1,300 people back into 1960 without a source.

## What not to celebrate

A peak is not a festival. It is a strain on a kiln and a housing stock and a school system. It is also, in this town, a short ridge. By the 1970s the civic story has moved. By the 1990s the tornado will make the old story photogenic again, as ruins.

For a present-tense shop, the usable warning is ordinary: the best years are the years you should be starting the next object, the way Ballman started cabinets while beds still sold. Fort Smith’s furniture houses, in the peak window, were about to be bought, partnered, and sued. That is a kind of not starting the next object.

Still frame: a shift change, cars with fins if the year is right, no banner. If the library only has a present-tense lot in Warren, do not caption it 1960. The peak is Fort Smith’s, and it was over before a lot of the people who now want the phrase were born. Say the window. Leave the banner down.
