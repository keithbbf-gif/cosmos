Edward Ballman got on a train in Indianapolis as a wood turner. The Fort Smith Historical Society’s March 2007 biography, drawn from a Bernard Johnson III typescript in the museum archives, is almost blunt about the assets: born in Indianapolis in 1858, a good public-school education, some skill at the lathe. He traveled in 1878 with William Ott’s crew to start a factory that would fail under its first name. He died in Fort Smith on July 13, 1923, age sixty-four. Fifty years of company-founding sit between those two dates. A journal article would later call him the king of Fort Smith furniture manufacturing. I am more interested in the turner.

A king is a civic convenience. A turner is a rate. Oak is ring-porous. The earlywood can crumble on a bead if the tool is dull or the speed is wrong. Factory turning is how many posts per hour, how much sandpaper, how much stain to hide the tear. That knowledge is what Ott needed in 1878. It is also what Ballman later turned into ownership, and then into a string of specialized houses. The turn was not a metaphor. It was a man who could already make the object the factory claimed to sell.

## From crew to paper

The ownership sequence is in the Ballman biography and should stay in that order. Ott, Meier & Co. open the Fort Smith Furniture Factory. The company does not prosper. Alex H. Reynolds buys it around 1880. Whether Ballman stayed through Reynolds is unclear. Then Jacob C. Huff, Fred Meier, and Ballman buy the plant. By the mid-1880s the name on the door is E. Ballman & Co.; Ballman has become the principal partner. About 1891 Meier, seventeen years older, retires for health and sells his interest. In 1894 E. Ballman & Co. joins Cummings Brothers Furniture Company. Ballman is president of Ballman-Cummings and owns half the issued stock.

That is a twelve-year climb from immigrant skill to controlling paper. It is not a novel. Missing from the public sketch: the notes he signed, the relatives he may have borrowed from, the weeks the plant ran short. Do not fill those in. The climb is already unusual enough. Most turners do not end up with half a corporation.

Meier’s age gap is a small human fact that the biography bothers to keep. A younger man buying out an older partner is a shop story as old as shops. Ill health is the given reason. Ill health is often also the polite reason. Leave it polite.

In 1891, the same biography says, Ballman married Louise Weigand, daughter of George Weigand, in Fort Smith. Two daughters: Marie, later Mrs. Howard C. Pratt, and Ed Louise. I am not going to write a parlor novel around a wedding. I am going to leave the Pratt name on the table, because in 1973 the Arkansas Supreme Court captioned a minority-stockholder fight *Howard C. Pratt et al. v. Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co.* An editor should confirm that the son-in-law and the appellant are the same household before the lawsuit becomes a family story. Until then, the hyphenated company outlived the turner long enough to be argued over by people who still carried his paper.

## What he understood that a turner might not

After 1894 Ballman did more than run one factory. He founded or co-founded houses that split the catalog the way a modern brand splits SKUs. Fort Smith Folding Bed and Table Company, 1902. Fort Smith Couch and Bedding Company, 1904. Border Queen Kitchen Cabinet Company, 1906. Fort Smith Metal Products Company, 1917. Fort Smith Manufacturers Company, 1921, to market the lines together. Each name is a later essay. The pattern belongs here.

A turner who becomes an industrialist has learned that a town cannot live on one object. Beds, couches, kitchen cabinets, folding tables, metal fittings — specialization is how you keep crews busy when a single fashion dies. It is also how you become the man the chamber points to. The Historical Society says he devoted himself, after Ballman-Cummings, to making Fort Smith a diverse furniture center. That sentence can be true and still be booster language. The company list is the proof that does not need the adjective.

Read the dates as a production calendar, not as a résumé. 1902 is a fold and a freight argument. 1904 is cloth and springs. 1906 is a kitchen that does not yet have built-ins. 1917 is iron in a war year. 1921 is a selling office that tries to make those four roofs look like one town. Boulden’s population table sits under the calendar: 11,587 people in 1900, 23,975 in 1910, 28,870 in 1920. The specialized houses are what a boom town buys when it is no longer a fort town and not yet an appliance town.

Metal Products in 1917 is the tell. Furniture is never just wood. Hardware, beds that fold, the fittings Higgins later listed as spinoffs — mirrors, cloth, steel. A king of wood who starts a metal house is admitting the object is a system. The 1921 cooperative is the same admission at the sales counter.

## The word “king”

Matthew Myers’s 2014 article in the *Journal of the Fort Smith Historical Society* used the word the papers like to repeat. Higgins, talking to the *Times Record*, repeated that the journal had dubbed Ballman the king. An elementary school in Fort Smith carries the name. Civic memory is doing its job: it picked one man out of a row of factories and gave children a hallway.

The job of this pack is to keep the word in quotation marks without sneering. Ballman was a principal owner, a serial founder, a man who lasted. He was not the timber. He was not the unnamed finish-room crew. He was not Cummings, and he was not the Ayers family who later controlled both Ballman-Cummings and Fort Smith Chair. King is a headline. The lathe is a fact.

If a later writer wants a Ken Burns still, the still is not a statue. It is a turned post with a stain that almost hides a torn earlywood pore. That is the Arkansas oak problem in one object. Ballman would have known it by feel.

## What not to borrow

Do not give him speeches. The biography does not. Do not give him a rags-to-riches childhood beyond the public-school line. Do not marry him to a plot. Do not make Bradley Brand Furniture a descendant. A Warren shop that turns a leg for a kitchen island is using the same knowledge class. That is a craft rhyme. It is not a will.

Ballman’s useful lesson for a present-tense shop is organizational, and even that should be quiet. Specialize the line. Do not ask one crew to be every object. Find a way to sell the lines together so the customer meets a town, not a single SKU. The 1921 Manufacturers Company is more instructive than the crown.

## After the man

Ballman-Cummings outlived the founder as a name and then, in the late 1960s, walked into a partnership with Fort Smith Chair that the Arkansas Supreme Court had to sort. The Pratt cases are a different essay. They are also the end of the king story: the company that carried his name stopped manufacturing. Fort Smith Chair kept making furniture under Ayers. Names persist after the lathe stops. That is a warning, not a tragedy.

I keep coming back to the train from Indianapolis. A used factory’s machines, a crew, a young man who could turn. Fort Smith supplied the river, the rails, the timber, and a market that was still filling houses on the edge of the Territory. Ballman supplied a hand and then a habit of starting the next company before the last one had become the only story. The school can keep the king. The shop can keep the habit.
