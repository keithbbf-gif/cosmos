Chuck Girard, looking at a wrecked downtown and a thinned riverfront, told the *Times Record* that the April 1996 tornado opened a door for cheaper furniture made in China. I will keep his sentence and refuse to let it do all the economics. Doors that big are usually already unlatched.

American case goods spent the 1990s and 2000s moving the making to water. North Carolina felt it first in the national telling. Arkansas hardwood sawmills felt it as a lost customer for grade lumber; a 2008 *Democrat-Gazette* piece said the household wood-furniture industry that had been centered in North Carolina had spent twenty years going to China, and that about seventy percent of grade hardwood was now “home dependent.” That is timber-talk, not a Fort Smith time book. It is still the weather a remaining Riverside campus had to ship into.

Higgins had already put Fort Smith’s furniture peak in the late 1950s and early 1960s. Pratt’s 1977 opinion found Ballman-Cummings without manufacturing operations. Turney went to auction in 1968. The row Girard named was not a mid-1990s invention the storm then killed. It was a skyline that had been thinning for a generation when the wind arrived. China did not invent that thinning. China met it.

## What a local director can see

Girard could see lots. He could see a row that would not rebuild as a row. He could see a market that had learned to buy a suite that arrived in a carton with a different origin stencil. He could not, in a newspaper interview, publish a tariff table. This essay will not invent the table either. It will say the local wound and the national move happened in the same decade and got braided in memory.

About two dozen manufacturers, he said, along what became Riverfront Drive: Ayers, Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey, Fort Smith Chair. A spoken list. A tornado that hit the list. A door. The *Times Record*’s 2019 manufacturing feature kept his sentences next to Higgins’s older ones — ten thousand wagons, ornate wartime furniture, one of three hubs, Ballman the king. The braid is civic: origin, peak, storm, container. History that wants a single villain will pick the last two. History that wants the dates will keep all four.

Riverside’s 1998 rebound talk — new square footage, a company that still intended to make in Fort Smith — sits awkwardly next to “the door opened.” The St. Louis Fed, in 2001, said Riverside had rebuilt the upholstery plant on the complex it already had and employed more than 1,300 in Fort Smith then. Arkansas Business, looking at 1998, talked about combined facilities and a rebound year. Those last numbers are company talk in a trade paper. Keep them nervous. Awkward is useful. A firm can rebuild a plant and still lose the dealer base over the next ten years. A tornado can be a kink and not the cause. Both can be true in one town.

The same 2019 feature said Riverside had manufactured in Fort Smith until just a few years earlier and was by then a distribution point. I will not freeze the conversion year from a “few years ago.” I will freeze the shape: making thinned; the carton stayed. Re-check the door in the year of publication. Distribution is a present-tense claim that expires.

## Price, fashion, the container’s cube

Fort Smith’s old talent was the cube: mixed cars, ICC fights, crates. The Pratt opinions even recite an ICC reason for the 1967 partnership — two factories’ goods in one trailer. The container is that talent moved to a port. Once the cube lives at a coast, a river city is a warehouse if it is lucky and a memory if it is not. High Point kept the selling cube. Fort Smith kept, for a while, a distribution cube. Making is the cube that left.

Fashion helped. Colonial oak and stained gum were a mid-century language. Harris and Maxwell had already counted the diet: gum cheap enough to stain, oak expensive enough to show. Later floors wanted other languages, or the same language at a price no Arkansas finish room could meet. I will not write “quality died.” Some imported goods were bad and some were not. Some Fort Smith goods were bad and some were not. Price-plus-logistics is enough of a villain without a sermon about craft.

The 2008 sawmill piece is the hinterland version of the same move. Grade hardwood had been a furniture customer. When the customer left North Carolina, Arkansas ridges felt it as a market, not as a riverfront photograph. Do not turn that piece into a Fort Smith plant study. Do use it as weather.

## What empty is

Empty brick is not a trade statistic. It is a walk. This essay has tried not to turn walks into proof. A walk can still be a lede. The row Girard named is not there as a working row. That is a fact a visitor can have without a table.

Do not write that China “destroyed Fort Smith furniture” as a headline. Write that a global shift in where suites are made met a local storm and a local conversion to appliances and trucking, and the row did not survive the meeting as a row. Long sentence. Honest sentence.

Jebaraj’s metro counts — 31,000 manufacturing jobs in 1999, then the losses through 2007 and after 2008 — are mixed manufacturing. 1999 is after the tornado. The city could still post a national-looking factory rate after the row had thinned. Appliances, metals, food, trucking. The empty furniture door is one door. It is not the whole city.

## A small shop in that weather

A Warren island that still leaves on a truck is not a rebuttal to a container. It is a niche that does not need a thousand dealers. Niches can live. They can also die of freight. They should not be asked to carry a civic revenge. Optional footnotes only.

McCourt’s folding stacks, if the listings hold, are a Fort Smith niche that never needed a High Point suite. Sound-Craft’s lecterns and Akin’s hotel dressers are other niches in other counties. Niches are not a hub. They are also not nothing.

The 2008 hardwood sentence is worth keeping next to Girard’s door. Household wood furniture centered in North Carolina moved over about twenty years. Grade lumber lost that customer. Seventy percent of grade hardwood, the paper said, was then “home dependent.” A Fort Smith finish room that had stained gum to look like walnut was already living in a fashion language the container could copy cheaper. The storm opened lots. The lots did not invent the container. Say both. Do not invent a customs year when Fort Smith “lost.” There is no such year in the sources I used. There is a peak window, a partnership fight, a tornado, a conversion to distribution, and a director looking at brick.

Still frame: a carton, or an empty door. No flag. No map of Asia. The origin stencil, if we have one we own, should be whatever it is. If we do not have one, we do not draw a fake “Made in…” for drama. Girard’s sentence is already drama enough. This essay’s job is to put a latch on it so the door cannot swing through the whole century. 1878 was not China. 1912 was not China. 1960 was not China. 1996–2010, in the national case-goods trade, often was. Say the years. Leave the civic shout for the lots.
