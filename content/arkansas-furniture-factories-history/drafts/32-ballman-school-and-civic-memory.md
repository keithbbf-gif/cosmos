The *Times Record*, in the manufacturing feature that leaned on Higgins, mentioned an elementary school named for Edward Ballman. I have not stood under the sign. I am willing to treat the naming as real and as a civic choice. Towns name schools for donors, for dead children, for presidents, and sometimes for the man who employed someone’s grandfather. Fort Smith chose the furniture founder.

<!-- PHOTO: fig-01 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — school sign or civic plaque only if owner-held; else a notebook on a desk (filename pending shop pull) -->

A school is a better monument than a statue of a king. Children walk it. It does not require a pedestal. It also cannot hold the 1885 drowning, the 1894 hyphen, the 1967 partnership, the gum majority, the fifty people in a 1996 upholstery plant. Civic memory is a reduction. This essay is about the reduction, not a complaint that reductions exist.

## What a name teaches

If the hallway says Ballman and the lesson says nothing, the name is atmosphere. If a fourth-grade Arkansas-history unit says the town made furniture, the name is a hook. I do not have the district’s unit. I have the risk: atmosphere wins. Furniture becomes a dead industry with a pleasant old name, like a flour mill on a letterhead.

The Journal’s “king” is the adult version of the same reduction. Useful for a headline. Fatal for a time book. This pack has tried to keep the lathe in front of the crown. A school cannot do that every morning. A museum can do it on a Saturday. Between them, a town decides whether furniture is a subject or a mascot.

## Other names that did not get hallways

Cummings did not. Huff did not. Ott did not, except in this pack. The Ayers family got a selling word and a court caption. Riverside got living memory and a tornado story. Girard’s Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey got a spoken list. Schaburg got a ghost. The sander got a belt.

Civic memory is not a democracy. It is a committee, a donor, a moment when the board needed a name. I will not invent the board meeting that chose Ballman. I will say that choosing him is consistent with how American factory towns work: one industrialist, one school, a later generation that thinks industry was a man.

Udouj’s obituaries — clubs, hospital boards, a farm — are the mid-century version of the same civic absorption. The factory man becomes a town man. The town man gets paragraphs. The paragraphs do not sand.

## Parker, the fort, the other memory

Fort Smith’s national memory is not furniture. It is the second fort, Judge Parker, the gallows, Miss Laura’s, now a marshals museum. Those stories have federal paint and tourist wayfinding. Furniture has a local journal and a school. The imbalance is not a conspiracy. Federal stories get federal sites. Factory stories get whatever a historical society can staff.

This pack is a way to staff the factory story without asking the National Park Service to care. It is also a way to keep the school from being the only sentence. Children can have Ballman. Adults can have Pratt and Table 8.

## A present-tense naming problem

Bradley Brand Furniture is a living name that wants search traffic. It should not want a school. It should not want to be mistaken for Bradley Lumber or Ballman. Civic memory that smears names is how SEO becomes a mess and how families get angry. Keep the school on Ballman. Keep the LLC on Pennington Street. Keep the lumber company on a Warren postcard as a mill, not a furniture house.

Still frame: a sign, if we have clearance, or a turned post with no plaque. I prefer the post. The post is what the man actually knew. The school is what the town needed later, when the post was no longer a payroll. Both can exist. Only one of them made a dresser. This essay will not take the school away. It will not let the school finish the history.
