The *Times Record*, in the manufacturing feature that leaned on Higgins, mentioned an elementary school named for Edward Ballman. I have not stood under the sign. I am willing to treat the naming as real and as a civic choice. Towns name schools for donors, for dead children, for presidents, and sometimes for the man who employed someone’s grandfather. Fort Smith chose the furniture founder.

A school is a better monument than a statue of a king. Children walk it. It does not require a pedestal. It also cannot hold the 1885 drowning, the 1894 hyphen, the 1967 partnership, the gum majority, the fifty people in a 1996 upholstery plant. Civic memory is a reduction. This essay is about the reduction, not a complaint that reductions exist.

## What a name teaches

If the hallway says Ballman and the lesson says nothing, the name is atmosphere. If a fourth-grade Arkansas-history unit says the town made furniture, the name is a hook. I do not have the district’s unit. I have the risk: atmosphere wins. Furniture becomes a dead industry with a pleasant old name, like a flour mill on a letterhead.

Higgins told the paper that Ballman came from Indianapolis in the late 1800s and that the Historical Society *Journal* dubbed him the king of Fort Smith furniture. Matthew Myers’s 2014 piece in volume 38 is the adult version of the same reduction. Useful for a headline. Fatal for a time book. The *Journal* also said furniture was the city’s largest industry and job provider in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. That sentence can live in a hallway if anyone writes it on a poster. Most hallways do not.

A school cannot put the lathe in front of the crown every morning. A museum can do it on a Saturday. Between them, a town decides whether furniture is a subject or a mascot.

## Other names that did not get hallways

Cummings did not. Huff did not. Ott did not, except in factory histories that bother with 1878. The Ayers family got a selling word and a court caption. Riverside got living memory and a tornado story. Girard’s Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey got a spoken list. Schaburg got a ghost. The sander got a belt.

Norge opened on February 1, 1962. Whirlpool bought the plant in 1968 and left in 2012. Baldor, Rheem, ABF — the Encyclopedia and the *Times Record* scatter those mid-century names as the next civic employers. I have not found, in the published sources I used, an elementary school named for a refrigerator. That is not a slight. It is a reminder that civic naming is uneven. Furniture got the founder’s name because furniture had already become the town’s origin story by the time someone needed a school name. Appliances got the payroll and, later, the empty bay.

Civic memory is not a democracy. It is a committee, a donor, a moment when the board needed a name. I will not invent the board meeting that chose Ballman. I will say that choosing him is consistent with how American factory towns work: one industrialist, one school, a later generation that thinks industry was a man.

Herman Udouj’s obituaries — clubs, hospital boards, a farm — are the mid-century version of the same civic absorption. The factory man becomes a town man. The town man gets paragraphs. The paragraphs do not sand. Do not dump an unverified board list onto Ballman’s essay. The shape is enough: later furniture also produced civic men, and civic men also did not get the hallway.

## Parker, the fort, the other memory

Fort Smith’s national memory is not furniture. It is the second fort, Judge Isaac Parker, the gallows, Miss Laura’s, and, as of 2023, a U.S. Marshals Museum. Boulden’s Encyclopedia entry spends its attraction paragraphs on the National Historic Site, Belle Grove, the Historical Society, the art museum, the nature center, the national cemetery. Those stories have federal paint and tourist wayfinding. Furniture has a local journal and a school.

The imbalance is not a conspiracy. Federal stories get federal sites. Factory stories get whatever a historical society can staff. The Society formed in 1977 and still operates out of the Museum of History. Volume 38 is the Ballman door. The school is the children’s door. Adults who never walk either door still know Parker, because Parker is on the highway signs.

The city’s population almost tripled in the 1880s — 3,099 in 1880, 11,311 in 1890, in the Encyclopedia’s table — the same decade railroads arrived and Garrison became a wholesale street. By 1910 the count was 23,975. Ballman’s working life sits inside that boom. A school named for him is a way to remember the boom as a person. Harris and Maxwell’s 1912 table is a way to remember it as 15,373,000 board feet of gum and oak. Both can be true. Only one of them fits on a sign.

Fort Smith’s other named memories have buildings. The National Historic Site holds the second fort. Miss Laura’s holds the regulated district. The U.S. Marshals Museum, opened in 2023, holds the deputies. Belle Grove holds houses. The national cemetery holds the oldest of the state’s three. Furniture holds a hallway and a Saturday case if the Museum has the case. Children who walk Ballman School will learn Parker from the highway before they learn a lathe from the hallway. That is not the school’s fault. It is the town’s memory budget.

The Encyclopedia’s education paragraphs are another civic file: a superintendent who would not start desegregation until 1957, a stairstep plan, *Rogers v. Paul* in 1965 directing immediate high-school desegregation. Those sentences are not furniture. They are what a school district actually argued about. A furniture founder’s name on an elementary door sits on top of that later fight without explaining it. Do not pretend the name is a lesson in labor. It is a name.

## A present-tense naming problem

Bradley Brand Furniture is a living name that wants search traffic. It should not want a school. It should not want to be mistaken for Bradley Lumber or Ballman. Civic memory that smears names is how search pages become a mess and how families get angry. Keep the school on Ballman. Keep the LLC on Pennington Street. Keep the lumber company on a Warren postcard as a mill, not a furniture house.

Fort Smith’s later naming habits — Manufacturing Week at the chamber, a university that grew out of a junior college, a mall on Rogers Avenue — are civic stills. They are not sanders. Do not confuse a week of posters with a finish room. Do not confuse a school name with a time book.

The *Times Record*’s 2019 feature celebrated National Manufacturing Week with UAFS. That week is how a later city remembers factories without having to name a sander. Ballman’s hallway is the older version of the same habit: a public name, a reduced story. Useful. Incomplete. Keep both. Give the children the name. Give adults Pratt, Table 8, and the fifty people in an upholstery plant the tornado took.

Still frame: a sign, if we have clearance, or a turned post with no plaque. I prefer the post. The post is what the man actually knew. The school is what the town needed later, when the post was no longer a payroll. Both can exist. Only one of them made a dresser. This essay will not take the school away. It will not let the school finish the history.
