On June 28, 1968, the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Arkansas told Carpenters Local 2746 that Turney Wood Products, bankrupt, did not have to honor the labor agreement it had signed before the filing. The shop in Harrison had been a union shop in its own newsletter’s telling — a three-year contract covering wages, hours, and conditions. The receiver had reopened the plant, cut pay, laid people off. The union sued. The contract lost.

That is the cleanest furniture-union story the public record has given this writer, and it is not a Fort Smith story. I am putting it first anyway, because factory labor history that only follows the riverfront will pretend Arkansas furniture had no unions. It had at least this one, and it had a court date.

## What a carpenters’ local is doing in a furniture plant

The Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners hung more than doors. Industrial locals in wood products are an old American pattern: the union follows the material. A laminated-pew factory in Harrison is wood products with a church customer. Local 2746’s paper, if we had the whole of it, would read like a mill contract more than a residential-carpenter contract. Wages, hours, conditions — the Encyclopedia’s trio — are the trio every such paper carries.

Turney’s August 1966 newsletter, as Ali Welky paraphrases it, announced the three-year agreement in the same issue that treated four women in the finish department as news. The unit and the finish room were living in one shop. I do not have the wage scale. I do not have the overtime clause. I have the fact that the company was willing to print the contract as a shop fact, the way it printed honors and sales boasts.

The 1967 sale of controlling interest to Tulsa businessmen, the March 18, 1968 shutdown, the March 22 filing, the May reopen under L. E. Durand as operating receiver, the June 28 decision, the June auction: that is a six-month lesson in how fast a contract becomes a souvenir. The Encyclopedia says the company had been over-extended. Claude Turney’s civic prizes the year before — Kentucky Colonel, Arkansas Traveler, a National Academy award — do not appear in the opinion. They do not have to. The court was not asked about prizes. It was asked whether a bankrupt furniture company still owed the paper it had signed while it was solvent.

The union filed an unfair-labor-practice complaint as well as the lawsuit. I have not read the complaint file. Welky records that it was filed. A later editor with the Western District papers can say whether the Board ever issued a complaint of its own. The published thing we have is 289 F. Supp. 143. The holding, as the Encyclopedia renders it, is blunt: the bankrupt company did not have to abide by its pre-bankruptcy agreement with the union.

## Fort Smith’s quieter file

I have not found, in the published sources I used, a bargaining history for Ballman-Cummings, Fort Smith Chair, or Riverside. That is not the same as “open shop forever.” It is a research gap. Adam Carson’s work on Fort Smith’s conservative economic culture — the 2013 thesis *Feet in the South, Eyes to the West* and the 2018 essay “Beyond Boosterism” — is the place an editor should start before writing a thesis about why the gap exists. A booster city can have unions and still talk as if it does not. A booster city can also crush them early and leave no pamphlet. Carson is a starting point, not a verdict I will steal.

The 1917 general strike is the loud Fort Smith labor event in the Encyclopedia: telephone operators at Southwestern Bell beginning September 19, lasting until late December, then a general strike that shut the city for nine days. Furniture is not named in the summary I have. Do not hang a dresser on that strike. Do notice that the city knew how to stop. A furniture history that never mentions 1917 is a history that thinks industry is only objects.

Higgins, talking to the *Times Record*, said Fort Smith had all the attributes of an industrial city: upholsterers, cloth, mirrors, steel fittings, a train of smaller shops. An industrial city is the kind of place that generates locals, or generates a policy against them. Either sentence needs paper. The paper, for the riverfront houses, is still in an attic or a Board file I have not opened.

## Pratt without a bargaining table

November 1967, the same season Turney sold controlling interest, Ballman-Cummings and Fort Smith Chair formed the Ayers Furniture Industries partnership. The Pratt opinions that followed — 1973 and 1977 — are capital arguing with capital. Minority stockholders wanted fair value. The company said a partnership was not a merger. No local files an amicus about sanders. When Ballman-Cummings later exists without manufacturing operations, as the 1977 opinion recites, the people who stop clocking in are not the plaintiffs. Union or not, they are the silent half of a liquidation. A labor essay has to say that even when the union paper is missing.

Riverside’s 1,300 in the 2001 St. Louis Fed story, if it holds, is a large enough payroll to have had some form of representation or a deliberate policy against it. I will not guess which. I will say that a thousand-person furniture campus in the 1990s sat in a national industry that was already losing shops to right-to-work states and then to water. Arkansas was not a bystander in that map. Jebaraj’s later metro counts — 31,000 manufacturing jobs in 1999, then the losses — are mixed manufacturing, not a union density table. They still describe a city that knew factory payrolls after the pew shop in Harrison had already gone to auction.

## After the contract dies

Turney’s buildings: Plant A still standing, other plants gone, Encyclopedia 2026 update. The local’s paper is a citation. The pews are in churches that may not know the court date. That is ordinary. It is also why a factory history should keep the citation: otherwise the pews become handmade Arkansas heritage with the fight sanded off.

A three-year contract signed in 1966 would have run into 1969 if the company had lived. It did not live. The receiver’s May reopen asked the floor to come back at a different price. The June opinion said the old price was not enforceable against the bankrupt estate. Auction sold the estate. That sequence is labor history even for a reader who never cares about pews. Bankruptcy is how a lot of American wood contracts ended in the late 1960s. Harrison gave us the docket.

Schmidt’s thesis on the Hello Girls is civic weather for Fort Smith, not a Carpenters file. September 19 to late December 1917 is a telephone story that became a city story for nine days. The Brotherhood of Carpenters did not need that strike to exist. A furniture essay needs it so the riverfront does not look like a town that never stopped. Stopping is a labor fact even when the stopped shops are not named.

Turney’s mid-1960s payroll — more than three hundred, three plants, a truck fleet — is the scale at which a local number makes sense. A 65-foot shop in another county is not that scale. Do not scold the small shop for lacking 2746. Do not praise the small shop as the freedom the court took away. The court took away a paper in Harrison. The paper had a number. Keep the number.

A present-tense small shop is almost never a bargaining unit. Do not romanticize that as freedom and do not scold it as failure. It is a scale. This essay is about the scale that had a local number.

Still frame: a stapled contract, or a parking lot. No raised fist clip art. If an editor finds a Fort Smith furniture local — number, years, a strike vote — this essay should be rewritten around that number. Until then, 2746 and 1968 are the load-bearing marks. They are enough to stop the sentence “Arkansas furniture was just families.” Families sign contracts too. Sometimes they sign them on the other side.
