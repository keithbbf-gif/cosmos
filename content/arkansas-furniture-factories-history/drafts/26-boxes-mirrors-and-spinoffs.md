To manufacture furniture, Higgins said, you need upholsterers, cloth, mirrors, steel fittings. All of that created a train of smaller industries, and thus it is documented that Fort Smith had the attributes of an industrial city. He was not describing a feeling. He was describing doors that were not the furniture doors.

The 2018 interview adds the sentence I keep: even with the furniture, you had to box the stuff to ship it, so someone came in with a chance to make boxes, and that is manufacturing too. The 34 manufacturers he mentioned in that conversation are this wider count, not Girard’s two dozen furniture names. Keep the numbers on their own benches. Girard’s list is the riverfront row — Ayers, Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey, Fort Smith Chair, and the rest of a remembered skyline. Higgins’s thirty-four is the industrial city those doors required.

The *Times Record* in 2019 put a later multiplier on the same idea. The National Association of Manufacturers, as that piece cited it, claims that for every worker in manufacturing another four are hired in support businesses. Treat the four as a trade-association number, not a 1910 Fort Smith census. The shape is Higgins’s train: cloth, glass, steel, crates, a man who makes only corner blocks. Jebaraj’s metro manufacturing peak of 31,000 jobs in 1999 is the late, mixed version of a city that still believed in that train, after furniture had already faded as the famous car.

## Gum, again, as a crate

Bulletin 106’s box-and-crate table is a monster next to the furniture table. Red gum: 47,363,000 board feet, almost forty-five percent of Arkansas box wood. Shortleaf pine next. The state that stained gum into walnut also nailed gum into shooks. I do not have a named Fort Smith crate house I will swear to. I have a statewide industry that furniture cannot live without, and a city that shipped furniture.

Three-ply veneer with crossed grain, they wrote, made light strong cases. A dresser in a light strong case is a dresser that can go to Dallas. A dresser in a bad case is a claim. The spinoff is not cute. It is the difference between a hub and a local cabinet shop.

The rest of Table 4 is a materials lesson the furniture houses borrowed. Cottonwood, cypress, sycamore, and ash, the bulletin said, were often selected for boxes that shipped provisions, because those woods are free from stain and odor. Elm and oak were preferred for crates that had to resist strains and shocks. A vanity mirror in an oak-reinforced crate is two spinoffs in one object. Few box mills, they wrote, turned out nailed boxes; they made shooks and sold them knocked down. Someone in Fort Smith, if Higgins is right, was in that business because the dressers would not walk.

Vehicle stock is the older cousin of the same statewide habit. Arkansas, they wrote, used about 76 million feet of wood for vehicle parts and only about 3.5 million for finished vehicles; most of the parts went north to Chicago, Cincinnati, St. Louis, and Memphis. Fort Smith’s Civil War diversion from wagons to furniture, in Higgins’s telling, is that cousin changing clothes. The crate shop is what you keep when the object is a dresser instead of a wagon bed.

## Mirrors

A dresser without a glass is a chest. A town that can silver or at least set glass is a town that can sell a bedroom. Fort Smith’s gas-fed glass industry is the civic cousin. Whether a particular mirror on a particular 1910 suite was made in town is a piece-level question. The spinoff claim is only that the *trade* had a reason to exist here. Do not invent “Fort Smith Mirror Works, 1901.” Do say that Higgins named mirrors because he had seen the dependency.

Glass is also breakable freight. The crate shop and the mirror trade are one argument. Anyone who has packed a vanity knows.

## Cloth and steel

Couch and Bedding, 1904, is the named furniture-side of cloth. The cloth itself — mills, jobbers, the railroad in — is the unnamed side. Metal Products, 1917, is the named furniture-side of steel. Railroad shops and later Baldor are the unnamed larger metal world. Spinoffs are not all small. Some are other industries that furniture gets to borrow.

Upholsterers are labor, not a plant. They can be a department or a contractor. Either way they are a skill the case-goods lathe does not teach. A hub that cannot hire them sells frames. St. Joe later sold frames on purpose. Fort Smith, in the civic telling, wanted the finished object. Finished means cloth.

## Little shops, garages, the undocumented row

Higgins’s “little shops and garages that supplied needed elements” is the hardest sentence to source and the truest to how factory towns feel. A man who makes only corner blocks. A woman who sews only dust covers. A shop that plates only a certain catch. These names die with a phone book. A pack that only writes Ballman and Riverside is a pack that believes in letterheads.

I will not invent the garages. I will leave a hole where they were. An editor who finds a 1948 classified section listing “furniture hardware, rear of…” should drop it in. Until then, the hole is the point.

Higgins’s Whirlpool sentence is the later version of the same train. It was not just the 6,000 people that plant employed at its peak, he told the *Times Record*; it was the little shops and garages that supplied needed elements. The Encyclopedia later puts that plant’s 2004 count at 4,600 and the mid-2012 close on the record. Confirm Higgins’s 6,000 against a second source before anyone uses it as a caption. The point holds either way: a large factory is a customer for smaller ones. When the large one changes products, the small ones change or die.

## When the train uncouples

If the furniture plant becomes a distribution point, the crate shop loses a customer or becomes a warehouse tape gun. The mirror trade buys imported glass already set. The upholsterer moves to a custom shop or to another town. Spinoffs are loyal to a volume, not to a civic story. Riverside’s conversion — manufactured in Fort Smith until a few years before 2019, then a distribution point, in the *Times Record*’s sentence — is a spinoff recession as much as a factory recession. Girard’s remark that the 1996 tornado opened a door for cheaper Chinese furniture is the civic compression of the same uncoupling. The crate still exists. It is often made somewhere else, around an object made somewhere else.

A small present-tense shop still has spinoffs, just fewer: a sharpener, a trucker, a kiln that belongs to someone else, a hardware jobber in another state. Bradley Brand’s LTL life is a spinoff life. The footnote can say so. The body stays with the Fort Smith train Higgins named.

Bulletin 106’s vehicle-stock table is the older statewide version of Higgins’s train: Arkansas cut parts for wagons other cities assembled. Fort Smith’s Civil War diversion from wagons to furniture, in his telling, kept the town in the finished-object business. The crate, the mirror, and the steel fitting are how a finished object leaves town looking finished.

Still frame: a corner cleat and a coil of banding, ugly, specific. If the library has a mirror wrapped in paper, even from a present-tense move, pull it and date it. The famous object can stay in the other essays. This one is the objects that never got a brand.
