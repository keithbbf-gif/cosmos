The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* puts two factory sentences in the same breath. Natural gas was discovered in the Fort Smith area in 1887 and later attracted manufacturers, “namely a notable glass-manufacturing industry.” Furniture manufacturing “also became increasingly central.” The “also” is doing honest work. The town was not a single-product postcard.

I am not going to write the glass industry as if I have its ledgers. I am going to write the pairing, because a furniture pack that ignores glass is a pack that thinks a dresser is only wood.

## A room is three materials

Wood, iron, glass. Ballman’s company list already knew: Metal Products in 1917, the wood houses earlier. Glass is the third, often purchased, sometimes local. A sideboard with a mirror is a collaboration whether the glass plant is two streets over or two states over. Higgins put mirrors in the spinoff train. The Encyclopedia puts glass in the civic train. Between them you get a town that can imagine a finished bedroom without ordering every pane from Pittsburgh.

Gas is the shared fuel. Glass wants it. Finish rooms want it. Kilns will take it. The 1887 field does not cause Ballman — he was already an owner — but it thickens the industrial menu in the same decade the population triples. Boom towns that find a fuel add trades. Fort Smith added at least two that still get named.

## Storefronts, not just suites

Garrison Avenue’s wholesale strip needed glass as badly as the factories needed it. A furniture town that cannot show the goods is a warehouse. Plate in a display window is a sales tool. Broken plate after the 1898 tornado, and again after 1996, is a civic scar that happens to be the same material as a vanity mirror. I will not cheapen the dead of 1898 with a glass metaphor. I will say that a town’s face and a town’s dressers share a supply.

## Why the pairing matters to memory

Tourism likes the fort, the judge, Miss Laura’s. Industry memory, when it exists, likes Whirlpool or Baldor because those payrolls were huge and recent. Glass and furniture are the middle memory: large enough to have been “the” industry in someone’s grandfather’s mouth, small enough to lose the building. Writing them together keeps Fort Smith from looking like it jumped from gallows to refrigerators.

It also keeps furniture from looking unique. Unique is how boosters talk. Adjacent is how factory towns work. A glass worker and a sander could live on the same street and drink in the same place and lose work in different decades. The pairing is a social fact, not just a materials fact.

## What I will not do

I will not name a glass company I have not confirmed. I will not claim Fort Smith glass in a particular labeled suite. I will not write “crystal” as if this were Bohemia. I will not give Bradley Brand Furniture a glass ancestor. Islands do not need a pane. Vanities do. This shop, unless a note says otherwise, is on the wood side of the room.

## After both trades thinned

When furniture manufacturing thinned, the mirror on a new suite often arrived already in the carton from somewhere else. When glass manufacturing thinned, the town’s windows still came from a catalog. The pairing ends as two import stories. The Encyclopedia’s “notable” is a past tense that should stay past until someone documents a present plant.

Still frame: a mirror plate, unframed, leaning, dusty, or a mill window with bubbles in old glass. Either is the pairing. A staged bedroom is the opposite of this essay. The room can wait. The pane cannot, if we are trying to remember that Fort Smith made more than one kind of finished object in the same heat.

Wood towns that also had glass were common enough in gas country. Fort Smith’s version matters here because the furniture row made the glass useful in a particular way: as the last thing you hung on a dresser before you crated it for Texas. The last thing is easy to forget. The bulletin forgot it; it was counting board feet. This pack will not.
