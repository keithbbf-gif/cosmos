November 1967. Two Fort Smith furniture corporations sign a partnership. The name on the paper is Ayers Furniture Industries. Each side puts up fifteen hundred dollars. Each agrees to sell its merchandise to the partnership. The partnership will promote, bill, collect, and deliver. John Ayers, who already runs both corporations, will run this too. Minority stockholders in Ballman-Cummings look at the arrangement and see their company disappearing into a chair factory without anyone writing them a check.

The Arkansas Supreme Court heard them twice. *Pratt v. Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co.*, 1973 and 1977. I am not going to retry the case. I am going to take the facts the opinions were willing to recite, because those facts are some of the last detailed sentences we have about this town’s old furniture names.

## What they said they were doing

John Ayers told the court that partnership ownership of the goods made it possible to load furniture from both factories in a single trailer. ICC rules, he said, otherwise would not permit it. He also said the partnership would kill duplicate billing and collection costs. Those are dock reasons. They are also control reasons. If one man already sits at both desks, a partnership is how the desks become one desk with a legal excuse.

The appellants did not accuse him of fraud. They said Ballman-Cummings was losing its identity in the market, losing money every year after 1967, while Fort Smith Chair stayed stable. They wanted the statute that pays a dissenting stockholder the fair value of his shares when a merger happens. The company’s position was that this was a partnership, and partnerships were allowed.

Ballman-Cummings amended its charter to permit partnership agreements. That amendment is a tell. Someone knew the old papers did not imagine this. Someone changed the papers and then did it anyway, over objection.

## What the 1977 opinion found on the ground

By the second chapter of the litigation, the partnership had been dissolved. Ballman-Cummings still existed as a corporation and had no furniture manufacturing. The opinion recites a negative net worth “around $700,000” and a board recommendation to liquidate. Fort Smith Chair was still in the manufacturing business, still marketing as Ayers Furniture Industries, and had made a profit in 1971, 1972, and 1973.

Before the partnership, the court noted, Ballman-Cummings had dealer relationships that went back a long time. After, those dealers met a different name. Identity, in furniture, is not a feeling. It is a stencil a store has been ordering for twenty years. When the stencil changes, the old corporation can look profitable on no floor and worthless on paper at the same time.

The 1973 opinion had already walked through the de facto merger argument. I will not summarize the doctrine as if this were a bar outline. Readers who need the test can read the case. Readers who need the town can take this: the last fight about Ballman-Cummings was not about a new bed design. It was about whether a family that owned two factories could fold their selling into one word.

## Why this belongs in a factory pack

SEO pages about “historic Fort Smith furniture” like founders and oak. They skip shareholder suits because suits are ugly and specific. Ugly and specific is where companies actually end. The 1894 hyphen was a beginning. The 1967 partnership was an ending that took ten years of opinions to finish writing.

It is also a labor story with the workers offstage. No finish-room voice appears in Pratt. The fight is capital. The floor is implied: one plant still making, one plant not, a town that can feel the difference on a Monday without being named in a caption.

Riverside, in these same years, was a different corporate animal — already once sold to Arkansas Best, still manufacturing, still the name civilians would keep. Pratt is the older row arguing with itself.

## What not to take from it

Do not turn John Ayers into a villain for a neat film. The appellants declined to allege fraud. Do not turn the minority into folk heroes. They wanted money for shares, which is their right under a statute, not a morality play. Do not give legal advice to a present-tense reader with an old stock certificate. Do not claim Bradley Brand Furniture has any paper in this fight.

Do take the ICC sentence into the freight essay. Do take the surviving chair factory into the Chair essay. Do take the dead manufacturing operations as the end of Ballman’s corporate line, which is not the end of Arkansas furniture.

## A still frame without a courthouse column

I do not want a marble stair. I want two stencils on one crate, or a trailer backed to a dock that used to be two docks. If the library has only a present-tense truck, date it and say it is the rhyme, not the 1967 vehicle. If we have nothing, the opinions are the picture. They are already in black and white.

Fair value is a cold phrase. It is also the only phrase the old house had left. Fort Smith Chair went on making, for a while, under a partnership name that had outlived the partnership. That is how furniture towns end their nineteenth-century names: not with a parade, with an appraisal fight and a board that recommends liquidation.

The river was still there. The row was already thinning. Whirlpool was already in town. The next names on the payroll would not be hyphenated.
