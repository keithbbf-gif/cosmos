November 1967. Two Fort Smith furniture corporations sign a partnership. The name on the paper is Ayers Furniture Industries. Each side puts up fifteen hundred dollars. Each agrees to sell its merchandise to the partnership. The partnership will promote, bill, collect, and deliver. John Ayers, who already runs both corporations, will run this too. Minority stockholders in Ballman-Cummings look at the arrangement and see their company disappearing into a chair factory without anyone writing them a check.

The Arkansas Supreme Court heard them twice. *Pratt v. Ballman-Cummings Furniture Co.*, 254 Ark. 570 (1973) and 261 Ark. 25 (1977). Howard C. Pratt and the other minority holders asked a Fort Smith chancellor either to stop the partnership or to make the company pay the fair value of their shares. The chancellor, at the close of their case, said a difference of opinion with management was not enough. Justice Lyle Brown, writing in 1973, sent the case back. The appellants had made a prima facie showing that a partnership can be a merger wearing another label. I am not going to retry the doctrine. I am going to take the facts the opinions were willing to recite, because those facts are some of the last detailed sentences we have about this town’s old furniture names.

## What they said they were doing

John Ayers told the court that partnership ownership of the goods made it possible to load furniture from both factories in a single trailer. ICC rules, he said, otherwise would not permit it. He also said the partnership would kill duplicate billing and collection costs. Those are dock reasons. They are also control reasons. If one man already sits at both desks, a partnership is how the desks become one desk with a legal excuse.

The 1977 opinion quotes enough of the agreement to show what “selling to the partnership” meant on a Monday. The partnership would buy every piece either plant made, f.o.b. the respective docks, and credit the selling house ninety percent of the listed sales price. Risk of loss moved to the partnership once the furniture left the shipping dock. The partnership would keep warehouses in Fort Smith and elsewhere, print catalogs, pay salesmen’s commissions, bill customers, collect, and take the furniture back if a dealer refused it. Defective manufacture went home to the plant that had built it. Each partner would furnish, at its own expense, the executive and clerical people the partnership wanted. Article II named one general manager “fully authorized to conduct the business and affairs of the partnership.” That manager was John Ayers.

The appellants did not accuse him of fraud. They said Ballman-Cummings was losing its identity in the market, losing money every year after 1967, while Fort Smith Chair stayed stable. They formally demanded fair value under Ark. Stat. §§ 64-707 and 64-804. When the company refused, they sued. The company’s position was that this was a partnership, and partnerships were allowed.

Ballman-Cummings amended its charter to permit partnership agreements, over the minority’s objection. That amendment is a tell. Someone knew the old papers did not imagine this. Someone changed the papers and then did it anyway.

The Ayers family, by stock, controlled both corporations. The directors interlocked. John Ayers was chief officer of Ballman-Cummings, chief officer of Fort Smith Chair, and general manager of the partnership. The 1973 opinion recites that structure as the weather the minority walked into, not as a secret they uncovered.

## What the 1977 opinion found on the ground

By the second chapter of the litigation — opinion by Justice Roy, April 4, 1977, rehearing denied May 16 — the partnership had been dissolved. Ballman-Cummings still existed as a corporation and had no furniture manufacturing. The opinion recites a negative net worth “around $700,000” and a board recommendation to liquidate. Fort Smith Chair was still in the manufacturing business, still marketing as Ayers Furniture Industries, and had made a profit in 1971, 1972, and 1973.

The numbers behind the shell are in the same pages. Before the partnership, Ballman-Cummings had shown a net operating profit in all years except one since the early 1950s. After November 1967 the operations showed a net loss each year, leaving a capital deficit of $767,590 by December 31, 1970. The record, the court said, showed assets then being liquidated. When asked about the losses, Ayers pointed to “loss cycles” and the loss of the “top production manager.” The opinion notes that no explanation was given for why corrective measures did not follow the first large loss. I will leave that sentence as the court’s. It is not a shop-floor diagnosis I can improve.

Only two witnesses testified: Ayers, and Eugene Rapley, by then employed at Riverside Furniture and formerly vice president and sales manager of Ballman-Cummings. Rapley said the Ballman-Cummings name had good recognition with dealers, most of whom had bought from the house for a long time. Before the partnership the two offices sat in different places and the managements were separate except for Ayers. Afterward the offices were in one location and the management functions were consolidated. Each company still decided its own designs, production schedules, and credit policies, and each still had a comptroller. But Rapley became sales manager of both houses and of the partnership. All sales ran through Ayers Furniture Industries. No furniture was manufactured without the joint approval of Ayers, Rapley, and Tom Condren — formerly Fort Smith Chair’s sales manager, then the partnership’s merchandise manager. One person decided transportation and shipping for both plants.

Invoices went out under Ayers Furniture Industries. Payments came back to that name. Advertising and marketing used that name. Sales lists showed the individual corporate names as well; the court’s footnote says those names were printed rather inconspicuously. A later footnote is the industrial sentence: Ballman-Cummings had formerly sold to hundreds of customers, and afterward sold only to the partnership.

Identity, in furniture, is not a feeling. It is a stencil a store has been ordering for twenty years. When the stencil changes, the old corporation can look profitable on no floor and worthless on paper at the same time. The last fight about Ballman-Cummings was not about a new bed design. It was about whether a family that owned two factories could fold their selling into one word. Readers who need the legal test can read the 1973 opinion.

## The town around the suit

The partnership year sits inside a thicker Fort Smith weather. In 1966, the compiled Udouj sketch says, Arkansas Best Corporation bought the merged Riverside and Twin Rivers houses. In 1968 the *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* dates Whirlpool’s purchase of the Norge refrigerator plant that had opened on February 1, 1962. Higgins, talking later to the *Times Record*, put the furniture industry’s crest in the late 1950s and early 1960s, then a fade. Pratt is the older row arguing with itself while the newer house is already a subsidiary and the appliance plant is already the civic future.

Riverside, in these same years, was a different corporate animal — already once sold, still manufacturing, still the name civilians would keep. The 1977 record even borrows a Riverside employee as a witness about the older house. That is not a merger of the two stories. It is a small-town fact: the salesmen who could explain Ballman-Cummings to a court had somewhere else to work.

The caption lawyers are Fort Smith firms. The fight was local paper about local plants. No finish-room voice appears in the transcript. The floor is implied: one plant still making, one plant not, a town that can feel the difference on a Monday without being named in a caption. The 1894 hyphen was a beginning. The 1967 partnership was an ending that took ten years of opinions to finish writing.

## What not to take from it

Do not turn John Ayers into a villain for a neat film. The appellants declined to allege fraud. Do not turn the minority into folk heroes. They wanted money for shares, which is their right under a statute, not a morality play. Do not give legal advice to a present-tense reader with an old stock certificate. Do not claim Bradley Brand Furniture has any paper in this fight.

Do take the ICC sentence into the freight essay. Do take the surviving chair factory into the Chair essay. Do take the dead manufacturing operations as the end of Ballman’s corporate line, which is not the end of Arkansas furniture.

## A still frame without a courthouse column

I do not want a marble stair. I want two stencils on one crate, or a trailer backed to a dock that used to be two docks. If the library has only a present-tense truck, date it and say it is the rhyme, not the 1967 vehicle. If we have nothing, the opinions are the picture. They are already in black and white.

Fair value is a cold phrase. It is also the only phrase the old house had left. Fort Smith Chair went on making, for a while, under a partnership name that had outlived the partnership. That is how furniture towns end their nineteenth-century names: not with a parade, with an appraisal fight and a board that recommends liquidation.

The river was still there. The row was already thinning. Whirlpool was already in town. The next names on the payroll would not be hyphenated.
