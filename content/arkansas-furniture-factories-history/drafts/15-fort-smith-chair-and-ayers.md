Harris and Maxwell, in 1912, already knew to split the table. Chairs and furniture, they wrote, are the same general class and are frequently made in separate factories. Small mills cut chair stock and sold it to houses that made nothing else. Table 8 counted furniture wood — 15,373,000 board feet, gum and white oak almost all of it. Table 10 counted chairs. In the few plants that made both and would not separate them, the whole output went into furniture. Fort Smith Chair Company is that split with a city name on it. The Arkansas Supreme Court would later treat it as the plant that still knew how to manufacture after Ballman-Cummings had become paper.

I will not dump Table 10’s statewide feet into this company’s yard. I do not have this company’s 1912 invoice. I have the statisticians’ habit, and a later court that still found a floor on one side of a hyphen.

I do not have a founding year I will swear to. Girard put Fort Smith Chair on the riverfront list with Ayers, as if they were neighbors and perhaps they were. By November 1967 they were something more intimate: partners in a firm called Ayers Furniture Industries, each corporation tossing in fifteen hundred dollars, each agreeing to sell its merchandise to the partnership so the partnership could bill, collect, promote, and deliver.

## The family in both boards

The Pratt opinions are not warm, which is why they are useful. The Ayers family of Fort Smith, by stock, controlled both Ballman-Cummings and Fort Smith Chair. The directors interlocked. John Ayers was chief officer of both houses and general manager of the partnership. Minority stockholders in Ballman-Cummings looked at that structure and saw a merger wearing a partnership costume. The court, in 1973, had to decide whether the costume counted.

The Fort Smith Historical Society biography names Ballman’s daughter Marie as Mrs. Howard C. Pratt. The 1973 caption is *Howard C. Pratt et al.* An editor should match the households before this becomes a story about in-laws. What the opinions already give, without that match, is colder: a hyphenated house with minority paper, a chair house that still made goods, and a family that could steer both.

Leave the legal test for the next essay. Stay here with the industrial fact: one family could steer the old hyphenated house and the chair house at the same time. That is how a row becomes a holding. It is also how a chair factory can look like the survivor when the case-goods name is the one on the lawsuit.

## Why chairs stay when suites go

A chair is a smaller cubic foot and a faster fashion cycle and, sometimes, a more stubborn local skill. The 1912 bulletin’s separate chair table is a hint that Arkansas already had a chair-stock economy underneath the suite economy. Fort Smith Chair, if it was any good, sat on that economy: posts, seats, rungs, a finish that can take kitchen use. White oak as the outside wood that has to look like furniture; gum as the cheaper rail you do not see unless you turn the chair over. Those are Table 8 habits leaking into Table 10. A chair factory still lives on them.

Girard put both names on the riverfront list. Riverfront Drive is the later civic name for a bank that furniture had been choosing since the 1885 lease move. I do not have a plat that puts Fort Smith Chair on a numbered door. I have a museum director’s mouth and a court that assumed a going corporation by 1967. Keep the door until a directory opens.

Suites die when a dealer in Dallas can get a cheaper suite from a container. Chairs die too, but they die later if the factory has a contract channel — institutions, dinettes, the kind of chair that is a dozen at a time. I do not have Fort Smith Chair’s customer list. I have the 1977 court’s remark that the company was still manufacturing, still marketing as Ayers, and profitable in 1971, 1972, and 1973, while Ballman-Cummings had no manufacturing operations and a negative net worth on the order of seven hundred thousand dollars. The partnership, that later opinion said, had already been dissolved. The board of the hyphenated house had recommended liquidation. Identity, the 1977 court noted, had been dealer relationships that went back a long time; after 1967 those dealers met a different name.

Those are court numbers, which means they are someone’s testimony frozen in an opinion. Treat them as the opinion’s numbers. They are still the clearest picture we have of which name still had a floor.

## Ayers as a word

Ayers is a family, a partnership, and a brand the chair company kept using after the partnership dissolved. Girard’s spoken list puts “Ayers” on the row as if it were a factory door. It may have been. It was certainly a selling door. John Ayers, in 1973, explained that partnership ownership of the goods let him load both factories’ furniture in one trailer — something ICC rules otherwise blocked. The name on the trailer was the point.

So when a later antique listing says “Ayers Fort Smith,” the honest gloss is: a selling identity tied to Fort Smith Chair and, for a few years, to Ballman-Cummings goods as well. It is not automatically a Ballman piece. It is not a Riverside piece. Ask for a stencil.

John Ayers also told the 1973 court that the partnership would kill duplicate billing and collection. Dock reasons and office reasons in the same breath. The 1973 opinion recites the appellants’ picture of combined operations: one sales manager, one designer, one purchasing desk for both roofs. I will not turn those job titles into a shop-floor novel. I will say that a row becomes a holding when the desks merge even if the brick does not. The next essay can keep the legal test. This one keeps the chair that still made a sound.

## Against a single survivor myth

Riverside is the name civilians remember because it lasted into living memory and took a tornado on camera. Fort Smith Chair is the name the court remembered because it was still making money while the hyphenated house liquidated. Both memories are true and incomplete. The row had other doors. This pack will not invent their ledgers to complete a set.

McCourt’s later folding chairs are a different trade. Do not write a century of “Fort Smith chairs” as one line. Write a chair factory that outlived a suite factory, under a family that held both, in a town that was already shifting toward appliances.

## What a shop in another county does with this

Nothing genealogical. A chair is still a chair. If a Warren shop never builds one, this essay is still the Fort Smith chapter that keeps suites from eating the whole story. If an editor later wants a present-tense chair paragraph, it needs a shop note. Until then, the chair in the photograph should be a chair we can credit.

The 1912 split — chairs here, furniture there — was a statistician’s convenience. Fort Smith spent the middle of the century tangling the convenience under one family’s paper, then untangling it in court. The chair company, for a while, was what was left that still made a sound on Monday morning.
