1001 North 3rd Street is a Fort Smith address that still, in public listings, makes folding chairs and tables. McCourt Manufacturing. Some directories say 1983. I will not freeze the year until a filing agrees. I will freeze the product class, because the product class is the only honest bridge to 1902.

Ballman’s Fort Smith Folding Bed and Table Company was a named house in a town that still had a row. The Historical Society lists it first among the specialized companies he started after Ballman-Cummings, 1902. Fort Smith Plywood Company’s 1966 trademark for folding tables and chairs — serial 72244480, “FORT SMITH FOLDING TABLES CHAIRS” — is a mid-century plywood line that later associated with Samsonite Furniture in another state. McCourt is a later, smaller, still-local door. Three folding stories. One city. No genealogy.

## Why folding stays when suites leave

A suite needs a dealer, a fashion, a container’s worth of faith. A folding chair needs a church basement, a city, a rental house, a school. The customer is an institution or a jobber who understands stacks. Stacks are freight. Fort Smith’s first industrial talent was stacks — wagons, then crates, then chairs that collapse. Higgins put ten thousand wagons a year just before the Civil War. A wagon is a hardwood object that has to nest its parts in a shop and then open on a road. A folding chair is a smaller version of the same argument.

When the suite market moved offshore, the stack market did not entirely. I do not have McCourt’s customer list. I have the logic that keeps a folding shop alive in a city that lost its row: local and regional institutional demand, a niche that imports do not always serve faster, a building that already knows metal and wood together. Metal Products, 1917, is the old named cousin among Ballman’s specialized houses. A folding chair is a metal-and-wood object whether anyone remembers Ballman or not.

Higgins told the *Times Record* that furniture pulled steel fittings among its spinoffs. A hinge is a steel fitting. 1902 and 1983 share that fitting. They do not share a minute book.

## The address as a fact

North 3rd is not Riverfront Drive. Do not drag McCourt onto Girard’s spoken list — Ayers, Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey, Fort Smith Chair. Do not write “the last factory on the row.” Write a later factory on a later street, in a city that still had manufacturing muscle — motors, trucking, leftover appliance space — when a folding shop could hire and ship.

1983, if the directories are right, sits after Higgins’s peak window and before Whirlpool’s close. Norge had been in town since 1962. Whirlpool had owned the refrigerator plant since 1968. ABF and Baldor were already civic names. A folding shop that opened in that weather opened in a factory city that had already changed objects once. It did not reopen furniture row. It occupied a niche the row had once understood.

Charles McCourt appears as owner in some listings. A later editor can confirm. This essay will not build a founder myth around a directory line.

## Samsonite, plywood, the middle folding

The 1966 trademark is the reminder that Fort Smith’s folding trade had a plywood-and-volume chapter that was not Ballman and not McCourt. South Fort Smith, Samsonite later, Murfreesboro, Tennessee, on the assignment trail. Names leave town. The habit of folding did not entirely. A history that only writes 1902 and 1983 has skipped the plywood chapter. A history that merges all three has skipped the truth.

Plywood is a mid-century material the 1912 bulletin did not have to count as furniture wood in the same way. A banquet table that folds is often a sheet good with a lock, not a gum-and-oak suite. Caption the material. Do not stain a rental-hall table into Circassian theater to make it belong in Table 8.

Institutional furniture is the statewide cousin. Turney’s pews, Sound-Craft’s lecterns, Akin’s hotel dressers, McCourt’s stacks. Four objects, four towns or streets, four letterheads. The customer is a room that is not a parlor. The freight is the talent that lasted.

## What “stayed” does not mean

It does not mean the industry stayed. It means a product class found a way to remain in the city after the civic story moved to Whirlpool and then to Whirlpool’s closing. It does not mean a tourist should put McCourt on a heritage trail without asking the company. It does not mean Bradley Brand Furniture makes folding chairs.

Girard’s two dozen doors are a district that a tornado and a container could unmake. A folding shop on North 3rd is a pin. Pins are not rows. Pins can still ship.

The search use is modest: Fort Smith still makes furniture-shaped objects you can stack. The sentence is true if the listings are true this year. Check the year. Re-check the door. Present-tense factory sentences go stale on purpose.

A fold is how you ship. That is the 1902 sentence and it is still the 1983 sentence if the year holds. A bed that stands in the day is a bed that can live in a boarding house. A table that collapses is a table that can ride in a crate without paying for air. A chair that stacks is a chair a church basement can store. Fort Smith in 1902 was a shipping town with a western customer. Fort Smith in 1983, if the directories are right, was an appliance-and-trucking town with institutional basements still in the region. The customer changed. The cube did not.

Higgins’s ten thousand wagons a year, just before the Civil War, are the oldest stack in the civic file. A wagon shop already understood parts that nest and then open. The Civil War diversion toward ornate furniture is the other fork: rooms instead of roads. Folding is the fork that kept a little of the road inside the room. Metal Products in 1917 put steel in the furniture town’s named list. A folding chair is wood and steel whether the letterhead remembers that year or not.

Do not write a tourist caption that puts McCourt on Girard’s riverfront list. North 3rd is a later street. Riverfront Drive is a spoken district. The 1996 path used downtown, industry, and the river. I have not put McCourt’s door in that path and will not. A later folding shop can live in a city that lost a row without being the row’s survivor. Survival is a product class. It is not a genealogy.

Whirlpool was still in town if 1983 is the year — Norge since 1962, Whirlpool since 1968, thousands still in the bay through the 2000s. A folding shop that hired in that weather hired in a factory city, not in a ruin. Jebaraj’s 31,000 in 1999 is mixed manufacturing. Some of those jobs were compressors. Some were freight. A few, if the listings hold, were stacks. Do not inflate McCourt into a civic savior. Do not erase it because it is not Ayers. Check the door. Caption the hinge. Leave 1902 on the 1902 letterhead.

Still frame: a stack, slightly dinged, a rental-hall color, no lifestyle picnic. If we cannot photograph McCourt, we do not steal their catalog. We photograph a folding hinge we own and caption it as the problem, not the plant. 1902 and 1983 can share a hinge. They cannot share a caption that says “since 1902” on a 1983 door. This essay will not write that caption. The trade stayed. The letterhead did not.
