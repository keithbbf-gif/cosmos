1001 North 3rd Street is a Fort Smith address that still, in public listings, makes folding chairs and tables. McCourt Manufacturing. Some directories say 1983. I will not freeze the year until a filing agrees. I will freeze the product class, because the product class is the only honest bridge to 1902.

Ballman’s Fort Smith Folding Bed and Table Company was a named house in a town that still had a row. Fort Smith Plywood Company’s 1966 trademark for folding tables and chairs is a mid-century plywood line that later associated with Samsonite Furniture in another state. McCourt is a later, smaller, still-local door. Three folding stories. One city. No genealogy.

## Why folding stays when suites leave

A suite needs a dealer, a fashion, a container’s worth of faith. A folding chair needs a church basement, a city, a rental house, a school. The customer is an institution or a jobber who understands stacks. Stacks are freight. Fort Smith’s first industrial talent was stacks — wagons, then crates, then chairs that collapse. When the suite market moved offshore, the stack market did not entirely.

I do not have McCourt’s customer list. I have the logic that keeps a folding shop alive in a city that lost its row: local and regional institutional demand, a niche that imports do not always serve faster, a building that already knows metal and wood together. Metal Products, 1917, is the old named cousin. A folding chair is a metal-and-wood object whether anyone remembers Ballman or not.

## The address as a fact

North 3rd is not Riverfront Drive. Do not drag McCourt onto Girard’s spoken list. Do not write “the last factory on the row.” Write a later factory on a later street, in a city that still had manufacturing muscle — motors, trucking, leftover appliance space — when a folding shop could hire and ship.

Charles McCourt appears as owner in some listings. A later editor can confirm. This pack will not build a founder myth around a directory line.

## Samsonite, plywood, the middle folding

The 1966 trademark is the reminder that Fort Smith’s folding trade had a plywood-and-volume chapter that was not Ballman and not McCourt. South Fort Smith, Samsonite later, Murfreesboro, Tennessee, on the assignment trail. Names leave town. The habit of folding did not entirely. A history that only writes 1902 and 1983 has skipped the plywood chapter. A history that merges all three has skipped the truth.

## What “stayed” does not mean

It does not mean the industry stayed. It means a product class found a way to remain in the city after the civic story moved to Whirlpool and then to Whirlpool’s closing. It does not mean a tourist should put McCourt on a heritage trail without asking the company. It does not mean Bradley Brand Furniture makes folding chairs.

The SEO use is modest: Fort Smith still makes furniture-shaped objects you can stack. The sentence is true if the listings are true this year. Check the year.

Still frame: a stack, slightly dinged, a rental-hall color, no lifestyle picnic. If we cannot photograph McCourt, we do not steal their catalog. We photograph a folding hinge we own and caption it as the problem, not the plant. 1902 and 1983 can share a hinge. They cannot share a caption that says “since 1902” on a 1983 door. This pack will not write that caption. The trade stayed. The letterhead did not.
