The company newsletter at Turney Wood Products, August 1966, noted that four women had begun working as laborers in the finish department. Ali Welky, writing for the *Encyclopedia of Arkansas*, reads the note as a challenge to the usual pattern: most female factory employees in that shop, at that time, were administrative. I have not held the newsletter. I trust the Encyclopedia’s paraphrase enough to build an essay on the smallness of the news.

Four is not a revolution. Four is a door. A furniture town that can only put women at a desk is a town that has decided who may ruin a stain. Finish is not a gentle job. Solvent, standing, a piece that will show a miss forever. Treating it as news that women did it in 1966 tells you what the prior years assumed.

## Fort Smith is not Harrison

I will not write “Fort Smith women sanded and stained” as a fact. I will not write that they did not. Couch and Bedding in 1904 is a product class that, nationally, often hired women in sewing and some cover work. Nationally is not a time book. The 1917 telephone operators’ strike in Fort Smith — months of it, then a general strike — proves that women in this city could shut a town down. It does not prove they stood at a furniture rub room.

The honest structure is: Harrison gave us a sentence. Fort Smith gave us a civic strike in another industry. Between them, a reader can stop assuming the furniture floor was only men without being handed a fake crew.

## Finish as a gendered idea

American factories liked to put women where the work could be called dexterous and the pay could be called less. Finish and upholstery sewing fit that habit. Machine rooms and millwright work fit the other habit. The habits are not laws. The 1966 newsletter is interesting because the company itself still found the crossing worth printing. If women had always been in that finish room, there is no note.

Turney was a union shop, Carpenters Local 2746. A labor contract that covers wages and hours also covers who is in the unit. I do not have the 1966 language on gender. The 1968 bankruptcy fight is about whether a bankrupt company must honor the contract at all. Women who had just entered the finish room entered a shop that was already walking toward auction.

## After the auction

Turney closed, reopened under a receiver, shed pay, and was sold in 1968. Whatever those four women had, they had it briefly. That is not a reason to skip them. It is a reason not to turn them into a permanent progress graph. Progress graphs are how 1966 becomes a poster.

Fort Smith’s later furniture years — Riverside’s upholstery plant with fifty people in 1996 — almost certainly included women. Upholstery sewing in mid-century America usually did. “Almost certainly” is not a caption. If an editor finds a Riverside newsletter or a union list, replace the hedge.

## What a present-tense shop does with this

A small shop’s crew is visible. If women run the finish, say so on a page that is about the shop, not about 1966 Harrison. Do not use Turney’s four as a brand ancestor. Do not use them as proof that Arkansas furniture was always equitable. It was a factory culture with a newsletter that still found women-in-finish remarkable in the mid-1960s.

Still frame: cans, rags, a window, no “Rosie” poster, no actress. If we have a rights-cleared photograph of a named woman at a named Arkansas furniture plant, that photograph should displace this entire essay’s caution. Until then, four women in a Harrison newsletter are the load-bearing fact. Small facts are how this pack stays out of the composite business.

The civic pairing I will allow: Fort Smith in 1917 learned that women on a switchboard could stop a city. Harrison in 1966 learned, in print, that women could stand in a finish room. Furniture history that only follows Ballman from the lathe to the school has not learned either lesson. This essay is a short one because the sources are short. Length would be invention.
