The company newsletter at Turney Wood Products, August 1966, noted that four women had begun working as laborers in the finish department. Ali Welky, writing for the *Encyclopedia of Arkansas*, reads the note as a challenge to the usual pattern: most female factory employees in that shop, at that time, were administrative. I have not held the newsletter. I trust the Encyclopedia’s paraphrase enough to build an essay on the smallness of the news.

Four is not a revolution. Four is a door. A furniture town that can only put women at a desk is a town that has decided who may ruin a stain. Finish is not a gentle job. Solvent, standing, a piece that will show a miss forever. Treating it as news that women did it in 1966 tells you what the prior years assumed.

## The shop that printed the note

Turney was not a garage by 1966. Claude H. Turney had opened in a Harrison garage in 1946, building furniture for the First Church of the Nazarene in town. Twenty years later the company was laminating red and white oak into pews, altars, pulpits, and lecterns, employing more than three hundred people in three plants, running trucks and private airplanes so crews could install what the plants had only partly assembled. A 1965 newsletter called Turney the largest exclusive church-furniture maker in the western hemisphere. That is their sentence. The August 1966 note sits beside that boast, not in a cottage.

A finish department in a shop that large is a station, not a hobby bench. Furniture plants of that generation ran a sequence: rough mill, glue, machine room, sanding, stain, rub, trim, pack. Harrison’s sequence had an extra last act — installation in someone else’s sanctuary — but the stain and rub still decided whether the oak looked like the catalog. A miss on a pew end is a miss a congregation will sit beside for decades. The company did not put four new laborers into a side room that did not matter. It put them where color is decided.

The same newsletter, Welky says, also recorded a three-year labor agreement with Carpenters Local 2746, Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners of America, AFL-CIO, covering wages, hours, and conditions. The four women entered a union shop. I do not have the 1966 language on gender. I will not invent a clause that welcomed them or a clause that tried to keep them out. I will say that a contract which covers the unit covers whoever is standing in the unit, and that the company still found the standing worth printing.

## Fort Smith is not Harrison

I will not write “Fort Smith women sanded and stained” as a fact. I will not write that they did not. Couch and Bedding in 1904 is a product class that, nationally, often hired women in sewing and some cover work. Nationally is not a time book. Higgins, talking to the *Times Record*, named upholsterers and cloth among the spinoffs a furniture city pulled in. He was describing a train of smaller industries, not a Fort Smith payroll with names and sexes on it.

The 1917 telephone operators’ strike in Fort Smith proves that women in this city could shut a town down. It does not prove they stood at a furniture rub room. Benjamin Boulden’s *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* entry is specific: on September 19, 1917, women employed as telephone operators at Southwestern Bell began a strike that lasted until late December and sparked a general strike that shut the whole city for nine days. Kyra Schmidt’s 2020 thesis, *Hello Girls on Strike*, is the civic monograph. Furniture is not named in the summaries I have. Do not hang a dresser on that strike. Do notice the dates. A city that lost nine days to a general strike in 1917 is a city that already knew women at work could stop the streetcars and the shops. A furniture history that never mentions 1917 is a history that thinks industry is only objects.

The honest structure is: Harrison gave us a sentence. Fort Smith gave us a civic strike in another industry. Between them, a reader can stop assuming the furniture floor was only men without being handed a fake crew.

## Finish as a gendered idea

American factories liked to put women where the work could be called dexterous and the pay could be called less. Finish and upholstery sewing fit that habit. Machine rooms and millwright work fit the other habit. The habits are not laws. The 1966 newsletter is interesting because the company itself still found the crossing worth printing. If women had always been in that finish room, there is no note.

Dexterous is a compliment that often arrives with a smaller envelope. Rubbing a stain to an even color is skill. So is knowing when gum will blotch because a sander burned it. So is standing in solvent air for a shift. Calling that work “light” is a classification, not a description. Harrison’s newsletter, as Welky reads it, was still living inside the classification: administrative women were ordinary; laboring women in finish were news.

Turney’s customer was a congregation, sometimes an Air Force chapel. The object had to look finished in a public room. That pressure is one reason finish is not a side job. It is also why a company that had just been named a civic prize machine — Kentucky Colonel, Arkansas Traveler, a National Academy award, all in 1967 — might still talk about four laborers as a curiosity. Civic air and shop-floor assumption can live in the same year.

## After the auction

The company started having money trouble, the Encyclopedia says, due in part to being over-extended. Controlling interest sold in June 1967 to three Tulsa businessmen. On March 18, 1968, operations suspended. March 22: bankruptcy. L. E. Durand was appointed operating receiver. The plant reopened in May. Workers were laid off or saw wages cut despite the contract. The union filed an unfair-labor-practice complaint and a lawsuit. On June 28, 1968, the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Arkansas held that the bankrupt company did not have to honor the pre-bankruptcy agreement. Auction followed that same June.

Whatever those four women had, they had it briefly. Twenty-two months, if they were still there when the receiver cut pay; less if they were among the layoffs. That is not a reason to skip them. It is a reason not to turn them into a permanent progress graph. Progress graphs are how 1966 becomes a poster. The court date is how 1966 becomes a factory fact: women entered a finish room in a shop that was already walking toward auction.

Plant A, 48,000 square feet, still stands in the twenty-first century, the Encyclopedia’s 2026 update says, and does not manufacture. Plants B and C are gone. The finish cans are gone with them. The pews, in churches that may never have heard the newsletter, are the objects that outlasted the note.

## A later upholstery count, still without names

Fort Smith’s later furniture years — Riverside’s upholstery plant with fifty people in 1996, as the St. Louis Fed told the story — almost certainly included women. Upholstery sewing in mid-century America usually did. Couch and Bedding had already admitted, in 1904, that a furniture town is also a cloth town. “Almost certainly” is not a caption. If an editor finds a Riverside newsletter or a union list, replace the hedge. Until then, fifty is a count of a plant the tornado took, not a sex breakdown.

The Fed named the count, not the fifty. Pratt named the capital, not the sanders. The Harrison newsletter named four women without naming them. That is the documentary ladder this essay is willing to climb. It will not invent a Fort Smith finisher to make the ladder look taller.

## What a present-tense shop does with this

A small shop’s crew is visible. If women run the finish, say so on a page that is about the shop, not about 1966 Harrison. Do not use Turney’s four as a brand ancestor. Do not use them as proof that Arkansas furniture was always equitable. It was a factory culture with a newsletter that still found women-in-finish remarkable in the mid-1960s.

A 65-foot shop in Warren with names that can still be said in a room is a different scale. Say those names on a heritage page if they want to be said. Do not paste them onto Harrison’s finish room or onto a riverfront time book we do not have.

Still frame: cans, rags, a window, no “Rosie” poster, no actress. If we have a rights-cleared photograph of a named woman at a named Arkansas furniture plant, that photograph should displace this entire essay’s caution. Until then, four women in a Harrison newsletter are the load-bearing fact. Small facts are how factory history stays out of the composite business.

The civic pairing I will allow: Fort Smith in 1917 learned that women on a switchboard could stop a city. Harrison in 1966 learned, in print, that women could stand in a finish room. Furniture history that only follows Ballman from the lathe to the school has not learned either lesson. The sources are short. They are also enough.
