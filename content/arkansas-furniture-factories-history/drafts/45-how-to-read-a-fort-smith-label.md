The useful Fort Smith furniture object, if you are trying to tell the truth, is not the carved crest. It is the back of a drawer, a paper label under a water stain, a stencil that still says a hyphen. Dealers and estate sales will give you a heavier gift: “Fort Smith oak,” said like a breed. That phrase is a town and a species and a wish. It is not a factory.

This pack has spent forty-odd essays refusing to complete Girard’s spoken list with invented letterheads. The same refusal belongs on a tailgate. If you cannot read a mark, you have a western Arkansas *type*. Types are real. Factories are named.

## What a mark can say

Ballman-Cummings, after 1894, is a hyphen. E. Ballman & Co., if it survives on an earlier piece, is a pre-hyphen house. Cummings Brothers, if anyone still has that stencil, is the other half of the merger. I have not published a census. I am naming the logical sequence from the Historical Society’s biography. Until a photograph exists, even those names are a hunt, not a guarantee on your dresser.

Ayers, after 1967, is a selling identity that could cover Fort Smith Chair goods and, for a few years, Ballman-Cummings goods sold through the partnership. The Pratt opinions are why that sentence has to be careful. “Ayers Fort Smith” on a tag is not automatically a Ballman suite and not a Riverside suite.

Riverside is the mid-century word civilians still know. It should appear as a stencil or a paper label, not as a vibe on a dark gum suite. Twin Rivers, if a mark exists, is the second Udouj roof. I have not held one.

Border Queen should be a kitchen cabinet mark, not a parlor mark. Folding Bed and Table is a 1902 class. Couch and Bedding is a 1904 class. Metal Products will not usually be on a wooden drawer. The 1921 Manufacturers Company was a selling office; I do not know whether it left a stencil. Mark that ignorance.

Fort Smith Chair is a chair factory’s name. Chairs and furniture were already separate tables in Bulletin 106. Do not put a chair factory’s name on a wardrobe because both are “Fort Smith.”

## What a mark cannot say

It cannot say the piece is “one of three hubs.” It cannot say the wood is walnut because the stain is dark. Harris and Maxwell counted red gum as most of the state’s furniture wood in 1912, stainable into Circassian walnut and oak. Open the back. Look at the pore. Look at the drawer sides. If you cannot tell gum from oak, write “stained hardwood” and stop.

It cannot say Bradley Brand Furniture made it. A Warren island in this century is a different shop and a different century. It cannot say Bradley Lumber made a suite. Bradley Lumber sold *stock*.

It cannot, by itself, date a piece to the week. Corporate names last longer than styles. Ballman-Cummings as a corporation outlived Ballman-Cummings as a factory. A late stencil can sit on an object made while the floor was already quiet. Pratt is the reminder.

## How to look without ruining the evidence

Photograph the mark in place, with a ruler if you have one, before solvent. Paper labels die in a strip. Stencils fade under a sander that was meant for a finish, not a history project. Write down where the piece came from — a Fort Smith house, an Oklahoma farm, a Texas dealer — as a provenance note, not as proof.

Measure. Kitchen-cabinet work heights before built-ins are not automatically 36 inches. Chair seat heights vary. A tape is a source.

Look at construction that this pack has already named as factory habits: stained gum faces, oak when the customer paid, crates that did not survive, hardware that may be from Metal Products or from St. Louis. Hardware alone is a weak mark. Hardware plus a stencil is a sentence.

## Forums and the complete row

Online listings will assign Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, and Covey to unmarked oak because Girard said those names in one interview. Those names are a witness list. They are not a labeling key. Until a directory and a surviving mark meet, leave them in his mouth.

The same listings will call almost any heavy western oak “Fort Smith.” The row was real. So were other towns. Harrison made pews. St. Joe made frames. Morrilton made lecterns. A pew end is not a Ballman dresser.

## What a museum can do that a driveway cannot

The Fort Smith Museum of History and the *Journal* are where a mark becomes a public comparison piece. If you have a label, ask them whether they have a twin. Do not email a JPEG and demand an appraisal. Do not lift their 1996 fire photographs to dress your listing.

A school named Ballman will not authenticate your washstand. Civic memory is a hallway. A stencil is a factory.

## Dates that help, and dates that pretend

A few dates in this pack are load-bearing if a mark exists. 1878 is Ott, Meier & Co., unlikely to be on a surviving consumer stencil. 1894 is the hyphen. 1902–1906 are the specialized houses. 1917 is metal. 1921 is a selling cooperative. 1946 is Riverside and, in another county, Turney. 1966 is Arkansas Best’s purchase and a folding-tables trademark that is not Ballman. 1967 is Ayers Furniture Industries. After the Pratt years, Ballman-Cummings as a *factory* mark should make you suspicious; the corporation could still exist on paper while the floor was dark.

None of those dates, stamped on a tag by a dealer last week, is evidence. A dealer date is a guess wearing ink. Ask what the guess is standing on.

The 1912 bulletin will not date your piece. It will only tell you that if the piece is Arkansas factory furniture from that industrial weather, gum and white oak are the prior. A mahogany-looking parlor suite from western Arkansas is often a finish-room success, not an import log.

## What to write on a tag if you sell

If you are writing a listing, the honest hierarchy is:

1. Photographed factory mark, transcribed exactly.
2. Construction notes (species guess labeled as a guess, hardware, work height).
3. Provenance (where you got it, not where you wish it lived).
4. Type (“Hoosier-style kitchen cabinet,” “mid-century case goods”) without a city if the city is a wish.

“Attributed to Fort Smith” is allowed if you say why — a mark fragment, a family story you repeat as a story, a dealer invoice. “Fort Smith Ballman-Cummings 1890 oak walnut” is four claims, at least two of them wrong on the calendar and the species. Do not write it.

Museums and the *Journal* can take a photograph and tell you they have a twin. They cannot take a vibe. Do not send them a room shot of a staged bedroom and ask which king made it.

## A present-tense shop’s version of a mark

If a Warren shop burns or stamps a name into a work top, that mark is this year’s fact. It does not borrow 1894. It should not say Fort Smith unless the object was made there. Heritage pages can tell the Fort Smith story in sentences. They should not tell it in fake vintage stamps.

Still frame: a stencil, close, dirty, readable. If Keith’s library has no Fort Smith mark, leave the slot empty or pull an unmarked back and caption it as the warning. The warning is the essay. Fort Smith furniture history is a set of named houses and a lot of oak that will never confess. Confession, when it happens, is ink.
