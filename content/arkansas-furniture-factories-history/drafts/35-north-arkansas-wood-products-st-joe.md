St. Joe is not a hub. It is a Searcy County town that held, for a while, a furniture plant with a sawmill, an assembly building, two wells, a furnace, and a short run of rail into a kiln. The Arkansas Register page for the North Arkansas Wood Products kiln is the document. Earl Martin is the name. The war is the first big order.

The Register’s narrative begins before the boxes. Martin operated a wood-products factory in Kansas City. On a fishing trip to the Buffalo River near St. Joe he saw timber enough to change the freight argument: cheaper to move the factory to the trees than to haul the trees to Kansas City. In 1941 he and Florence Martin bought six and a half acres from a descendant of G. B. Brown, on land that had been the Gray Eagle ore claim in 1900 and later the Kansas Lead and Zinc Company. About that time a railroad had been built some two and a half miles from the site, and St. Joe had become a town. While he built the Arkansas business he taught woodwork at Arkansas Tech in Russellville and kept a salaried job, commuting from Rainbolt Mountain. I have not seen the teaching contract. I will leave the Tech sentence as the Register’s.

In 1943 the federal government gave him a contract for powder boxes. Peak employment, the Register says, hit a hundred people on two shifts making powder drying racks for Naval Ordnance. He finished stages of the work in the living room of the house next to the plant. That living room is the opposite of a High Point showroom and I will not decorate it. It is a sentence about scale: the house and the factory were one property.

## The kit chair

The same year, Perfection Sales Company in St. Joe began marketing an upholstered chair called the Perfect Chair Kit. The customer assembled it and saved, they said, half the cost. North Arkansas Wood Products sawed and marked the framework; Perfection bought the frames. Knockdown as a price, not as a campaign aesthetic. St. Joe got there with a wartime chair and a mark on a stick.

The plant also turned out upholstered frames, studio-couch arms, bookcases, rockers, occasional chairs, and “Rol-A-Way” chairs for children. That list is a small factory trying to be a catalog. It is also a frame house more than a finish house. Fort Smith Couch and Bedding wanted the cover. St. Joe, in this telling, often wanted the bones.

A furniture town that only counts finished suites will miss this object class. Frames are how upholstery actually begins. Higgins named upholsterers among Fort Smith’s spinoffs. St. Joe sold the spinoff as the product.

## The kiln

Completed in 1946 — that year again — a Moore steam cross-circulation kiln, 90,000 board feet, eleven 48-inch fans, a boiler on mill waste and sawdust. Green lumber rolled from the sawmill to the kiln on a stub of rail. By 1946 the plant had forty to fifty people and needed another thirty-five to log and saw. At peak seasons, more than sixty, sometimes three shifts.

This is the power essay in a small town. Scrap is fuel. Rail is a few hundred feet, not a transcontinental boast. Hardwood is the ridge above the shop. You do not need Girard’s two dozen doors to have a factory. You need a kiln that will take a charge and a contract that will take the output.

The building itself is why the kiln, not the house, is on the Register. The Register calls it a simple industrial structure with a hand-laid veneer of local crystals, the stones set by Searcy County people who worked at the factory. Listed March 4, 1998, with local significance under Criterion A for the war work and the payroll, and Criterion C for the vernacular stone. I will not invent a mason. The hands are the same hands the page already said made the boxes.

## Basswood for a Last Supper

In the early 1960s the sculptor Domenic Zappia came to Martin for laminated basswood for a life-size Last Supper. The Register keeps the sentence. I keep it because it is the odd job that proves a wood plant is a wood plant: it can make a rack for powder and a blank for a sculptor if the species and the glue are right. Do not turn it into a pious caption. Turn it into a materials caption.

The same early 1960s brought a civic money argument. The Searcy County Industrial Development Corporation met on whether to help the Martins raise a building more suited to the operation, a loan from the Small Business Administration and the rest from St. Joe and Marshall residents. In the Register’s telling, Martin opted instead for a move to Yellville, where railroad and water would keep the factory more efficient and local leaders could fund a new facility. The St. Joe property sold in 1974. I do not have a Yellville plant history in the sources I used. I will not write one. The kiln page is a St. Joe page.

The Register’s significance section is civic, not romantic. For more than forty years, it says, the company was a catalyst for Searcy County’s economic well-being. The area was not congruous for growing anything but timber. The plant steadied the rural population by keeping people from dropping away any faster than they already had by the start of the war. Mr. and Mrs. Martin, the page adds, were major contributors to civic and educational life in St. Joe and the surrounding communities. I will not invent the gifts. I will say the kiln was nominated because a small county decided a stone industrial shed was how it remembered a payroll.

Donna Ellis submitted the information in February 1998. The listing followed on March 4. That is a documentation chain: a local researcher, an agency page, a Criterion A war-and-payroll argument, a Criterion C stone-veneer argument. Highway 65 is the location line. Do not lift the agency photograph. Ask. The crystals on the kiln are the still I want if we ever get a permit: industrial, local, laid by the same people the page said made the boxes.

## Why St. Joe belongs beside Fort Smith

Because “Arkansas furniture factories” is not a riverfront monopoly. Because frames and kits are how furniture actually moves when money is short. Because a Register page is the kind of source a factory history promised to prefer. Because 1943–1946 in St. Joe is the war and the kiln, while 1946 in Fort Smith and Harrison is the returning-founder myth. Martin’s living room is a useful solvent for myths.

Harris and Maxwell, in 1912, had already counted furniture as a small slice of Arkansas wood products beside boxes. St. Joe’s powder boxes are the war version of that older ratio. The chair frames are the furniture version. One plant, both sentences.

A Warren shop that cuts parts for someone else to finish is in St. Joe’s structural family, not Fort Smith Chair’s. That is not an insult. Frame work is work. Factory history that only loves the finished suite has already chosen the object that photographs. St. Joe is the correction.

Still frame: a kiln door, stained by steam, or a chair frame with a mark. If we cannot photograph the Register site, we do not lift their photo. We describe the door until someone gets a permit. The living room can stay a sentence. I do not want a reenactment with a powder box on a kitchen table. The Navy order is already stranger than a reenactment.
