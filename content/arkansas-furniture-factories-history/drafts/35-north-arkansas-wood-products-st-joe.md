St. Joe is not a hub. It is a Searcy County town that held, for a while, a furniture plant with a sawmill, an assembly building, two wells, a furnace, and a short run of rail into a kiln. The Arkansas Register page for the North Arkansas Wood Products kiln is the document. Earl Martin is the name. The war is the first big order.

In 1943 the federal government gave him a contract for powder boxes. Peak employment, the Register says, hit a hundred people on two shifts making powder drying racks for Naval Ordnance. He finished stages of the work in the living room of the house next to the plant. That living room is the opposite of a High Point showroom and I will not decorate it. It is a sentence about scale: the house and the factory were one property.

## The kit chair

The same year, Perfection Sales Company in St. Joe began marketing an upholstered chair called the Perfect Chair Kit. The customer assembled it and saved, they said, half the cost. North Arkansas Wood Products sawed and marked the framework; Perfection bought the frames. Knockdown as a price, not as a campaign aesthetic. The joinery pack in this repo has essays about furniture that comes apart on purpose. St. Joe got there with a wartime chair and a mark on a stick.

The plant also turned out upholstered frames, studio-couch arms, bookcases, rockers, occasional chairs, and “Rol-A-Way” chairs for children. That list is a small factory trying to be a catalog. It is also a frame house more than a finish house. Fort Smith Couch and Bedding wanted the cover. St. Joe, in this telling, often wanted the bones.

## The kiln

Completed in 1946 — that year again — a Moore steam cross-circulation kiln, 90,000 board feet, eleven 48-inch fans, a boiler on mill waste and sawdust. Green lumber rolled from the sawmill to the kiln on a stub of rail. By 1946 the plant had forty to fifty people and needed another thirty-five to log and saw. At peak seasons, more than sixty, sometimes three shifts.

This is the power essay in a small town. Scrap is fuel. Rail is a few hundred feet, not a transcontinental boast. Hardwood is the ridge above the shop. You do not need Girard’s two dozen doors to have a factory. You need a kiln that will take a charge and a contract that will take the output.

## Basswood for a Last Supper

In the early 1960s the sculptor Domenic Zappia came to Martin for laminated basswood for a life-size Last Supper. The Register keeps the sentence. I keep it because it is the odd job that proves a wood plant is a wood plant: it can make a rack for powder and a blank for a sculptor if the species and the glue are right. Do not turn it into a pious caption. Turn it into a materials caption.

## Why St. Joe belongs in a Fort Smith pack

Because “Arkansas furniture factories” is not a riverfront monopoly. Because frames and kits are how furniture actually moves when money is short. Because a Register page is the kind of source this pack promised to prefer. Because 1943–1946 in St. Joe is the war and the kiln, while 1946 in Fort Smith and Harrison is the returning-founder myth. Martin’s living room is a useful solvent for myths.

A Warren shop that cuts parts for someone else to finish is in St. Joe’s structural family, not Fort Smith Chair’s. That is not an insult. Frame work is work. This pack has been too willing, in the Fort Smith essays, to treat the finished suite as the only object. St. Joe is the correction.

Still frame: a kiln door, stained by steam, or a chair frame with a mark. If we cannot photograph the Register site, we do not lift their photo. We describe the door until someone gets a permit. The living room can stay a sentence. I do not want a reenactment with a powder box on a kitchen table. The Navy order is already stranger than a reenactment.
