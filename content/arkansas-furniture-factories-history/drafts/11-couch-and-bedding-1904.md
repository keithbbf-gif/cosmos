A couch is a wooden argument wrapped in someone else’s crop. In 1904 Ballman founded or co-founded Fort Smith Couch and Bedding Company. The Historical Society puts it two years after folding beds and two years before Border Queen cabinets. The sequence is a town learning that “furniture” is not one trade.

Higgins told the *Times Record* that furniture pulled spinoffs: upholsterers, cloth, mirrors, steel fittings. He was describing the industrial city. The 1904 letterhead is the spinoff with a name. A couch house has to buy textiles, springs, tacks, and labor that is not a lathe crew. Bedding adds ticking and the kind of cleanliness argument that a dresser never had to make.

The town around the letterhead was still climbing. Boulden: 11,587 people in 1900, 23,975 in 1910. Natural gas, found in 1887, had already pulled glass. Furniture, the encyclopedia says, was becoming central to the metropolitan economy. A couch company is how “central” stops meaning only case goods. It is also how a western rail town admits it must buy cotton and wire from somewhere that is not a Boston Mountain ridge.

## Frames first

North Arkansas Wood Products, decades later and a different county, would saw frames for a “Perfect Chair Kit” and sell them to a marketing company. That is the frame business in the open. Fort Smith Couch and Bedding, if it was a full manufacturer, had to be frame *and* cover, or it was a cover house buying frames. I do not have the 1904 bill of materials. I will not decide which. I will say that every upholstered object hides a hardwood decision: gum or oak, dowels or screws, a spring bar that will rack if the corner is cheap.

The 1912 bulletin’s furniture table is mostly case-goods thinking. Table 8: 15,373,000 board feet, red gum 9,061,000, white oak 5,586,000, 85.44 percent grown in Arkansas. Upholstery eats those feet as hidden rails — gum cheaper at $20.29 a thousand, oak the respectable face at $32.45. It also eats wire and cloth that never appear in a Forest Service wood table. Harris and Maxwell could count a rail. They could not count a yard of ticking. A town that adds a couch company is adding imports. Cotton and wool do not grow on Belle Point in factory quantity. The railroad that shipped dressers out now shipped batting in.

Higgins’s 2018 interview added the crate as its own shop. Someone had to box the goods. A couch is an awkward crate. Springs rattle. Cloth stains. Volume is a different religion than a nested table. The 1904 house, if it shipped, had to learn a packing trade the folding-bed house could treat as a fold.

## Women, and the finish-room cousin

Upholstery rooms in American factories were one of the places women were allowed on the floor earlier than in some millwright crews. I do not have a 1904 Fort Smith payroll that proves that here. Turney Wood Products in Harrison would note, in 1966, four women in the finish department as news. Do not back-date Harrison onto 1904 Fort Smith. Do say that a bedding house changes who walks in the door. That change is a later essay’s job to document, not this one’s job to invent.

The civic weather a dozen years later is easier to date and still not this plant’s. On September 19, 1917, Boulden writes, Southwestern Bell’s telephone operators walked out; a general strike then shut the city for nine days. Kyra Schmidt’s thesis follows those “Hello Girls.” I will not put them in this finish room. I will leave the date on the calendar as proof that Fort Smith’s industrial decade had more than one kind of shop floor, and that a historian who wants women in 1904 needs a different page than a strike in 1917.

## Fire, moth, and the other inventories

A wood shop burns. A bedding shop burns faster and smells like a different disaster. Insurance, sprinklers, the way you store butts of cotton — these are factory facts that case-goods men can ignore until they add a couch line. I have no 1904 fire story for this company. I will not write a fire for color. I will write that the product class forces a different kind of caution, and that a historian who finds a fire should put it in `verify` and then in the body.

Moths are a quieter inventory. Bedding that sits in a warehouse through an Arkansas summer is a biology problem. Case goods are a moisture problem. The 1904 company had to staff both.

## Riverside is not this plant

Riverside Furniture, after 1946, became the mid-century name people attach to Fort Smith upholstery. The St. Louis Fed’s 2001 *Regional Economist* piece on the April 21, 1996, tornado said Riverside lost an 80,000-square-foot upholstery plant, about fifty people, and some two million dollars. The Weather Service put touchdown at 11:12 p.m. CDT on the west side of downtown. That plant is not Fort Smith Couch and Bedding. If you collapse them you get a century-long sofa that never existed. The 1904 house is an early admission. Riverside is a later scale. The craft — frame, spring, cover — is the rhyme. The wind is a later weather.

FFO Home making mattresses in a piece of the old Whirlpool building is a still later rhyme — the 2019 *Times Record* piece is the source for that occupancy, and it should be confirmed before a present-tense sentence. Fort Smith keeps returning to sleep as a manufactured object. The buildings change. The need does not.

Metal Products in 1917 is the later confession that a couch also needs iron. Springs and tacks are not a lathe problem. They are a keg problem. The 1904 house had to solve it by purchase or by later making. I do not have the 1904 purchase book. I have the sequence.

## Why the date matters for the kitchen

Border Queen in 1906 is a hard kitchen object: wood, tin maybe, a Hoosier-style logic. Couch and Bedding in 1904 is a soft parlor object. Ballman was selling the whole house, not a style. A dealer who could get a cabinet and a couch from the same town was a dealer who could stop in Fort Smith and be done. The 1921 cooperative only makes sense if 1904 and 1906 already exist.

Girard’s later riverfront list did not bother to say “Couch and Bedding.” He said Ayers, Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey, Fort Smith Chair. Spoken maps drop the early letterheads. Keep this one anyway. The 1904 house is how the town learned it needed cloth and a different kind of caution. Without it, the hub story collapses into oak dressers and a king. The Journal’s claim that furniture was the largest local industry in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries has to include the rooms that were not case goods. A parlor is a room. A couch is how a factory enters it.

A Warren shop that builds islands and cutting boards is on the 1906 side of that split. Soft goods are not its trade. The useful inheritance is the refusal to pretend one object is the town. Fort Smith’s furniture century was a catalog. This pack’s job is to keep the catalog from collapsing into “oak dressers.”

The photograph I want is a bare frame, not a staged living room. If the library has webbing on a bench, even from a present-tense repair, pull it and say what year it is. A 1904 couch in a stock photo is someone else’s parlor.
