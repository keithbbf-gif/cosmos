By 1921 Edward Ballman had a problem he had built on purpose: too many names. Folding beds in 1902, couches in 1904, kitchen cabinets in 1906, metal fittings in 1917, the old Ballman-Cummings house from 1894. A dealer in Texas did not want five conversations. Fort Smith Manufacturers Company, the Historical Society says, was created to market the various lines in a cooperative manner. That sentence is a sales department pretending to be a town.

He would be dead two years later. The March 2007 biography gives July 13, 1923, age sixty-four. I will not write the cooperative as a last will. I will write it as the last company on the Society’s list, founded while he was alive, aimed at a dealer’s desk. The brick did not need him to stay stubborn.

Cooperative is a soft word. It can mean a true co-op with member votes. It can mean a jointly owned selling office. It can mean one man’s holding story dressed for a newspaper. I do not have the 1921 articles. I will not pick a legal form. I will stay with the job: one face for several factories.

## Why 1921

The war is over. The 1917 strike is four years back — operators out from September 19 into December, a general strike that shut the city nine days, in Boulden’s telling. The city gets an automobile bridge to Oklahoma on May 11, 1922, at the west end of Garrison, which is almost the same moment as this founding. Freight is changing from river-and-rail certainty toward trucks that will later need their own rules. A selling cooperative in 1921 is a bet that the next decade’s dealers will buy a *place* — Fort Smith — the way they already buy Grand Rapids as a place.

Boulden’s census puts 28,870 people here in 1920. Higgins’s later “one of three hubs” phrase is a historian talking to a newspaper. The Manufacturers Company is the hub as a letterhead. You do not need a national scoreboard if you can be the one stop on the way into Oklahoma. You do need the stop to feel like one stop.

It is also a bet against fashion death. If folding beds slump, kitchen cabinets might not. If metal is short, wood can still ship. A combined catalog is a hedge. Ballman had been hedging with new companies since 1902. 1921 hedges the hedges.

## The mixed car

Furniture freight is a cubic-foot religion. A carload of one SKU is simple and often wasteful. A mixed car — beds, cabinets, a few couches — is how a dealer of modest size gets a town’s worth of goods. The cooperative’s unspoken product is the mix.

Bulletin 106 had already priced the stubbornness of dimensioned wood. Furniture squares and wagon stock paid about 34 percent more than boards on the Ozark Forest example from Leslie. A mixed car of finished rooms is how a town stops shipping squares and starts shipping invoices. Harris and Maxwell’s Table 8 — 15,373,000 board feet of furniture wood, gum and oak almost all of it, 85.44 percent grown in Arkansas — is the material under the order pad. The cooperative does not grow the gum. It tries to sell the gum as a place.

Forty-six years later, John Ayers would tell the Arkansas Supreme Court that the Ayers Furniture Industries partnership existed in part because ICC rules would not let him load two corporations’ furniture in one trailer unless a partnership owned the goods. Each corporation put up fifteen hundred dollars in November 1967. That is 1967 law talk, not 1921. I put it here as the later proof that mixing was never just a catalog problem. It was a regulatory problem. The 1921 company is the early civic attempt. The 1967 partnership is the later legal attempt. Do not merge them.

## What a dealer actually met

If the cooperative worked, a salesman carried one line card and several factory promises. If it did not, the dealer still knew which plant had the cabinet he wanted and which plant was late. Brick is stubborn. Crews are stubborn. A finish room that is proud of cabinets will not hurry because a couch house needs the same car.

I have no 1923 dealer letter. The failure modes are ordinary and do not require one. Credit risk: who carries the receivable? Quality: whose stencil takes the complaint? Priority: whose order ships when the siding is short? A cooperative that does not answer those three becomes a letterhead.

The Journal’s later claim — furniture as Fort Smith’s largest industry and job provider in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — is the civic weather this paper was trying to organize. Higgins’s “one of three” phrase is the national weather. Neither sentence names the 1921 officers. Both sentences are why someone thought a cooperative was worth chartering. A still on Garrison after rain, the west-end bridge new in 1922, is the town the dealer was supposed to buy. The catalogs were supposed to make the town portable.

## Hub as a sales word

“One of three hubs” is a historian’s later phrase. Fort Smith Manufacturers Company is the hub as a business plan. You do not need to be Grand Rapids if you can be the one stop on the way to Oklahoma. You do need the stop to feel like one stop.

Girard’s riverfront row — about two dozen names, Ayers and Garrison and DeSoto and Ward and Covey and Fort Smith Chair in his spoken list — is the physical version of the same idea. The cooperative is the paper version. Between them they explain why Fort Smith furniture is remembered as a *district* even when the surviving objects carry different names. A still frame: several catalogs on a table, one order pad, no salesman posed as a visionary. If the library has only a present-tense desk, date it. The 1921 paper is the thing we do not have.

## What this is not

It is not a workers’ cooperative. Nothing in the Society’s sentence says the finishers owned it. It is not Bradley Brand Furniture’s ancestor. A small shop’s “line” — islands, boards, a couple of named series — is a catalog problem at a different scale. Optional footnotes can point at heritage. The body should not.

It is not proof that Ballman was a visionary. It is proof that he could see a dealer’s desk. That is a colder compliment and a better one.

## After the paper

I do not know, in a source I trust, when Fort Smith Manufacturers Company died. Names like that fade into the later row, into Ayers, into Riverside’s mid-century gravity. An editor with a charter revocation date should put it in the front matter. Until then, 1921 is a founding, not a lifespan.

The usable picture is a table with several catalogs and one order pad. If the library has that table, even in a present-tense sales office, pull it and date it. If not, leave the slot. The cooperative was an attempt to make Fort Smith one invoice. The invoices, like the brick, kept their own names.

A Warren shop that now prints two or three series on one site is solving a smaller version of the same desk problem. That is a catalog rhyme. It is not a charter. The 1921 paper stays in Fort Smith.
