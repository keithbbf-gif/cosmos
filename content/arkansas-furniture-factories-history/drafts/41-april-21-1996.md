11:12 p.m., April 21, 1996. The National Weather Service’s assessment is the still I trust: touchdown on the west side of downtown Fort Smith, historic buildings first, then an industrial strip, then a north-side neighborhood, then the Arkansas River, then Van Buren. About ten miles of path in the narrative; about seven miles in one executive-summary sentence. Two children dead on the north side. The furniture industry is not the center of those deaths. It is in the path because the industry had put itself in the path a century earlier.

The survey’s Chapter 1 is more precise about the geography. The tornado first developed just west of the Arkansas River near the Garrison Street Bridge, on the Oklahoma side of the line near the confluence of the Arkansas and the Poteau — the same meeting of waters that had made Belle Point a fort and a landing. It moved into the downtown business district, then northeast into an industrial area, then into the north-side houses, then across the river again into west Van Buren about 11:18 p.m. Half a mile wide. F2 through the historic district, F3 as it entered the industrial and residential ground, winds estimated near 200 miles an hour. Train cars overturned. Roofs and walls torn off well-built houses.

I wrote Riverside’s plant in another essay. This one is the town’s night: Garrison Avenue, the Eads Brothers Furniture building that took the wind and then, on April 24, the fire when the utilities came back, three neighbors gone with it. Griffin Properties losing downtown buildings. A business district walked by a storm on a Sunday, which officials later called luck. Luck is a small word. Use it for the empty offices. Do not use it for the north side.

## Two tornados in the civic file

1898 already tore Fort Smith and killed more than fifty people. The Encyclopedia keeps that storm. 1996 is the one that hit a furniture memory that was still standing as brick. People who write only 1996 as origin-of-decline are forgetting 1898 and forgetting the national import wave. People who write 1996 as only weather are forgetting Girard’s row and the Eads sign.

The honest stack: a city that puts warehouses and showrooms on a river and a downtown will, sooner or later, meet a storm that uses the same map. The factories chose the map in 1885, when Ballman’s shop moved toward the river. The storm did not need a thesis.

Sunday night is an industrial fact. The Fed’s 2001 rebuild story noted the luck of a late Sunday: the storm walked through a business district after the Saturday crowds and before Monday’s shift. For Riverside’s fifty upholstery workers, Sunday-night luck means they were not in the building. Luck is a word that should stay small. The north-side children were not in a plant and they were not lucky.

## What burned after the wind

The *Democrat-Gazette*’s thirty-year piece, crediting the Museum, is careful about Eads: minor tornado damage, then a fire three days later. That sequence matters. A lot of “tornado wrecked the furniture building” talk collapses the days. Collapse is how insurance and memory both go wrong. Write the days.

The NWS notes that shortly after the tornado formed it destroyed a high-voltage line crossing the river, blacking out much of west and north Fort Smith. Sirens were not sounded. A power loss at the police department cut the primary communications path used to decide on the sirens. That is a warning-story sentence, not a furniture sentence. It belongs here because the same outage is the weather that later made the Eads fire possible when utilities came back on April 24.

Downtown historic buildings that were four and five stories became lots. The Fed’s 2001 rebuild story is the civic after. This essay does not need the whole rebuild. It needs the fact that a furniture-named building on Garrison was in the photograph set the Museum still holds. Rights required to show it. Fact allowed without the file.

## Industrial, then residential

The NWS notes warehouses and industrial buildings east of the river just north of downtown, rail cars off tracks, a wastewater plant. That is the strip where a row would live. Girard’s Riverfront Drive names — Ayers, Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey, Fort Smith Chair — sat in that kind of geography. Riverside’s upholstery plant, 80,000 square feet and about fifty people, sat in it too. The Fed named the plant loss: two million dollars, Buddy Spradlin talking. Keep those numbers in the Riverside essay. Keep the strip in this one.

Then the north-side houses, Morrison Elementary damaged in the civic file, the deaths. A factory history that stops at the strip is a factory history that used the storm as decoration and refused the neighborhood. Mention the neighborhood. Then return to the strip if the essay is still about furniture. Both sentences can fit.

Van Buren took the same storm into west-side houses, a junior high, a new fire station, tractor-trailers overturned on Interstate 40. Furniture history does not own Van Buren. The path does.

## 1996 is not 1912

Do not let a tornado become the only date in the file. Table 8 is 1912. Ott is 1878. The peak window is circa 1960. Whirlpool’s close is 2012. 1996 is a wound and a camera. It is not the birth of the industry and not, by itself, the death. Girard’s “opened a door” is a director looking at lots and at a national market. Keep his sentence. Keep the other dates.

The NWS outbreak language is larger than Fort Smith: supercells from northwest Texas and southwest Oklahoma, a watch hours earlier, a later tornado at St. Paul in Madison County that killed two more people about fifty miles northeast. Do not drag those miles onto Riverfront Drive. The furniture fact is local: downtown, industrial, river — the map the factories chose.

The warning file is civic night, not factory night, and still belongs in a paragraph. The Storm Prediction Center had identified the potential two days out. A tornado watch covered Fort Smith and St. Paul three hours before the downtown touchdown. A severe-thunderstorm warning sixteen minutes prior mentioned the watch and the chance of tornadoes. A tornado warning four minutes prior arrived as the police department lost power and the sirens stayed silent. Early media coverage and hospital and senior-home plans, the survey said, still got people into shelter. I will not write the roar. I will write that Sunday night had a paper trail before it had a furniture photograph.

Riverside lost an 80,000-square-foot upholstery plant, about fifty people, two million dollars. Keep the rebuild in the Riverside essay. Keep the empty loading doors here. A campus can rebuild next door. A row of separately owned brick cannot. That split is why 1996 is both a company kink and a district ending. Do not let one eat the other.

Still frame: a lot, or a brick with no roof, no skyline hero, no cello. If we cannot pull a Fort Smith lot, we do not substitute Joplin or another city’s storm. Empty is better than a wrong town. The path was this town’s path. The furniture was in it because the furniture had always been in the geography the wind used. That is the only causation I will write. The rest is weather, and weather does not owe us a plot.
