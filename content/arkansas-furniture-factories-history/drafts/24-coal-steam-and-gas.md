Billy Higgins put the ingredients on one bench: coal in the district, hardwood on the ridges, therefore steam engines, therefore a town that could run power equipment. Fort Smith furniture, in that telling, is not a style. It is a BTU problem that got solved locally.

A lathe does not turn because a founder is virtuous. It turns because a belt is moving, and a belt moves because a shaft is turning, and a shaft turns because a boiler or a later motor says so. Ott’s Indiana machinery in 1878 assumed a twist. The town had to supply the twist.

The Encyclopedia’s civic timing is the other half of Higgins’s bench. Railroad transportation arrived in the 1870s and gave the city an alternative to the river. In the 1880s the population nearly tripled — 3,099 in 1880, 11,311 in 1890 — and Garrison Avenue became the wholesale and retail center of the region. An electric streetcar network grew with the city. Natural gas was discovered in the area in 1887 and later attracted manufacturers, “namely a notable glass-manufacturing industry.” Furniture manufacturing “also became increasingly central.” Coal, hardwood, rails, gas: the power menu lengthened in the same decades the furniture row thickened. I will not drop a mine name I have not checked. Western Arkansas coal is a documented industrial fact. Proximity is the point.

## Coal first

Western Arkansas coal is a documented industrial fact; I will not drop a mine name I have not checked. The point is proximity. You can haul coal farther than you can haul cheap hardwood and still make the math work, but a furniture town that sits near both does not have to choose. Steam is hungry in the winter and hungrier when you add dry kilns.

Kilns are the second boiler. Green gum will not be a dresser tomorrow. North Arkansas Wood Products’ 1946 Moore kiln at St. Joe is a later, documented picture of the same hunger: ninety thousand feet of capacity, eleven 48-inch fans for cross-circulation, a steam boiler fired on mill waste and sawdust. Railroad cars rolled a short length of rail from the sawmill into the kiln, loaded with green lumber. Earl Martin’s plant is not a Fort Smith 1885 boiler log. It is the physics written down because a later factory sat still long enough for the Arkansas Register to describe it. Scrap from the saws is not waste if it keeps the steam up. Furniture towns burn their mistakes and call it efficiency.

Harris and Maxwell, in the forest chapters of Bulletin 106, were already worried about a different fire: the kind that runs through cut-over pine and kills the seedlings. They estimated Arkansas’s merchantable timber at 78.7 billion board feet and the yearly cut of all products at about five billion, more than the annual growth. Furniture’s closed heat loop — burn the offcut, dry the next load — is not the conservation they were asking for. It is a plant staying warm without buying as much coal. The moral is mixed. The loop is real.

## Gas in 1887

The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* dates natural gas in the Fort Smith area to 1887 and ties it to a glass industry. Furniture was already in town. Gas does not invent a furniture factory. It changes the cost of heat and, later, the civic mix. A glass house wants steady, clean heat. A furniture house wants the same for finish rooms and kilns once the piping exists. I will not claim a specific plant converted in a specific year. I will claim the city’s power menu got longer.

Glass and furniture then sit beside each other as mid-sized factory trades that are not farms and not the fort. The later essay on that pairing can take the objects — a mirror for a dresser, a pane for a storefront. This essay stays with the burn.

## Electricity as a quiet thief

Line-shaft shops photograph well. Electric motors do not, which is why people write as if factories stopped being interesting when the belts came down. A motor on each machine is a different labor and a different maintenance and a different fire map. Mid-century Riverside is already in that world. The peak Higgins named — late 1950s, early 1960s — is an electric peak as much as a furniture peak.

Norge in 1962 is an electric-and-gas industrial object of another order. The Encyclopedia dates the refrigerator, freezer, and air-conditioner plant to February 1, 1962, and Whirlpool’s purchase to 1968. The appliance plant did not replace steam with a speech. It replaced a scale. Furniture’s power story is the small story that made the large story possible: a town used to buying and burning fuel for factories.

The 1996 tornado later showed what a power line is worth to an industrial strip. The NWS assessment records a high-voltage transmission line across the Arkansas going down and taking power from much of west and north Fort Smith. Sirens were not sounded. A furniture campus that had rebuilt with reinforced concrete shelters, if the Fed’s Spradlin paraphrase holds, had learned a later lesson about fuel and cover. That lesson is 1996, not 1887. The older lesson is simpler: no twist, no dresser.

## Waste as a fuel policy

Harris and Maxwell worried, in the forest chapters, about stave men who took the best of a white oak and left the rest. A furniture factory is the opposite kind of sinner and saint: it wants more of the tree, and it will burn what it cannot sell. That is not conservation in the modern sense. It is a closed heat loop that kept a plant from buying as much coal. The moral is mixed. The loop is real.

A present-tense shop with a dust collector and an electric wide-belt is living after this story. It still has a power bill. It still has scrap. It does not have a river-bank boiler. Do not pretend a 65-foot Warren shop is a steam plant. Do notice that the scrap pile is still a decision.

The 1898 tornado, which the Encyclopedia says killed more than fifty people, is the older reminder that a factory town’s heat and glass and roofs share a weather. The 1996 path through downtown and the industrial strip is the later reminder. Power is not only a boiler. It is a line that can go down on a Sunday night and take the sirens with it.

## Against the picturesque stack

A brick chimney is a documentary temptation. Use it if we have one we can credit. Do not use a stock mill chimney from another state. Do not put a boy with a coal scuttle in the frame. The power story is enough: coal, then gas, then a grid, always scrap.

Fort Smith became a furniture hub because it could turn iron. It could turn iron because it could boil water and, later, because it could pipe gas and buy current. The dressers are the pretty output. The stack is the input. The *Times Record* interviews still start the manufacturing story with coal, hardwood, and steam. Keep that order. The stain room comes after the fire is lit.

Still frame: brick that has been hot, or a belt scar on a ceiling. If Keith’s library has either, from Fort Smith or from a South Arkansas mill, pull it and say which. Analog heat is allowed. A fake 1880s fireman is not.
