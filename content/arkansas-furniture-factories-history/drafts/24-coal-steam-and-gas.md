Billy Higgins put the ingredients on one bench: coal in the district, hardwood on the ridges, therefore steam engines, therefore a town that could run power equipment. Fort Smith furniture, in that telling, is not a style. It is a BTU problem that got solved locally.

<!-- PHOTO: fig-01 D:\BBF\BBF Photos — boiler brick, stack, or mill waste pile (filename pending shop pull) -->

A lathe does not turn because a founder is virtuous. It turns because a belt is moving, and a belt moves because a shaft is turning, and a shaft turns because a boiler or a later motor says so. Ott’s Indiana machinery in 1878 assumed a twist. The town had to supply the twist.

## Coal first

Western Arkansas coal is a documented industrial fact; I will not drop a mine name I have not checked. The point is proximity. You can haul coal farther than you can haul cheap hardwood and still make the math work, but a furniture town that sits near both does not have to choose. Steam is hungry in the winter and hungrier when you add dry kilns.

Kilns are the second boiler. Green gum will not be a dresser tomorrow. North Arkansas Wood Products’ 1946 Moore kiln — ninety thousand feet, eleven 48-inch fans, a steam boiler fired on mill waste and sawdust — is a later, documented picture of the same hunger. I do not have the 1885 Ballman plant’s firing log. I have the physics. Scrap from the saws is not waste if it keeps the steam up. Furniture towns burn their mistakes and call it efficiency.

## Gas in 1887

The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* dates natural gas in the Fort Smith area to 1887 and ties it to a glass industry. Furniture was already in town. Gas does not invent a furniture factory. It changes the cost of heat and, later, the civic mix. A glass house wants steady, clean heat. A furniture house wants the same for finish rooms and kilns once the piping exists. I will not claim a specific plant converted in a specific year. I will claim the city’s power menu got longer.

Glass and furniture then sit beside each other as mid-sized factory trades that are not farms and not the fort. The later essay on that pairing can take the objects — a mirror for a dresser, a pane for a storefront. This essay stays with the burn.

## Electricity as a quiet thief

Line-shaft shops photograph well. Electric motors do not, which is why people write as if factories stopped being interesting when the belts came down. A motor on each machine is a different labor and a different maintenance and a different fire map. Mid-century Riverside is already in that world. The peak Higgins named is an electric peak as much as a furniture peak.

Norge in 1962 is an electric-and-gas industrial object of another order. The appliance plant did not replace steam with a speech. It replaced a scale. Furniture’s power story is the small story that made the large story possible: a town used to buying and burning fuel for factories.

## Waste as a fuel policy

Harris and Maxwell worried, in the forest chapters, about stave men who took the best of a white oak and left the rest. A furniture factory is the opposite kind of sinner and saint: it wants more of the tree, and it will burn what it cannot sell. That is not conservation in the modern sense. It is a closed heat loop that kept a plant from buying as much coal. The moral is mixed. The loop is real.

A present-tense shop with a dust collector and an electric wide-belt is living after this story. It still has a power bill. It still has scrap. It does not have a river-bank boiler. Do not pretend a 65-foot Warren shop is a steam plant. Do notice that the scrap pile is still a decision.

## Against the picturesque stack

A brick chimney is a Ken Burns temptation. Use it if we have one we can credit. Do not use a stock mill chimney from another state. Do not put a boy with a coal scuttle in the frame. The power story is enough: coal, then gas, then a grid, always scrap.

Fort Smith became a furniture hub because it could turn iron. It could turn iron because it could boil water and, later, because it could pipe gas and buy current. The dressers are the pretty output. The stack is the input. This pack will not forget the stack just because the dressers photograph better.

Still frame: brick that has been hot, or a belt scar on a ceiling. If Keith’s library has either, from Fort Smith or from a South Arkansas mill, pull it and say which. Analog heat is allowed. A fake 1880s fireman is not.
