Arkansas Best Corporation was a Fort Smith name that knew trailers. In 1966, according to the compiled Udouj sketch, it bought the merged Riverside and Twin Rivers houses. I want a 1966 clipping before I treat the price as real. I am willing, now, to treat the pairing as the sentence the town was already living: furniture and freight had been one industrial weather since the first carload of dressers left the river.

The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* lists ABF Freight System, a less-than-truckload carrier and Arkansas Best subsidiary, among the hometown companies that defined mid-century Fort Smith alongside Baldor and the Norge/Whirlpool plant. Many of those hometown companies, the entry says, can trace their origins to the 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s. Furniture is older. Trucking is the later fame. The 1966 purchase is the moment the later fame bought the older fame.

The *Times Record* in 2019 still had the pairing in the metro numbers. Trade, transportation, and utilities were the largest employment sector in the Fort Smith area, about twenty-three percent. Manufacturing was second, sixteen to eighteen percent depending on the season. Mervin Jebaraj, at the University of Arkansas Center for Business and Economic Research, told an Invest Fort Smith summit that the metro still had more manufacturing jobs than any other Arkansas metro — and that the manufacturing count had peaked at 31,000 in 1999, then fallen. Those are later figures. They are still the civic weather a 1966 purchase pointed toward: a town that makes objects and a town that moves objects, counted as two sectors that live in the same parking lots.

## Why a freight company wants a factory

Vertical integration is the textbook word and I will use it once. A carrier that owns a shipper can fill space it already has to run. A factory that owns a carrier — or is owned by one — can argue about damage in the same building. Mid-century conglomerates also bought things because they could, because the tax year looked right, because a local board wanted a local deal instead of an out-of-state one.

I do not know which reason won in 1966. I know the objects and the trailers were already neighbors. John Ayers, a year later, would tell a court that ICC rules decided how two furniture plants could share a trailer. Arkansas Best lived those rules for a living. Buying Riverside is, among other things, buying a reason to understand furniture cubes from the inside.

Harris and Maxwell, in 1912, had already priced the older version of the same intimacy. Furniture squares and wagon stock, they wrote, paid about thirty-four percent more freight than boards from a central Ozark shipping point. Dimension is expensive to move. Finished furniture is more expensive still, and more fragile. A hub is a place where that tax is still worth paying. A trucking corporation that buys a furniture house is a later chapter of the same tax.

Higgins told the *Times Record* that Fort Smith grew as a manufacturing town because railroad transportation became popular and because coal and hardwood sat nearby. The 1870s rails gave the city an alternative to the river. The 1922 automobile bridge at the west end of Garrison, dated in the Encyclopedia, gave it a rubber crossing into Oklahoma. By 1966 the vehicle had changed again. LTL is how a furniture town stays a freight town after the siding thins.

## What happens to a stencil under a parent

Dealers still said Riverside. Consumers still said Riverside. The parent’s name sat in the annual report. That split is ordinary and still worth saying, because local memory collapses it. “Riverside was Arkansas Best” is true as paper and false as a shopping sentence. The finish room did not become a trucking finish room. The trucking terminal did not become a stain room. The board, if the purchase holds, became a place where both arguments had to sit.

Herman Udouj’s later civic life — clubs, farm, hospital boards — reads like a man who remained a local face after the sale. That is a guess until an officer list says so. Many founders stay as presidents of subsidiaries. Many do not. Do not write a handshake photograph you have not seen.

## 1966 in the town’s other furniture story

The same year-ish climate produced Ayers Furniture Industries (1967) and the beginning of Ballman-Cummings’s end. One house was being bought by a larger Fort Smith corporation. Another pair of houses was being folded by a family into a partnership. The row was consolidating from both directions: outside paper and inside paper. Higgins’s peak — late 1950s, early 1960s, then a fade — sits immediately behind. Whirlpool’s purchase of Norge is 1968. The late sixties are when Fort Smith’s manufacturing identity begins to tilt from dressers to compressors without admitting it yet.

The Encyclopedia’s civic list in those decades is the tilt written as success: Baldor motors, ABF trailers, a refrigerator plant that would employ thousands, a city that had spent the 1950s trying not to live on Fort Chaffee. Riverside under Arkansas Best is furniture remaining inside that list, not furniture leaving it. The *Times Record* later still named Riverside among the furniture houses that had lasted into living memory, and still named the Riverfront Drive row that had not.

Girard, speaking as director of the Fort Smith Museum of History, counted about two dozen furniture manufacturers along that riverfront. A trucking parent does not erase those doors. It does change which door a later newspaper can still find.

## What the purchase does not mean

It does not mean Riverside stopped being a furniture company. It does not mean ABF delivered every suite. It does not mean a Warren shop has a trucking ancestor. It does not mean ArcBest, the later name, owes a product placement.

It does mean that any honest Riverside history after 1966 has to read like a subsidiary history until a later sale or spin says otherwise. I have not mapped the full ownership chain to the present High Point showroom company. That chain belongs in `verify` until someone walks the filings. Present-tense marketing that says “since 1946” is a founding year, not an ownership year.

Do not invent a purchase price. Do not invent share terms. Do not write Herman into an officer’s chair after the sale unless an officer list says so. The compiled sketch and the civic obituaries are not that list.

## Freight as the older partner

This pack’s rail essay will take the 1912 rates and the mixed car. This essay’s job is the 1966 moral: in Fort Smith, the furniture industry was never only makers. It was movers. A city that outfitted wagons in 1850 and sold LTL in 1966 is the same city changing vehicles. Arkansas Best buying Riverside is a family reunion the families did not plan.

The *Times Record* in 2019 still named manufacturing and freight as the paired civic muscles. Jebaraj’s warning at that summit was that Fort Smith should not treat manufacturing as something that happened before. A 1966 purchase of a furniture house by a freight corporation is one early version of that pairing written as a deal. It is not a guarantee that the pairing would last in the same objects.

Still frame: a trailer, a dock plate, no logo we do not have rights to. If the library has a crate and a bill in one picture, that is the purchase without a boardroom. Boardrooms photograph as power. Docks photograph as the reason the power wanted the plant.
