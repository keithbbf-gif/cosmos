Two rivers meet at Fort Smith. The company names knew it. Riverside first, then Twin Rivers Furniture Corporation, a second factory the compiled Udouj sketch places a few years after 1946. I have not held a Twin Rivers catalog. I have the pattern: a family that had one roof built a second roof rather than asking the first crew to be every object.

The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* puts the geography under older names than any letterhead. Belle Point is the bluff on the Arkansas just north of the Poteau. French place-names — Poteau, Belle Point, Massard Prairie — sit in the entry as evidence of trappers who used the confluence before the fort, before the wagons, before the furniture. Twin Rivers is that habit turned into a second corporation. It is almost too on the nose, which is why a later writer should not lean on it for a film title.

Ballman had done the two-roof problem with letterheads — folding beds in 1902, couch and bedding in 1904, kitchen cabinets, metal products in 1917. The Udoujs did it with a second corporation that still sounded like water. Specialization, or overflow, or a tax story I do not have. Until a plant list appears, stay with the fact of two names and a later merge.

The compiled sketch’s other number sits across the same years. Between 1949 and 1968, it says, they tripled revenue to about thirteen million dollars. Fort Smith’s census in those decades is the civic rhyme: 47,942 in 1950, 52,991 in 1960, 62,802 in 1970. Households were still being added. Higgins’s furniture peak — late 1950s, early 1960s — sits inside that climb. A second factory in those years is a bet that the ridge will hold.

## Why a second factory instead of a bigger one

A bigger one is a bet that the same product will keep selling. A second one is a bet that you need a different product, a different shift culture, or a firewall if a fire or a union vote goes wrong. Twin Rivers may have been upholstery. It may have been case goods that would not fit on 6th Street. It may have been a name to sell a line the way Ayers later sold two plants under one partnership. I will not pick.

The compiled sketch’s thirteen million crosses the merge and the Arkansas Best purchase, so the “they” is already a moving target. Mark it as a claim that needs a 1960s business page. If it holds, it is a mid-century furniture number that explains why a trucking corporation would want the plants. If it does not hold, the two names still existed and the later selling word was still Riverside.

John Ayers, a year after the 1966 climate, would tell a court that ICC rules decided how two furniture corporations could share a trailer. The Udouj merge is the other Fort Smith answer to two roofs: put them under one buyer instead of one partnership. I do not have the 1966 employee memo that would tell which answer the crews preferred. I have the pattern. Fewer letterheads. One package for a banker. A rumor on Monday.

## 1966

In 1966, the sketch says, the two companies merged and were purchased by Arkansas Best Corporation. The next essay takes the buyer. This one takes the merge as an industrial event. Two roofs become one selling story. Dealers meet fewer letterheads. Bankers meet one package. Crews meet a rumor.

The Encyclopedia lists ABF Freight System — a less-than-truckload carrier and Arkansas Best subsidiary — among the hometown companies that defined mid-century Fort Smith alongside Baldor and the Norge/Whirlpool plant. Furniture is older. Trucking is the later fame. The merge is the moment the older fame got packaged for the later fame to buy.

Mergers on a shop floor are not civic. They are whose superintendent stays, whose stencil wins, whether Twin Rivers becomes a building without a name. I do not have the 1966 employee memo. I will not write one. I will say that the word Riverside is the word that won. Twin Rivers survives as a footnote and as geography.

1966 is also the year before Ayers Furniture Industries. Fort Smith’s older houses were consolidating at the same moment the newer house was being bought. I cannot prove a coincidence. I can name a climate: the 1960s were when Fort Smith furniture stopped looking like a row of independent letterheads and started looking like fewer, larger, more paper-heavy objects. Norge had already opened on February 1, 1962. Whirlpool would buy that plant in 1968. The peak Higgins named sits just behind that climate.

## Confluence as a habit

Fort Smith’s whole manufacturing story is confluence: river and rail, wood and iron, furniture and then appliances, Arkansas and Oklahoma. Twin Rivers is the habit turned into a second corporation. Lean on the payroll problem instead of the water. Two factories are two sets of dry kilns, two insurance policies, two places a tornado can hit. In 1996 the National Weather Service’s path ran through an industrial strip after downtown. The St. Louis Fed later wrote that Riverside lost an 80,000-square-foot upholstery plant that employed about fifty people. Whether that plant sat on Twin Rivers’ old footprint I do not know. The risk was always plural.

The *Times Record* in 2019 still used one surviving word — Riverside — for the company that had manufactured until a few years earlier and was then a distribution point. Twin Rivers is not in that sentence. A second corporation can do its work and then disappear into the first corporation’s name. That disappearance is the merge.

## Against a dynasty caption

Herman and Rudolph as founding brothers is a usable sentence if the 1936 and 1946 sources hold. It is not a reason to write a family saga. Arkansas Best’s purchase is a reason to write the opposite: the family factory became a subsidiary. Mid-century Fort Smith is full of that move. Local names enter larger paper. ABF Freight is the famous version. Riverside is the furniture version.

Bradley Brand Furniture is an LLC that never merged with a second river. Do not rhyme them. The only honest rhyme is two products under one county’s freight bill — islands and boards, not Riverside and Twin Rivers.

## What to pull for a picture

Two doors. Or water that is actually two waters, captioned as geography, not as a logo. If the library has a Twin Rivers stencil, that is a find and should be treated like a Border Queen label: photograph first, restore later. If not, the merge is a paper event and can live without a portrait of smiling brothers. Smiling brothers are how this century gets turned into a pageant.

Girard’s later riverfront list — Ayers, Garrison, DeSoto, Ward, Covey, Fort Smith Chair — does not include Twin Rivers. That absence is not proof the second factory sat off the river. It is proof that the spoken skyline kept the winning word. The *Times Record* in 2019 still had furniture row on Riverfront Drive as a memory and Riverside as a living company name. Twin Rivers lives in the compiled sketch and in the water.

Still frame: two doors, or two waters captioned as geography. The compiled sketch’s 1949–1968 climb, if it holds, is the reason a buyer wanted both roofs in one package. The Encyclopedia’s 1970 census — 62,802 — is the city that package sat inside. Twin Rivers is the name that did not walk out of the lawyer’s office. Riverside is. Leave the brothers out of the picture until a stencil says otherwise.
