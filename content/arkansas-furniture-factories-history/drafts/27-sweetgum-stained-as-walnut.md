Red gum, Harris and Maxwell wrote in 1912, had risen from obscurity to prominence. Once considered practically worthless, it now stood high among furniture and finish woods. It could be finished to imitate Circassian walnut, oak, mahogany, and other expensive cabinet woods. If you took rotary-cut veneer and picked the figure, few could tell it from Circassian walnut.

That is not a craft secret. It is a government bulletin saying the quiet part in public. Arkansas produced more red gum lumber than any other state, they said, and half the mill output was further manufactured at home. Fort Smith’s furniture century sat on that half.

They also wrote the sentence the catalog would have liked better. Its own grain, finished naturally from carefully selected logs, was scarcely surpassed by any wood of this country. In Europe the same tree was known as satin walnut. The factory did not have to fake a foreign wood. It often did anyway, because a dealer in a hurry wanted a name the parlor already knew.

Table 8 is the policy’s math sitting next to the boast. Red gum: 9,061,000 board feet, 58.94 percent of Arkansas furniture wood, $20.29 a thousand at the factory. White oak: 5,586,000 feet, 36.34 percent, $32.45 a thousand. Together about ninety-five percent. Red oak a distant third at 346,000 feet. Cherry, they said, was too scarce in Arkansas to matter; the commercial supply lived in Pennsylvania and other Northern states. Black walnut’s in-state cut was small, and most of what was further manufactured at home still went into furniture — a leftover fame, not a forest. Mahogany appears as imported desire. The folklore woods are not the factory woods.

## The tree the catalog would not name

Sweetgum is a bottoms tree, *Liquidambar styraciflua*, interlocked grain, a mind of its own when you plane it. Marketing departments still flinch at the word. “Gum” sounds like chew. “Red gum” was the trade name that let a factory put it on a tally. “Circassian walnut” was the name that let a dealer put it in a parlor.

I do not need to retell tyloses and movement. One factory sentence is enough: interlocked grain will pick out under a dull knife, and a finish line answers with speed, sandpaper, and filler. The factory consequence is larger than the plane. A town that could stain could sell fashion without a fashion forest. Walnut in Arkansas was already scarce in their telling. Cherry too. Oak as a face was expensive. Gum as a face was a finish problem, and finish problems are what a hub hires.

Eighty-five percent of Table 8’s furniture wood was grown in Arkansas. The gum majority of that home-grown diet is the reason a western household in 1910 could afford a dark parlor suite that said walnut in the catalog. Higgins’s Fort Smith — one of three U.S. furniture hubs, in his later telling — sat on a bottoms tree and a stain department. Ballman came from Indianapolis for hardwood and steam, not for a Circassian forest.

## Theater as an industrial policy

Faking a wood is easy to sneer at from a hand-tool bench. It is also how a western household in 1910 got a “walnut” suite it could pay for. The lie is in the name. The object is still a gum object, with gum’s movement and gum’s pore, and it will tell on itself in a dry house. Factories knew. They still stained. Dealers knew. They still ordered.

Border Queen cabinets that “looked like oak” in a catalog are this policy in a kitchen. Ballman-Cummings suites that photographed dark are this policy in a dining room. I will not open a specific piece I have not opened. I will say the policy was statewide, counted, and priced.

The price gap in Table 8 is the policy’s math. Oak at $32.45 a thousand. Gum at $20.29. Stain is cheaper than the twelve-dollar difference if your labor is a finish line and not a one-off.

Shortleaf pine’s “small showing” in furniture, they wrote, was remarkable given how much other Arkansas industry demanded it. Three-quarters of the state’s manufacturing wood could be pine and the finish rooms would still want a hardwood they could stain. Warren’s mills, in the Encyclopedia’s 1907 figures, could run 100,000 to 150,000 board feet a day and still ship furniture *stock* rather than furniture. The stain theater is a Fort Smith job. The pine belt’s job was boards.

## Interlock, tear, the thing the stain cannot hide

Gum tears. A factory answers with volume. A small shop answers with a sharper iron and a decision not to fake walnut today. Both answers are honest in their own buildings.

When a later owner strips a “walnut” dresser and finds gum, the disappointment is a 1912 success. Harris and Maxwell predicted gum might in time be as valuable as white oak. They were writing as boosters of a species. The market, later, often went the other way: gum as paint-grade, oak as the word people still want. The factories of 1912 were already living in the future where the word and the tree had divorced.

Tupelo, in the same bulletin, is the cousin that almost tells the same story. Once little used, then discovered as a substitute for yellow poplar, often marketed as “bay poplar,” sometimes wrongly called black gum. The furniture table did not need tupelo to make its point. It needed gum and a customer who would not put a fingernail on the pore. The rest of the bulletin’s species list — hickory for handles, osage for posts and felloes, persimmon for shuttles — is Arkansas making other objects. Furniture’s object was a stained room.

Bulletin 106’s future-supply chapter was already nervous. The yearly cut of all products, they estimated, ran ahead of annual growth. Hardwoods, they thought, had a better chance than pine because they sprout after fire. Red gum was one of the species they expected to hold its place. They were writing as foresters who wanted the tree to have a next century. The furniture factories of 1912 were writing as buyers who wanted this year’s stain to hold. Those two writings are not the same job.

## Present tense, without a sermon

A Warren shop that shows sycamore and walnut and oak under their names is not morally superior to a 1912 finish room. It is a different customer. The customer who wants a named species will pay for the name. The customer who wanted a dark parlor paid for the dark. Do not scold the dead for stain.

Do scold the living caption that calls every dark Fort Smith piece “walnut.” Look at the pore. Look at the back. Look at the bulletin. If you cannot look, say “stained hardwood” and go home.

Harris and Maxwell collected the table first-hand from manufacturers. That is why it is still the lantern. Later employment figures — 31,000 manufacturing jobs in 1999 — are metro and mixed. Later company talk is talk. Table 8 is a count, and the count is mostly gum.

Still frame: a raw gum board with a streak of walnut-colored stain wiped across it, no pretty room. If the library has that wipe, the essay is done in one picture. If not, the bulletin’s sentence is the picture. Few could tell. That “few” is the hub’s boast and the antique trade’s headache. Both are earned.
