Petit Jean Mountain looks, in a brochure, like a reason. Sound-Craft Systems, in the company’s own telling, has been building lecterns, portable sound, and presentation furniture there since 1947, above Morrilton, above the river valley. I have not seen the 1947 charter. I will treat the year as their claim and the object as real enough to need an essay.

A lectern is a case good with a slope and a public life. Churches, schools, courtrooms, hotel ballrooms — the customer is an institution, like Turney’s customer, at a smaller permanent scale. Turney wanted a thousand sanctuaries a year at its height. Sound-Craft, in later public listings, is a handful of people. That is not a fall from grace I can plot without a mid-century payroll. It is a reminder that Arkansas furniture includes shops that stayed shops.

## Materials without a sermon

The company’s copy lists solid hardwoods, veneers, plastic laminates, carpet. That is a factory list. Laminate is not a sin. Carpet on a podium is a rental-hall fact. A pack that only praises oak pews will miss the object that actually stands in a Holiday Inn meeting room. Fort Smith’s 1912 diet was gum and oak. A 1970s lectern may be lauan and laminate and honest about the job.

I will not invent a founder standing in the mist. The mountain is a location with a road and a freight problem. Getting a podium down Petit Jean is a cube problem with a grade. Getting it to a campus in another state is the same problem Fort Smith solved with a siding, solved here with a truck.

## 1947 in the statewide cluster

1946–47 is a cluster: Riverside, Turney, St. Joe’s kiln, Sound-Craft’s claimed start. Returning men, leftover wartime habit, oak still cheap enough, institutions still building. A writer who wants a single Arkansas furniture renaissance year can have 1946 if he admits it is a cluster, not a capital. Fort Smith was the old capital. These shops are the dispersion.

Sound-Craft’s later life — custom AV furniture, workstations, conference rooms — is a product shift toward offices. Offices are where church-furniture skills go when the sanctuary market thins. I do not have their year-by-year line book. The shift is visible in the present-tense copy. Mark it as copy.

## Not a row, still a factory

Girard’s two dozen doors are a district. Petit Jean is a pin on a map. Both make furniture. The SEO temptation is to drag Sound-Craft into “Fort Smith furniture history” because the user asked for Fort Smith. Resist. The user also asked for Arkansas. Morrilton is Arkansas. A lectern is furniture. The pack’s title is allowed to leave the river.

Turney made lecterns too. Do not merge. If a sanctuary has a lectern and a pew from the same decade, look for marks. Marks are how this pack stays out of the antique-forum business.

## Present tense

If the shop is still building, a later editor should say so with a date, not with “to this day.” I will not write “to this day.” I will write that public pages in the mid-2020s still describe a Morrilton shop with a 1947 claim. That is enough.

Bradley Brand Furniture is not a lectern house. The optional footnote is only the statewide habit: small Arkansas shops making institutional or kitchen objects after the row thinned. No bloodline.

Still frame: a sloped top, a microphone hole, no mountain sunset. If the library has a podium, even a shop-made analog, pull it and say where it was built. If not, leave the slot. The mountain will try to steal the picture. Let the slope win.
