Petit Jean Mountain looks, in a brochure, like a reason. Sound-Craft Systems, in the company’s own telling, has been building lecterns, portable sound, and presentation furniture there since 1947, above Morrilton, above the river valley. I have not seen the 1947 charter. I will treat the year as their claim and the object as real enough to need an essay.

A lectern is a case good with a slope and a public life. Churches, schools, courtrooms, hotel ballrooms — the customer is an institution, like Turney’s customer, at a smaller permanent scale. Turney wanted a thousand sanctuaries a year at its height. Sound-Craft, in later public listings, is a handful of people. That is not a fall from grace I can plot without a mid-century payroll. It is a reminder that Arkansas furniture includes shops that stayed shops.

The company’s mid-2020s public page still says lecterns and podiums, designed and built in the United States since 1947. It lists a physical address at 1313 Highway 64 West, Morrilton, a post-office box in the same town, weekday hours, a dealer code. Those are living-shop facts, not a founding myth. I will not invent the founder who first claimed the year. I will not invent a president who stood behind one of the slopes.

## Materials without a sermon

The company’s copy lists solid hardwoods, veneers, plastic laminates, carpet. That is a factory list. Laminate is not a sin. Carpet on a podium is a rental-hall fact. A history that only praises oak pews will miss the object that actually stands in a Holiday Inn meeting room. Fort Smith’s 1912 diet was gum and oak. A later lectern may be lauan and laminate and honest about the job.

The present-tense line list on the same site is broader than a church desk: roll-top mixing desks, workstations, carts, credenzas and rack cabinets, custom designs, rack boxes, options. That is presentation furniture as a trade, not a single sloped box. Offices and auditoriums buy racks the way congregations once bought pews. I do not have the year each line began. Mark the list as copy.

I will not invent a founder standing in the mist. The mountain is a location with a road and a freight problem. Getting a podium down Petit Jean is a cube problem with a grade. Getting it to a campus in another state is the same problem Fort Smith solved with a siding, solved here with a truck. Highway 64 is not Riverfront Drive. It is still a way out.

## 1947 in the statewide cluster

1946–47 is a cluster: Riverside on a Fort Smith letterhead, Turney in a Harrison garage, St. Joe’s Moore kiln completed, Sound-Craft’s claimed start above Morrilton. Returning men, leftover wartime habit, oak still cheap enough, institutions still building. Camp Chaffee had been activated in 1942; churches and schools and civic halls were still catching up with rooms that needed a place to stand and speak. A writer who wants a single Arkansas furniture renaissance year can have 1946 if he admits it is a cluster, not a capital. Fort Smith was the old capital. These shops are the dispersion.

Turney, in the Encyclopedia’s telling, made pulpits and lecterns as part of a laminated-oak church line and died at auction in 1968. Sound-Craft’s later life — custom AV furniture, workstations, conference rooms — is a product shift toward offices. Offices are where church-furniture skills go when the sanctuary market thins. I do not have their year-by-year line book. The shift is visible in the present-tense copy. Mark it as copy.

Do not merge the lecterns. If a sanctuary has a lectern and a pew from the same decade, look for marks. Marks are how this history stays out of the antique-forum business. Harrison’s object was a room. Morrilton’s object, in the later catalog, is a station: a slope, a mic hole, sometimes a rack.

## Not a row, still a factory

Girard’s two dozen doors are a district. Petit Jean is a pin on a map. Both make furniture. The search temptation is to drag Sound-Craft into Fort Smith furniture history because a reader asked for Fort Smith. Resist. The reader also asked for Arkansas. Morrilton is Arkansas. A lectern is furniture. The title is allowed to leave the river.

Institutional furniture is the statewide habit this cluster keeps naming. Turney’s pews. Sound-Craft’s slopes. Akin’s later hotel dressers in Monticello. McCourt’s folding stacks in Fort Smith. None of those objects is a parlor suite. All of them have a public job and a freight bill. Fort Smith Chair, if it sold institutional seating, was an ancestor in type, not in paper. Do not merge letterheads because the customer is a school.

Harris and Maxwell’s 1912 table is case-goods thinking: gum and oak by the million feet. A lectern eats those feet as a small case, or it eats veneer and laminate and does not apologize. Either sentence is a factory sentence. The mountain does not change the joinery.

Petit Jean is a state-park mountain in the civic mind and a grade in a truck’s mind. Morrilton sits in the river valley below. The company’s published address is not “the mountain” as a miracle. It is Highway 64 West, a numbered road a crate can use. Fort Smith’s factories wanted a siding because the Arkansas and the rails were the way out. A lectern shop wants a road that will take a slope to a campus without pretending the view is the product.

Camp Chaffee, activated March 27, 1942, had already put a large institutional payroll into western Arkansas. Churches, schools, and civic halls were still catching up with rooms that needed a place to stand and speak. 1947, if the claim holds, is a year those rooms were still being built. I will not invent a chapel order. I will say the customer class — institutions — is the same class Turney later rode at a thousand churches a year. Scale differed. The slope is the shared object.

A present-tense dealer code and an 800 number are factory facts of a later kind. They mean the shop still expects someone to order a rack. They do not prove 1947. They do not prove a headcount. Leave the year in verify until a filing or a local history agrees. Leave the object in the essay because the object is on the page.

Turney’s 1965 hemisphere boast and Sound-Craft’s 1947 claim are both company sentences. One is in an Encyclopedia paraphrase of a newsletter. One is on a living shop’s home page. Treat both as documents. Do not let the louder sentence eat the quieter shop. A handful of people making lecterns in Morrilton, if that is the later scale, is still a factory fact. It is not a fall from Harrison I can plot without a mid-century Sound-Craft payroll I do not have.

## Present tense

If the shop is still building, a later editor should say so with a date. Public pages in the mid-2020s still describe a Morrilton shop with a 1947 claim and a Highway 64 door. That is enough. Re-check the door in the year of publication. Present-tense factory sentences go stale on purpose.

Bradley Brand Furniture is not a lectern house. The optional footnote is only the statewide habit: small Arkansas shops making institutional or kitchen objects after the row thinned. No bloodline.

Still frame: a sloped top, a microphone hole, no mountain sunset. If the library has a podium, even a shop-made analog, pull it and say where it was built. If not, leave the slot. The mountain will try to steal the picture. Let the slope win.
