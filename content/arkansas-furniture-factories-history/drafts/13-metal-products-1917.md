Wood towns like to forget iron until a hinge fails. In 1917 Edward Ballman founded or co-founded Fort Smith Metal Products Company. The Historical Society lists it with the furniture houses, which is the tell: this was not a random foundry. It was the fittings the other letterheads had been buying from someone else.

1917 is also a war year. The United States entered in April. Boulden dates the other industrial fact of that year with more care than a furniture caption usually wants: on September 19, women employed as telephone operators at Southwestern Bell began a strike that lasted until late December and sparked a general strike that shut the whole city for nine days. Kyra Schmidt’s thesis follows those operators. I will not invent a munitions order for Metal Products to make the year feel important. I will not pretend the strike was about furniture. I will leave the date on the company and let a later researcher find the contracts. A war year is still a year when iron is expensive and spoken for.

Boulden’s population table puts 23,975 people in the city in 1910 and 28,870 in 1920. Metal Products opens in that climb, after the specialized wood houses of 1902, 1904, and 1906, before the 1921 selling cooperative. The calendar is a material calendar. Wood, then cloth, then a kitchen, then iron, then a single handshake for the dealer.

## Why a furniture man starts a metal house

Folding beds need hinges. Kitchen cabinets need bins and catches. Couches need springs and tacks. Beds that do not fold still need rails and bolts. Every one of those parts can be purchased from St. Louis or Chicago. Buying them locally is how a hub keeps the margin and the schedule. It is also how a hub survives a freight snarl.

Harris and Maxwell’s 1912 furniture table had already counted the wood those fittings would hold: 15,373,000 board feet, gum the majority, white oak the face, 85.44 percent grown in Arkansas. They did not count the kegs of tacks. A metal house is the part of the object the Forest Service could not see. Higgins, talking to the *Times Record*, put steel fittings on the same spinoff list as cloth and mirrors. Metal Products is that list with a charter date.

Higgins’s spinoff list — upholsterers, cloth, mirrors, steel fittings — is the civic version. Metal Products is the corporate version. Ballman had been splitting wood lines for fifteen years. Splitting into metal is the same habit crossing a material line. The 1921 Manufacturers Company would then try to sell the materials as one town.

I do not have a 1917 catalog. Cast beds were a national product; so were stamped rails; so were the small goods a finish room uses up by the keg. Until a letterhead or a city directory lists the SKUs, stay with the class: furniture metal, made in the furniture town.

## Labor that is not a lathe

A metal house wants different lungs. Foundry smoke if they cast. Press noise if they stamp. A burn that is not a wood burn. The furniture row’s labor pool does not automatically staff a metal shop. You hire men (and, later, women) who have been in other Fort Smith metal trades, or you train them and lose them to a better wage when a bigger plant opens.

Norge would not arrive until February 1, 1962, when Boulden says the company opened a factory for refrigerators, freezers, and air conditioners; Whirlpool bought it in 1968. Baldor’s motor story is mid-century. In 1917 the city’s metal world was smaller and more mixed: railroad shops, the fittings a river town always has, glass after the gas fields. Metal Products sat in that mix, not in a vacuum called “the furniture industry.”

A still frame for this shop is not a restored bed in a parlor. It is a latch, close, slightly greasy, or a stamped rail that has never been polished for a museum. If the library has only wood, leave the iron slot empty. Empty is better than a munitions photograph we do not have a right to and a contract we do not have.

## Glass town, furniture town, iron town

Natural gas in 1887 pulled a glass industry the *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* still bothers to mention. Furniture is listed beside it as increasingly central to the metropolitan economy. Iron is the third leg that essays like this one have to write in or the city becomes two pretty specialties. A mirror for a dresser is glass. A rail for a bed is iron. A case is gum and oak. The room is all three. Ballman’s company list, read in order, is a man assembling the room’s materials under Fort Smith names.

The automobile bridge at the west end of Garrison would open on May 11, 1922. Metal Products is five years earlier. The fittings still had to cross by the older means — rail, ferry, a wagon that this town used to build by the thousand. A metal house in a wagon town is not a paradox. It is the same freight argument in a harder material.

## What not to do with 1917

Do not write a patriotic paragraph about doughboys sleeping on Fort Smith beds unless you have an order. Fort Smith has a Spirit of the American Doughboy monument; Boulden mentions it among civic markers. A monument is not a purchase order. Do not use the general strike as proof that Metal Products was a labor pioneer. Do not merge this company with later Fort Smith steel or with Arkansas Best’s trucking. Do not give Bradley Brand Furniture a hardware origin story. A Warren shop buys hinges like everyone else unless a shop note says otherwise.

Do use 1917 as a reminder that factory history is dated. The year is not flavor. It is a constraint. Iron in 1917 had other buyers. Glass, after 1887, had its own plants. Furniture had its row. A metal house sat between those facts and tried to make the row less dependent on a keg from St. Louis. Whether it succeeded is a directory question. That it was founded is the Society’s sentence.

## The quiet present tense

Every island and cabinet that leaves a small Arkansas shop still lives or dies on fasteners. Confirmat, insert, bed bolt, a hinge that will not sag — the joinery pack in this repo already treated those as knockdown morals. This essay’s moral is historical: Fort Smith named the problem as a company in 1917. The problem did not begin there. It became local there.

A photograph of a latch, close, ugly, slightly greasy, is worth more than a restored bed in a parlor. If the library has only wood, leave the metal slot empty. Empty is better than a catalog scan we do not have rights to.

The Manufacturers Company, four years later, would try to make Metal Products and Border Queen and the couch house appear to a dealer as one handshake. That handshake is the next essay. This one is the cold part in the hand: the iron.

Girard’s later riverfront list did not say Metal Products. Spoken maps keep the mid-century doors. Keep the 1917 letterhead anyway. A hub that cannot name its iron is a hub telling on itself, the same way a hub that cannot name Schaburg is only a brick story. The fittings were the schedule. The wood was what the fittings were allowed to hold.
