Wood towns like to forget iron until a hinge fails. In 1917 Edward Ballman founded or co-founded Fort Smith Metal Products Company. The Historical Society lists it with the furniture houses, which is the tell: this was not a random foundry. It was the fittings the other letterheads had been buying from someone else.

1917 is also a war year. The United States entered in April. Fort Smith women at Southwestern Bell walked out in September and a general strike shut the city for nine days. I will not invent a munitions order for Metal Products to make the year feel important. I will not pretend the strike was about furniture. I will leave the date on the company and let a later researcher find the contracts. A war year is still a year when iron is expensive and spoken for.

## Why a furniture man starts a metal house

Folding beds need hinges. Kitchen cabinets need bins and catches. Couches need springs and tacks. Beds that do not fold still need rails and bolts. Every one of those parts can be purchased from St. Louis or Chicago. Buying them locally is how a hub keeps the margin and the schedule. It is also how a hub survives a freight snarl.

Higgins’s spinoff list — upholsterers, cloth, mirrors, steel fittings — is the civic version. Metal Products is the corporate version. Ballman had been splitting wood lines for fifteen years. Splitting into metal is the same habit crossing a material line. The 1921 Manufacturers Company would then try to sell the materials as one town.

I do not have a 1917 catalog. Cast beds were a national product; so were stamped rails; so were the small goods a finish room uses up by the keg. Until a letterhead or a city directory lists the SKUs, stay with the class: furniture metal, made in the furniture town.

## Labor that is not a lathe

A metal house wants different lungs. Foundry smoke if they cast. Press noise if they stamp. A burn that is not a wood burn. The furniture row’s labor pool does not automatically staff a metal shop. You hire men (and, later, women) who have been in other Fort Smith metal trades, or you train them and lose them to a better wage when a bigger plant opens.

Norge would not arrive until 1962. Baldor’s motor story is mid-century. In 1917 the city’s metal world was smaller and more mixed: railroad shops, the fittings a river town always has, glass after the gas fields. Metal Products sat in that mix, not in a vacuum called “the furniture industry.”

## Glass town, furniture town, iron town

Natural gas in 1887 pulled a glass industry the *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* still bothers to mention. Furniture is listed beside it. Iron is the third leg that essays like this one have to write in or the city becomes two pretty specialties. A mirror for a dresser is glass. A rail for a bed is iron. A case is gum and oak. The room is all three. Ballman’s company list, read in order, is a man assembling the room’s materials under Fort Smith names.

## What not to do with 1917

Do not write a patriotic paragraph about doughboys sleeping on Fort Smith beds unless you have an order. Do not use the general strike as proof that Metal Products was a labor pioneer. Do not merge this company with later Fort Smith steel or with Arkansas Best’s trucking. Do not give Bradley Brand Furniture a hardware origin story. A Warren shop buys hinges like everyone else unless a shop note says otherwise.

Do use 1917 as a reminder that factory history is dated. The year is not flavor. It is a constraint. Iron in 1917 had other buyers.

## The quiet present tense

Every island and cabinet that leaves a small Arkansas shop still lives or dies on fasteners. Confirmat, insert, bed bolt, a hinge that will not sag — the joinery pack in this repo already treated those as knockdown morals. This essay’s moral is historical: Fort Smith named the problem as a company in 1917. The problem did not begin there. It became local there.

A photograph of a latch, close, ugly, slightly greasy, is worth more than a restored bed in a parlor. If the library has only wood, leave the metal slot empty. Empty is better than a catalog scan we do not have rights to.

The Manufacturers Company, four years later, would try to make Metal Products and Border Queen and the couch house appear to a dealer as one handshake. That handshake is the next essay. This one is the cold part in the hand: the iron.
