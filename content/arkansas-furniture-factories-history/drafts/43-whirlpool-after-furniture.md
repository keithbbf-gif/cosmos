On February 1, 1962, the Norge Company opened a Fort Smith factory for refrigerators, freezers, and air conditioners. The *Encyclopedia of Arkansas* keeps the date. Whirlpool bought the plant in 1968 and expanded it. For a generation the civic story could say manufacturing and mean compressors as easily as dressers. Then, in October 2011, Whirlpool said it would close in mid-2012 and move production to Mexico and other North American sites. As recently as 2004 the plant had about 4,600 people. By 2011, about a thousand.

This is not a furniture essay that lost its way. This is the afterlife of a furniture town that successfully became something else and then lost that something else too. Higgins’s peak window and Norge’s opening overlap. The town did not wait for the row to die before it invited the next object. That invitation is the most important industrial decision after 1878, and it has nothing to do with Ballman’s lathe except that the lathe had already taught the city how to run a factory.

Higgins, talking to the *Times Record* in 2018, put Whirlpool at about 6,000 at its peak and said the town at one point had 34 manufacturers. The Encyclopedia’s 4,600 is a 2004 number. I will not reconcile them into one payroll. I will say the plant was huge, then smaller, then gone. Higgins also said Dixie Cup and Whirlpool had set up or were moving in as furniture faded, and that appliances, like furniture, demanded spinoffs — little shops and garages. The box shops that had crated dressers could crate refrigerators. That is a continuity of cube, not a continuity of letterhead.

## 31,000

Mervin Jebaraj, talking to the Invest Fort Smith Summit as the *Times Record* reported in 2019, said the metro’s manufacturing employment peaked at 31,000 in 1999, then lost 6,500 jobs by 2007 and 7,500 more after 2008. Those are mixed-manufacturing numbers: appliances, metals, food, the rest. They are not dresser numbers. They are the numbers that explain why a visitor in 2000 could still call Fort Smith a factory city after the row had thinned.

1999 is after the tornado and before the Whirlpool collapse. The city had already learned to live without a furniture skyline and still post a national-looking manufacturing rate. Then the appliance skyline went. The afterlife has an afterlife.

The same 2019 piece put manufacturing at 16 to 18 percent of Fort Smith metro employment, second behind trade, transportation, and utilities, and still the highest manufacturing share of any Arkansas metro. Jebaraj’s line, which the paper quoted, is the one to keep: do not think of manufacturing as something that happened before. High compared with the rest of Arkansas is one sentence. High compared with a lost row is another.

## Reuse, not resurrection

The 2019 paper said the massive Whirlpool building had been repurposed as warehouse space, and that one section had become the national headquarters for FFO Home, with mattresses made on site. I want a 2026 occupancy check before a present-tense caption. The shape is the point: a building that had been refrigerators became storage and sleep. Sleep is a furniture cousin. Couch and Bedding, 1904; Riverside’s upholstery plant; FFO’s mattresses — Fort Smith keeps returning to the bed. The buildings change owners. The need does not.

Warehouse is the other reuse. Distribution, the Riverside conversion, the leftover cube talent. A town that used to make objects becomes a town that moves objects. That is not nothing. It is not 1878.

Camp Chaffee, activated March 27, 1942, had already taught the city a large payroll that was not furniture. During the war the post trained armored divisions and held about 3,400 German prisoners of war in three compounds. After the war it was deactivated and activated many times. The Encyclopedia says the 1950s and 1960s were the years Fort Smith struggled and succeeded in becoming less reliant on the post. Norge is that success with a compressor. Baldor and ABF, hometown companies in the same telling, are motors and freight. The furniture town became a Sunbelt factory town. Carson’s 2013 thesis is the academic door on that conversion. This essay will not steal his argument. It will say the conversion had a date: February 1, 1962.

Natural gas, discovered in 1887, had already pulled glass manufacturers. Furniture had already become central to the metropolitan economy in the Encyclopedia’s early-twentieth-century telling. Norge did not arrive in an empty factory city. It arrived in a city that knew how to run a shift and wanted a larger object. Scale is the difference. A dresser line fits a riverfront brick. A refrigerator line wants a bay the furniture row never needed. The later reuse — warehouse, mattresses — is that bay looking for a third object. Sleep is a cousin. Storage is a cube. Neither is a suite.

## What furniture people should not claim

Do not claim Whirlpool as a furniture descendant. Do not claim the 4,600 as proof the row “became” appliances. Some sanders became assembler. Many did not. Labor markets are not reincarnation. Do not claim Bradley Brand Furniture has a Whirlpool story.

Do claim the sequence as civic: furniture hub, then appliance-and-motor-and-truck city, then a city arguing about what manufacturing still means. Chamber talk in 2019 about automation making the remaining jobs safer and higher paying is chamber talk. Keep it attributed. The plant that left is still the plant that left.

## Baldor, Rheem, the other names

The Encyclopedia and the *Times Record* scatter the other mid-century names: Baldor, Rheem, ABF, later Silgan, Glatfelter in a reused Mitsubishi building at Chaffee Crossing. The 2019 feature’s list is longer — Rockline, Dixie Cup, Ingersoll Rand, Mars, Gerdau, Bekaert in Van Buren. This essay will not become a chamber list. It will say the list exists so a furniture history cannot pretend the town emptied in 1965.

Higgins’s 34 manufacturers, if the count holds, is a city, not a row. Some of those doors were never furniture. Some of the furniture doors were already dark when Norge opened. The civic habit is to narrate one industry after another as if they were chapters of the same book. They shared a labor market and a rail yard. They did not share a stencil.

The 2018 paper’s closing civic note is Higgins calling Fort Smith the second-largest city in the state and attributing that rank largely to manufacturing. The Encyclopedia’s later census line is more cautious: after 2020 Fort Smith ranked third, Fayetteville second. I will not fight the ranks. I will say the civic boast and the census table are both trying to explain a factory city that outgrew its first object. Dressers, then compressors, then an argument about what manufacturing still means. FFO’s mattresses, if the 2019 occupancy still holds, are a small return to the first object’s cousin inside the second object’s bay. Confirm before a present-tense caption. The sequence does not need the mattress to be true. The sequence already has dates.

Still frame: a bay too tall for a dresser line, or a mattress in a rack. If we cannot photograph the old Whirlpool, we do not lift a news photo. We describe the scale. Scale is the difference between Riverfront Drive and Norge. Fort Smith learned the larger scale, rode it, and watched it leave. The dressers had already left. The afterlife of furniture, in this city, is an appliance story that also ended. What remains is the habit of wanting a payroll that makes things. The last essay takes the small things that still get made.
