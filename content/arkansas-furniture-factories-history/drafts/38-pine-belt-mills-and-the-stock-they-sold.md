Harris and Maxwell called it remarkable. Shortleaf pine was the state’s great manufacturing wood, and furniture factories almost did not use it. Table 8 is gum and oak. The box table is gum and then pine. Vehicle stock is its own hunger. A pine-belt town — Warren, Crossett, Fordyce, the south-Arkansas names the woods essays already walked — could run a civilization on yellow pine and still be a supplier, not a suite maker.

This essay is the statewide correction to a Fort Smith sentence that can sound like “Arkansas made furniture.” Arkansas made *wood products*. A fraction of those products, counted in 1912, were furniture. The fraction had a geography: hardwood hinterlands and a river city that knew how to finish. The pine belt’s geography was different: volume, paper later, flooring, millwork, the construction of other people’s rooms.

Bulletin 106’s furniture table is clean enough to repeat. 15,373,000 board feet. Red gum 9,061,000, 58.94 percent. White oak 5,586,000, 36.34 percent. Together about ninety-five percent. 85.44 percent grown in Arkansas. Shortleaf’s small showing, they wrote, was remarkable given how much other Arkansas industry demanded it. Three-quarters of manufacturing wood in the state could be pine and the furniture rooms would still want hardwood. That sentence is this essay in miniature.

## Stock, again

Furniture stock from a pine-belt mill, when it existed, was often the hardwood the mill could also reach — oak in Warren’s postcard, mixed cuttings — or the pine that a cheap kitchen would take painted. Do not write a Crossett dining suite into existence. Write a carload of dimension that a factory somewhere else would stain.

Bradley Lumber’s postcard, kept on ARGenWeb, already split the catalog: hardwood flooring, furniture stock, pine millwork, construction materials, mixed cars. Southern Lumber, Bradley Lumber, Arkansas Lumber — 100,000 to 150,000 board feet a day by 1907, 300 to 400 men each in the Encyclopedia’s snapshot — were volume machines. Volume is pine’s talent. Furniture, in the year we can count, wanted the other talent: a pore you can stain and a face you can sell as itself.

The woods essays own the tree. This essay owns the factory refusal. Why refuse pine in 1912? Fashion, dent, the pore, the customer’s hand. Why accept it later? Paint, price, a kitchen that wanted a table more than a species name. A present-tense pine table is allowed if the caption says pine. A 1912 Fort Smith factory pretending pine was oak is less documented than gum pretending walnut. Do not invent that pretense. The bulletin’s “small showing” is enough.

## Boxes as the pine furniture

If you want pine in the furniture story, look at the crate. Pine and gum shooks around an oak-faced dresser: the room is hardwood, the journey is mixed. Higgins’s box shops are this sentence in Fort Smith. He told the *Times Record* that even the furniture had to be boxed to ship, and that someone had to come in with an opportunity to make boxes. The pine belt’s box plants are the same sentence without the dresser. A state that ships fruit, ammunition, and dry goods is a state that makes boxes whether it makes suites or not.

St. Joe’s powder boxes were a war version. The 1912 box table is the peace version, already huge: 105 million board feet in that industry alone, against 15 million in furniture. Read those two totals again. Arkansas was a box state that also made furniture. Memory reversed the ratio because dressers photograph better than shooks.

Harris and Maxwell wished stave men would cut furniture squares so less white oak rotted in the woods. That wish is the hinge between pine-belt volume and furniture-factory hunger. Squares are stock. Stock is not a suite. The pine belt could honor the wish with oak cuttings and still keep the lights on with yellow pine.

White oak, in Table 8, cost $32.45 a thousand at the factory. Red gum cost $20.29. The price gap is a factory decision: face and understudy. Pine, when it entered a furniture room at all, was not winning that price argument. It was winning the box argument and the millwork argument. Construction materials on Warren’s postcard are the volume cousin. Flooring is the cousin that takes a finish. Furniture stock is the cousin that leaves for someone else’s machine room.

Higgins’s Fort Smith box shops and the pine belt’s shook plants are the same industrial family on different maps. A dresser photographed in a parlor has already forgotten the crate. Memory likes the parlor. The bulletin liked the count. 105 million against 15 million is the count.

## Rails, then paper, then other objects

The Encyclopedia’s Bradley County entry is the civic version of the same split. In the 1880s Jay Gould extended the St. Louis, Iron Mountain and Southern into Arkansas. Bradley County was served by the Little Rock, Mississippi River and Texas Railway, linking Warren to Dermott. The stage, the entry says, was set for exploiting shortleaf yellow pine as the Great Lakes cut thinned. Warren’s three mills followed. Population doubled between 1900 and 1910. That is a pine-belt origin, not a furniture-row origin.

Later weathers stayed in wood products without becoming suites. A 1939 fire at Southern and a rebuild. A 1949 tornado that destroyed a mill in Warren and killed more than fifty. Potlatch’s purchase of Bradley and Southern, 1958 in the Encyclopedia’s later revision. Kraft cheese, Jack Winter garments, pink tomatoes. Crossett’s paper story and Fordyce’s mill memory belong to their own encyclopedias. This essay will not raid them for color. It will only say: when a heritage page in Bradley County talks about mills, it is talking about the volume that made furniture *possible* elsewhere, and about flooring and millwork that are cousins. It is not talking about Riverfront Drive.

## A shop that shows pine

If a Warren shop uses yellow pine in a painted island or a utility top, that is a later, honest pine. Caption it. Do not call it Fort Smith furniture. Do not call it Bradley Lumber furniture. Call it pine, made now, in a county that used to drown the country in the species. That sentence is better than a fake suite.

Do not write a Crossett furniture factory into the sentence to make the belt look more like Fort Smith. Crossett is a paper and pine town unless a source says otherwise. Fordyce is a mill name unless a source hands us a letterhead. Honesty about absence is part of the correction.

Bradley County’s later objects are the reminder that a pine town does not stay only pine. Pink tomatoes, a Kraft cheese plant, Jack Winter garments — the Encyclopedia lists them as postwar industries. None of them is a suite. All of them are what a mill county does when the first cut is gone and the second growth has to share the payroll. Fort Smith learned the same lesson with Norge. Different objects. Same civic move. Furniture stock, on the Warren postcard, is the one object in that list that still sounds like a dresser and is not one. Leave it as stock.

Still frame: a pine board with a grade stamp, or a shook. No sunset through longleaf. The remarkable fact is the refusal. Furniture, in the year we can count, wanted the other trees. The pine belt kept the lights on anyway. A statewide factory history that cannot live with that split will keep writing one industry where there were two.
