The stencil still says Fort Smith, 1946. The selling floor that matters is High Point, North Carolina, a trade showroom, a headquarters line on a LinkedIn page. The *Times Record* in 2019 said Riverside had manufactured in Fort Smith until a few years earlier and was then a distribution point. I want a last-crate date before I put a year on the wall. I do not need a year to name the conversion. The town that made the object became a town that stores and designs the object, or stores the object someone else made.

This is not a scandal unique to Arkansas. American case-goods companies spent the 1990s and 2000s learning to be brand offices. High Point is where that learning is a civic industry. Fort Smith is where that learning looks like a quieting of a riverfront. Both sentences can sit in one essay if neither is asked to be a sermon.

## What “distribution” actually is

A distribution point is forklifts, a warehouse management system, a claim department, a trailer schedule. It employs people. It is not nothing. It is also not a finish room. Higgins’s industrial city — the upholsterers, the box shops, the fittings — thins when the object is already finished when it arrives. You still need a box sometimes. You do not need a town of sanders.

Girard’s “door” for Chinese furniture is the popular version. The quieter version is a Fort Smith company that still has a 1946 founding story and a 6th Street address and a catalog that does not require a Fort Smith kiln. Marketing will say “designs and develops.” That can be true. Design is work. It is not the work this pack set out to remember when it said *factories*.

Current public pages talk about thousands of retail doors and a trade-only showroom. Those numbers move. Do not freeze a 3,000 in a caption as if it were a 1912 board-foot table. The 1912 table was a government count. The 3,000 is a sales sentence.

## High Point as a second river

Fort Smith’s first selling geography was the Territory and Texas, then a national dealer base that still thought of the town as a source. High Point is a source of *attention*. You go there to be seen by the same buyers who stopped needing your freight car. A Fort Smith company that opens a High Point showroom is not betraying the river. It is admitting where the river now ends.

The risk is the civic one. Children in Ballman Elementary do not tour a High Point market. They can tour a museum that says there used to be a row. The conversion, if no one writes it down, becomes a shrug: we used to make furniture, the way every American city used to make something. This pack is the refusal of the shrug.

## Ownership, again

I have not walked the full chain from Arkansas Best’s 1966 purchase to the present privately held company. There were almost certainly sales, management buyouts, foreign sourcing agreements. Until the chain is in `verify` as filings, do not write a morality about who “took the jobs.” Write the visible conversion: less manufacturing in Fort Smith, more showroom somewhere else, the founding year kept because founding years are cheap and kilns are not.

## What a small shop is not

A 65-foot shop in Warren that still machines hardwood is not “the opposite of Riverside” in a morality play. It is a different scale that never had a High Point problem because it never had High Point volume. It can still die of freight and fashion. It cannot die of a 1,300-person campus becoming a warehouse. Different deaths.

Optional footnotes may point at heritage. The body should not say that Bradley Brand Furniture is what remains of Fort Smith’s factories. What remains of Fort Smith’s factories is a mixed set: memory, a distribution door, McCourt’s folding trade, mattresses in a former appliance plant, and whatever small shops did not get a newspaper feature. Riverside’s conversion is one large, nameable piece of that set.

## The last manufacturing year

Someone in Fort Smith knows the week the last Riverside case good was finished in town. That week should replace “a few years ago” in the 2019 paper. Until it does, the essay stays slightly out of focus on purpose. False precision is how factory history becomes fiction.

Still frame: racks of identical cartons, a label that says Riverside, a forklift, no founder portrait. If we cannot pull that frame, pull a sample board and say it is design without a kiln. Either picture is truer than a website hero image of a staged bedroom in a city that no longer sands the rails.

1946 remains a real year. 2019 remains a real newspaper sentence. The years between them are the factory. The years after are the showroom. This pack will not pretend they are the same job.
