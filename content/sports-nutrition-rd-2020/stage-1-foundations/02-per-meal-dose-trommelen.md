# The 20-gram ceiling was a 4-hour story

For a decade the locker-room version was tidy: 20–25 g of high-quality protein “maxes out” muscle protein synthesis; the rest is oxidized; split your day into tidy boluses. That version came from good acute trials — Moore 2009, Witard 2014 — that measured a few hours after a modest feeding, often after a small amount of muscle work.

Then Maastricht put 100 grams of milk protein on the table after lifting and watched for **twelve hours**.

## What Trommelen 2023 actually did

Trommelen, van Lieshout, Nyakayiru and colleagues randomized recreationally active young men to 0, 25, or 100 g of intrinsically labeled milk protein after resistance exercise. They used a quadruple-tracer design and took blood and muscle over 12 h. The 100 g feeding produced a larger and longer anabolic response than 25 g: more dietary amino acids in the blood, more of them in mixed-muscle, myofibrillar, connective, and plasma protein, and a higher whole-body net balance. Amino-acid oxidation did not swallow the extra protein the way the old cartoon said it would. The 25 g curve was clearly flattening by the end of the day. The 100 g curve was still climbing at 12 hours.

That paper is *Cell Reports Medicine*, 2023, doi:10.1016/j.xcrm.2023.101324. It is not *The Journal of Physiology*, which is how it sometimes gets miscited on social media. Get the journal right; the finding is startling enough without a wrong masthead.

## What it does not say

It does not say 100 g is the new target per meal. It does not say hypertrophy will be larger if you eat one giant steak instead of four normal plates. It is an acute tracer study in young men after one session. Witard and Mettler came back in 2024 arguing about *efficiency* — a higher fraction of the 25 g meal landed in muscle. Trommelen’s group answered in *IJSNEM* that you cannot call 25 g more efficient when its incorporation had already saturated and the 100 g meal was still being used at hour 12.

Both things can be true at once:

1. A moderate high-quality meal still lights up MPS in the timeframe we used to measure.
2. A much larger meal is not “wasted” on a 12-hour clock. Digestion just keeps feeding the system.

Macnaughton 2016 already hinted the old 20 g number was small-session thinking: after whole-body lifting, 40 g of whey beat 20 g. That was foundational. Trommelen stretched the clock and the dose.

## What I tell people on a Tuesday

If lunch is 30–40 g of real protein after a normal session, you are not leaving a known, large, free adaptation on the table. That is still how most training weeks should look.

If dinner is a 70–100 g protein sitting because that is how the family eats, or because you trained at 8 p.m. and you are not having four more meals — that is not a metabolic crime. The 2023 data say those amino acids keep working while you sleep.

If someone uses Trommelen to justify one protein feeding a day and a bag of candy otherwise, they did not read the paper. Whole-day energy, micronutrients, and the rest of training still exist.

ISSN 2017’s per-serving line — about **0.25 g/kg** or 20–40 g, with 700–3,000 mg leucine — remains a practical *minimum stimulus* for a high-quality feeding. It was never a hard ceiling. We just talked like it was.
