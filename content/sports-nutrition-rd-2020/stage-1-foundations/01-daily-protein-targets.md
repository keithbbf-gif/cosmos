# The daily number still does most of the work

If you only keep one protein idea from the last six years, keep this one: **the day beats the shake.** Timing papers get the press. The training studies that actually change a physique still hang on whether the person ate enough protein, enough energy, and lifted.

## What we walked in with

The ISSN’s 2017 protein stand — still the document most sports RDs quote — put most exercising people at **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day**, with higher figures (about 2.3–3.1 g/kg) floated for lean-mass hold during a hard calorie cut, and a note that some resistance-trained people have used more than 3 g/kg while losing fat mass in short trials. That is a position paper, not a law of nature, and it is older than this series’ window. It is also the floor the 2020s papers keep testing.

Morton and colleagues, in 2018, ran the meta-regression everyone still screenshots: extra protein on top of resistance training added a little lean mass and a little strength. Past about **1.6 g/kg/day** the curve flattened for the average participant. That paper is foundational. It is also an average of many protocols, many of them in people who were not already eating like physique athletes.

## What 2020–2022 actually added

Tagawa’s 2020 dose-response meta-analysis (105 trials, 5,402 people) is the unglamorous update. Lean-mass change still moved with protein across a wide range, but the *return per extra 0.1 g/kg* was larger below about **1.3 g/kg/day** than above it. Resistance training flattened that drop-off. In plain language: if someone is at 0.8–1.0 g/kg, getting them to a real athletic intake is where most of the mass effect lives. If they are already at 1.6–2.0 and lifting, you are arguing over smaller increments.

Their 2022 follow-up on **strength** was blunter. Extra protein helped strength when people were actually on a lifting program. Without the lifting, the protein increment did not do the job. The spline they fitted for trained strength work bent over around **1.5 g/kg/day**. That is not “1.5 is the magic number.” It is “do not expect a second helping of powder to replace a second set.”

Nunes et al. (2022) looked at healthy, non-obese adults who *increased* daily protein. The lean-mass effect with resistance exercise was real and small (standardized mean difference about 0.22). Lower-body strength ticked up mainly when intake reached ≥1.6 g/kg with lifting. Handgrip and “function” tests barely moved. If your athlete’s goal is a better 1RM and a bit more lean mass, this is supporting evidence. If the pitch is “protein will transform how you move,” the paper does not say that.

## How I would use the number

For a healthy adult who lifts and is roughly in energy balance, I still start at **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** of total protein from food, and I count the food. That band covers ISSN’s exercising range, sits on or a little above Morton’s plateau, and matches what UEFA’s 2021 football statement put on the page for players (1.6–2.2 g/kg, often already hit in Premier League diet recalls).

I move the number up when:

- the athlete is in a real energy deficit
- the sport is weight-class and the cut is not a rumor
- the protein is mostly lower-leucine plants and the total was not increased to compensate
- the person is a masters athlete who keeps missing meals

I do not move it up because a sachet said “anabolic.” I do not move it down because a breakfast influencer ate 12 g at 7 a.m. and “felt light.”

Per-kilogram math should use a body mass that makes sense. A 140 kg lineman with a large fat mass is not the same problem as a 62 kg climber. Some practitioners use fat-free mass for the second case. The papers above mostly used whole-body kilograms. Say so when you switch.

## What this is not

A daily protein target is not a treatment. It is not a kidney protocol. It is not a promise that lean mass will rise if training and energy are a mess. The 2020s metas are consistent on the unsexy point: **protein is a supporting actor.** The lead is still the training and the energy budget.
