# Sleep is the long recovery session. Protein is not a sleep drug.

I have been asked if casein “improves sleep.” I have been asked if a protein deficit is why someone wakes at 3 a.m. I have been asked if tryptophan-rich foods are a protocol. Most of those questions want a supplement. Most of those athletes need a bedtime and a dinner.

## What we actually measured

The pre-sleep literature (Snijders 2019 update; Reis 2020 review; the Maastricht overnight tracers) measured **amino-acid appearance and muscle protein synthesis during sleep**. It did not measure sleep architecture as a primary outcome in a way I would hang a protocol on. UEFA 2021 treated the night as a regenerative phase that happens to be a fasting phase. That is the right frame. Fill the fasting phase if the day was short of protein. Do not medicalize the cottage cheese.

Halson’s sleep-and-athlete work — much of it cited in the football statement — is about schedule, light, caffeine, travel, and load. Protein is barely in that conversation. If an athlete eats a huge spicy meal at 11 p.m. and then sleeps badly, that is a GI and timing problem, not a reason to ban protein at night.

Trommelen 2023’s 12-hour curve means a 7 p.m. feeding is still on the books at 7 a.m. If sleep is the priority and a late shake keeps someone wired because they *believe* it will, I will move the protein earlier. Belief is not a tracer, but it is a bedtime.

## A night card that stays in bounds

- Aim for the sleep hours the sport actually needs. That is coaching and medicine, not protein R&D.
- If the last meal was a carbohydrate-only buffet, add a protein food. That is the Snijders/Reis/UEFA line.
- If reflux or comfort is the issue, change the form (more liquid, less fried) rather than dropping the grams.
- Do not promise better sleep from an amino-acid product. Do not promise a treatment for insomnia. That is a claims-policy line and also not what the papers measured.

Overnight recovery is hours of low activity when remodeling can run if the precursors are there. Protein is a precursor story. Sleep is a sleep story. They share a clock. They are not the same intervention.

If an athlete is sleeping five hours because of travel or a newborn or a night match, I will not add a 40 g feeding at 12:30 a.m. to “make up the MPS.” I will try to get them six hours and a dinner that already had protein. Reis 2020’s caution about unmatched daily protein in some pre-sleep trials is the methodological version of that instinct: if you only add night protein by adding *daily* protein, you have not isolated bedtime. You have isolated “more food.”

Caffeine after an evening session is a more common sleep thief than casein. I mention that because people come to me with a protein question when they have a 200 mg question. This series is protein. The honest human sentence is still: go to bed.
