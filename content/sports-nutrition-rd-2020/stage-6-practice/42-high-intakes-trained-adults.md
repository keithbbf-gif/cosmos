# Very high protein in healthy lifters is a small literature. Treat it that way.

ISSN 2017 mentioned intakes above 3 g/kg in resistance-trained people and body-composition changes in short work. Antonio’s 2016 one-year crossover — 14 healthy trained men, habitual ~2.5 g/kg vs periods above 3 g/kg — reported no adverse movement in the blood lipids and the routine liver and kidney *lab numbers they measured*, and no fat-mass gain despite more calories. That paper is foundational, small, self-reported, and in healthy volunteers. It is not a medical clearance. It is not a 2024 multi-center trial. I will not pretend it is.

## What I will and will not say

I will say: in healthy, resistance-trained adults, the published high-intake work has not shown a lab-panel disaster in the measures those papers chose. I will say Tagawa 2020 still saw some lean-mass association out toward high intakes, with shrinking returns, especially without lifting.

I will not say high protein is “safe for kidneys.” I will not say it treats or prevents kidney disease. I will not say it is safe in people with diagnosed disease, in pregnancy, or in anyone a clinician has already restricted. Those sentences are how a sports draft becomes a problem.

Nunes 2022 excluded people who were not healthy. That is a feature. It is also a fence.

## Practice

- Most athletes I see need to get to **1.6–2.2 g/kg**, not to 3.5.
- If someone is already at 3 g/kg because they like meat, I look at the rest of the diet and the energy budget before I congratulate or panic.
- If they are pushing 3 g/kg to “force” hypertrophy on a lazy program, I send them back to Tagawa 2022.
- Anyone with a clinical history gets a clinician, not a citation from a 14-person crossover.

Antonio’s design is also a calorie story. When the men ate more protein they ate more energy (~30 to ~34 kcal/kg) and did not gain fat mass in that sample. That sentence has been used to sell “protein is free.” It is not. It is a small crossover with self-report. Tagawa 2020 already told you the lean-mass return shrinks as you climb. I will not take a Bod Pod study of 14 men and write a metabolic-ward conclusion.

Women are almost absent from this high-intake safety-ish literature. Antonio’s own 2018 follow-up note said so. If a female athlete wants to live at 3 g/kg, I look at food quality, energy, and whether she is actually hungry — and I still will not write a medical guarantee. The claims policy is the point of this draft.
