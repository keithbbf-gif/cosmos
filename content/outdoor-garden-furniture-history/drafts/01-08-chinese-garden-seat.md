A barrel that rings like a pot is allowed to live in the rain.

The Chinese garden seat — zuodun, the drum stool — shows up in paintings and in kiln lots as a pierced ceramic cylinder, sometimes stone, sometimes wood under cover. Barrel body. Nail-head bosses around the rim because the ceramic is remembering a drum. Openwork so the fire could take a thick pot and so the object could be lighter than it looks. You sit on the lid of a drum. You also set a tray on it. Dual use is old. Instagram did not invent it.

I start with ceramic because it is the outdoor material Western catalogs keep forgetting they already own as planters. High-fired porcelain and stoneware do not swell. They chip. They break if you ice them full of water and the freeze comes. They are cold. They are honest. A garden that can leave a stool out and have it still be a stool in April is a garden that understood the assignment better than a parlor that dragged a needlepoint chair onto the terrace "just for the party."

There is a naming theft here too. American shops now sell wooden barrels, upholstered drums, and resin garden stools and call them all Chinese garden seats. Some are citations. Some are nightstands that learned a silhouette. If the object cannot take a season outdoors, it is not a garden seat. It is a bedroom stool with a passport stamp.

Stone versions — carved drums, marble, sometimes paired with a small table — belong with the scholar's garden: rocks that are furniture, furniture that is geology. Taihu stones are not chairs. I mention them because the Chinese garden treats sitting as one event among walking, looking, and stopping. The seat is not a sectional. It is a pause. That is a useful corrective to the American patio, which tries to reproduce the living room at 1:1 scale under a weaker roof.

Wood garden furniture existed. It also needed the same roof logic as anywhere. Lacquer is a film. Film fails in sun. The pieces that lived in pavilions lived like porch furniture. The pieces that lived in the open were mineral.

I have been asked to make "something like those blue-and-white stools" in walnut with a cushion tied on. I can make a drum form in walnut. I will not call it a garden seat unless the client has a pavilion. I will call it a parlor stool that borrowed a silhouette. Names are duties. BBF collections can show a drum form if they want. The essay next to it has to say indoor or outdoor. Adjacent does not mean we launder a kiln into a finish sample.

Shop notes, because ceramic still has some.

A pierced stool collects leaves. Leaves hold water. Water plus freeze is how a pretty pot becomes sherds. Turn it over or take it in if your winter is real.

Do not put a glass top on a drum and call it a dining table for six. The old object is a seat and an occasional stand. Load it like a seat and an occasional stand.

Glaze can be slick when wet. That is not a lawsuit I want to inherit from a catalog sentence about "playful seating."

If you make furniture, notice the dual use without making every garden object a storage ottoman. If you buy furniture, buy ceramic or stone when you mean to leave it, and buy wood when you mean to roof it. If you like the blue-and-white, like it as a climate choice, not as a Chinoiserie costume for a foam cube.
