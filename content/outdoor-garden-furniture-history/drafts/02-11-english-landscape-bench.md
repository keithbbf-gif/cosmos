Capability Brown did not invent sitting down. He invented a reason to walk farther before you did it.

The English landscape garden of the eighteenth century — and the picturesque quarrel that followed — treats the grounds as a sequence of pictures. You are led. You are withheld. You are given a bench when the painter in charge wants you to stop. That bench might be a plank on stumps. It might be a rustic construction of branches that is trying to look like it grew there. It might later be iron painted green so it disappears into the idea of a park. The constant is not the material. The constant is the stop.

Rustic furniture is the honest weirdness in this chapter. Rootwood, burls, branches nailed into chairs that look like they would snag a coat. The Victorians will industrialize that look. The eighteenth century uses it as an argument: we are not in a French salon. We are in nature. Of course the nature is as managed as a dining room. The furniture has to perform wildness. That performance has a shop cost. Irregular stock does not sit flat. Bark holds water. Joints in the round are harder than joints in the square. A rustic chair that lasts is a better carpenter than the silhouette suggests. A rustic chair that lasts one season is a prop for a painting party.

The park bench as a public object grows out of this stop. Once the garden is a park and the park is a city, the bench has to take strangers, knives, and weather with less staff. Cast iron and slats will take that job. The English garden bench you can still buy — teak slats in a metal or teak frame, a gentle back, arm scrolls — is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century product that claims an older calm. Some of those makers were serious. Lister, Barlow Tyrie, the shipyard habit of teak. We will get to the tree. The form is a long sit facing a picture.

I have a prejudice, and I will say it. A bench that faces a blank fence is an English idea with the painting cut out of the frame. You can still sit. You will sit less. People are not noble. They will not contemplate a vinyl shed because the catalog said "traditional garden bench." Give them a tree, a gap in the hedge, a downhill, even a vegetable row that changes. The bench is a camera. Load the film.

Wood in an English wet climate is a different teacher than wood in Arizona. Rot is the exam. Oak that was heartwood and well detailed could last. Softwood painted could last if someone painted it. Teak arrived as the wood that did not need as much of that conversation. Before teak was a patio brand, it was a naval habit. England had a navy. The garden borrowed the deck.

I live in a wet enough place to respect this. Southern Indiana is not the Home Counties, but our porches mildew and our end grain drinks. When an American client wants "an English bench" I want to know if they want the picture, the teak, or the class costume. Three bids. Three winters.

BBF collections can sit a bench indoors as a hall piece or a dining banquette and still be honest. A garden bench indoors is a form that came inside. Say so. A dining bench outdoors is a form that went out without asking the English questions: what do you look at, and who will paint it, and where does the water leave the slat.

If you make furniture, cut a drip. The slat should not hold a puddle like a canoe. If you buy furniture, sit in the spot in the weather you actually have, not the weather in the novel. If you are a designer, put the composed view on the plan or strike the word English.

Iron is about to industrialize the garden. The next draft is a foundry in Shropshire that put ferns on a bench and shipped a style to people who had never seen the Severn.
