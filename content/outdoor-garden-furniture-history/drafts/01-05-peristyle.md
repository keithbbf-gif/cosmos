Walk a Pompeian house in your head and stop in the hole of light.

The atrium had a roof with a rain basin. Deeper in, if the house could afford it, there was a peristyle: columns around a garden, a walk in shade, a rectangle of sky the family owned. That is not a patio set. That is a room that negotiated with weather by architecture. The colonnade is the porch. The impluvium and the fountain are the climate machines. The benches and the couches — stone, wood we mostly lost, bronze we mostly melted — sat inside a decision someone already made about sun.

I like starting here because every catalog that says "outdoor living room" is trying to purchase a peristyle on a slab. They buy the sofa silhouette. They skip the colonnade. Then they are surprised that the sofa cooks.

Roman outdoor sitting was not one object. There were exedrae — curved stone benches in public porticoes and private gardens — that still make sense as a form. You sit in a conversation, not in a row facing a television of hedge. There were couches for dining outdoors when the weather and the insects allowed. There were marble tables whose tops we still have because marble does not owe anyone a film. The woods are the ghost. Carbonized beds from Herculaneum and a few bronze frames tell you the joinery existed. Weather and reuse took the rest. That is the first materials lesson in this series: what survives in a museum is what weather and scrap dealers could not eat. Stone and bronze testify. Pine does not.

Shade is furniture. I will say that until it bores you. A colonnade is a piece of furniture the size of a building. A pergola is the cheap descendant. An umbrella is the descendant that folds. If you specify a deep-seat sofa for a south slab and "we will add shade later," you have specified a perishable prop. The Romans put the shade in the plan. That is why their outdoor rooms still photograph as rooms.

Water is furniture too, in that climate. A basin cools air. It also stains stone and invites mosquitoes if you are in Indiana and you copy the postcard. Do not copy the postcard. Copy the assignment: reduce the sun, give the body a hard seat that can be left, keep the walking path dry enough to be a path.

Insects were a brief then too. A colonnade does not stop a mosquito. Cushions still came and went. The difference is that the architecture had already decided the sit was worth a building. We decide the sit is worth a carton and then argue with August.

I am not going to tell you to build a peristyle behind a ranch house. I am going to tell you that when a client asks for an outdoor room, I want to know where the columns are — even if the columns are a roof overhang, a tree, a screen porch, or a wall. No columns, no room. You have a lawn with ambitions.

BBF indoor work lives in rooms that already have ceilings. That is why this history is adjacent and not a product line. We understand rooms. The garden stole the word room and forgot the ceiling. The peristyle did not forget.

If you make furniture, look at the exedra before you draw a straight bench that faces nothing. If you buy furniture, buy the shade first. If you are a designer, draw the colonnade — whatever form it takes — on the same page as the chairs. The chairs are the easy part. They always were.

Next we stay in Rome long enough to talk about marble and bronze, and why the metal you wanted already got melted.
