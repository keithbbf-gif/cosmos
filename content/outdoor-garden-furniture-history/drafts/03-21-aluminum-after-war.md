The chair got light enough to steal, and that was progress.

After 1945 the United States had aluminum habits from aircraft and a backyard that wanted furniture. Robert Brown and Hubert Jordan started Brown Jordan in California in 1945. Tropitone shows up in the mid-1950s. Woodard, already a name in iron from Owosso, Michigan, had to live in a world that also wanted light. The famous lines — Tamiami and its cousins, strap and lace seats, tubular frames — are not my product and not my romance. They are the moment outdoor furniture becomes modern in the factory sense: repeatable, stackable, shippable, less of a foundry, more of an extrusion.

Aluminum's virtue is honest. It does not make rust coins on a deck the way cheap steel does. It can be light. It can be a tube. A tube can be a frame. A frame can take a sling or a strap and drain. That is a good patio machine. I would rather sit in an honest aluminum sling than in a wooden dining chair that was dragged outside for a wedding and then forgotten.

The vices are honest too. Cheap aluminum bends. Welds crack. Thin tube ovals in a stack if the stack is a storage system for a restaurant and you are not a restaurant. Powder coat on aluminum can still fail — not as rust bleed, but as film peel and as oxidation underneath that looks like a white scab. Bare aluminum gets a skin. Cast aluminum can be heavy again, which is useful in wind and useless if you bought it for lightness.

Heat. A dark metal seat in July is a skillet. Straps help. Sling helps. Bare tube arms will write a lesson on a forearm. The old strap chairs knew this. Some of the new "cast aluminum dining collections" forgot because the scrollwork wanted to be iron.

Stacking is a virtue I respect more than matching. A restaurant stack is a climate: you can take labor off the floor. A household stack in a garage is a winter plan. A household stack left stacked in the sun is a way to bleach the top chair and stick the paint of the rest. If you stack, stack as a ritual, not as a sculpture.

I will not narrate Brown Jordan as if I sat in their design studio. Public history is enough: California, aluminum, a look that said patio was not Victorian iron. The copies are the rest of the history. Most Americans did not buy the name. They bought the department-store cousin that used the same idea with thinner walls. The cousin is why people think aluminum outdoor is disposable. Disposable was a gauge choice.

Strap furniture — vinyl lace, later better textiles — was the sit that matched the light frame. The strap fails in the sun and at the rivet. That is a reskin, not a moral collapse, if the frame was built to take a new strap. Ask whether it was. A frame that cannot be re-strung is a chair with a calendar you cannot extend.

This shop does not extrude tube. Adjacent means I know when lightness is the brief. Indoor, lightness can be a vice — a table that feels cheap when you put a hand on it. Outdoor, lightness can be the thing that lets you bring the chair in before the hail. Different rooms, different morals. BBF collections stay with the indoor moral unless the object says otherwise.

If you make furniture, specify wall thickness like you mean it. If you buy furniture, lift the chair and flex the arm. If you are a designer, do not specify a named classic and then substitute a cousin to save a number without telling the client which winter they bought.

Wood is next as a material, not as a porch story. Teak first, because it is the tree everyone thinks they have already understood.
