Someone wrote the standing and sitting before anyone wrote the chair.

A formal French garden is a script. Axes. Rooms of hedge. Fountains as special effects. The furniture — when it is not the stone that came with the architecture — is blocking. You sit where you can see the next fountain and be seen from the terrace. You stand in a bosquet if the point is privacy. You take a painted chair out of an orangery because the citrus house is a warehouse for trees and, on the right afternoon, for people.

I start here so we can retire a lazy comparison. Every wide patio with matching chairs gets called "a little Versailles" by someone who has not been asked to walk that far in gravel. Versailles is labor, water pressure, and a king. What you can steal is the idea that seats have marks on the stage. Not a cluster in the dead center because the delivery driver set the carton down there.

The orangery is the useful object for a furniture shop. It is a room that is almost outdoors. It holds plants that cannot take a French winter. It also holds furniture that cannot take a French winter. Painted wood, light chairs you can carry, metal that came later. The ritual of moving the citrus is the same ritual as moving the chairs. If your climate is Indiana, you already know a version of this: the porch furniture that comes in, the citrus that dies if you pretend you live in Nice.

Painted garden wood is a film story. Pale gray-greens, creams, the colors that photograph as "French." The paint is a sacrificial skin. It will chalk. It will need a scrape. That is not failure if you budget it. It is failure if you believed the paint was a mineral. I have more patience for a chair that expects to be painted every few years than for a chair that claims to be finished forever and then peels in sheets.

Bosquets — the rooms cut into the hedge — are peristyles made of plants. Shade, enclosure, a bench. When Americans plant a boxwood square around a fire pit, they are reaching for a bosquet and a campfire at once. Pick a temperature. Conversation in shade is one brief. Marshmallows are another. The furniture for each is different. One can be a still bench. The other needs clearance and a material that will not be the thing that ignites.

I will not costume a Midwestern yard in embroidered silk just because a plate from the 1680s exists. I will ask where the axis is. Even a small yard can have an axis: the door to the maple, the kitchen window to the shed you painted so you could stand it. Put the seat on that line or admit you want a huddle. Huddles are honest. They are not French theater. They are a porch conversation that moved onto grass.

BBF indoor dining is already blocking. Host, glare, path to the kitchen. Outdoor blocking is the same work with worse lighting and a grill. Adjacent means we already know how to refuse a table in the middle of a walking path. The garden just has more walking paths that nobody drew.

If you make furniture, draw the terrace as a stage plan, not as a showroom cluster. If you buy furniture, move the carton off the delivery spot before you decide it "fits." If you are a designer, write who is looking at whom. If the answer is "the television we might add later," you are not in a French garden. You are in a living room that lost its walls, and we have a different draft for that.

England will answer France by loosening the hedge and keeping the bench. The bench becomes a place to look at a painting the land is pretending to be.
