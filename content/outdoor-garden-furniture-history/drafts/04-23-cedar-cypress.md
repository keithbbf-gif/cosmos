Some trees show up already arguing with fungi.

Western red cedar — *Thuja plicata* — has thujaplicins in the heartwood. Bald cypress — *Taxodium distichum* — has a Southern reputation in boats, tanks, and porch stock that earned it. Eastern red cedar is a juniper that smells like a chest and is a different shop conversation. I group the first two because American outdoor carpentry actually used them for benches, Adirondacks, and porch parts when teak was a story from a catalog and pine was a short life.

Extractives are not a force field. They are a statistical kindness. Heartwood, not sapwood. A cedar Adirondack with a lot of white sapwood is a two-tone science project. I have seen "cedar" patio benches that were mostly pale and then surprised their owners by going dark in patches. That is the tree being accurate. The tag was optimistic.

Softness is the other half. Cedar is a pleasure to work and a dent waiting for a belt buckle. If the brief is a dining table where someone will drop a pot, cedar is a porch wood wearing a table costume. Cypress can be a bit tougher depending on the stock, still not ipe, still not a butcher block. Use them where the sit is the job and the abuse is weather, not kitchen.

Stability is pretty good if the piece was dry. Outdoor dry is a moving target. A wide cedar seat will still cup if you seal one face and leave the other to drink. Film on the top only is how you build a banana. If you finish, finish a system. If you do not finish, detail the water off and accept the gray. Gray cedar is honest. Black mildew is a cleaning job. People confuse them and then sand off a year of surface for no reason.

Smell. I like it. It is not a grade. A fragrant board can still be juvenile, knotty, and weak at a tenon. Grade the board with your eyes and a knife, not with your nose in the store aisle.

Fasteners in cedar need thought. The wood is soft. A screw can pull if the sit is a lever. Washers, bigger heads, not a skinny drywall thread. I have seen cedar Adirondacks loosen at the arm bolt because someone used a hardware-store special and a hope. The tree did not fail. The connection failed.

This shop uses cedar in indoor contexts when the brief wants it — chests, linings, the occasional panel — and we know the difference between a lining and a dining table. Adjacent means I will not specify cedar as an outdoor dining top for a client who lives with cast iron cookware on the patio. I will specify it for a roofed bench, a porch ceiling, a chair that can take a dent. BBF collections should not call every aromatic board an outdoor solution.

Cypress in the South was a working wood: boats, vats, porch parts that had to take wet air. When a client from the Gulf says cypress I listen for that working memory. When a catalog says cypress on a pale picnic table I listen for a price. Same word. Different board. Ask for heartwood and a thickness that is not a veneer of reputation on a pine core. I have been shown "cypress" that was a smell and a story. The knife told on it.

If you make furniture, keep feet out of planters and put a sacrificial bottom on posts. If you buy furniture, look for heartwood color, not only the word cedar. If you are a designer, write species and heartwood, not "natural wood, outdoor." Natural is not a species.

Ipe is the opposite personality: so hard it taxes the shop, so dense people think it is immortal. It is not immortal. It is just slow.
