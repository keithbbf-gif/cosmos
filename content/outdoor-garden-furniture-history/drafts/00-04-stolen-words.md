Start with the words that got fat.

*All-weather* used to be a claim you could fail. A ship's teak grate was all-weather because the tree had already voted, and the navy had already paid for the lesson. A powder-coated aluminum frame with a sling that drains is all-weather in a narrower sense: it does not hold a puddle and it does not swell. A printed resin weave that looks like rattan is all-weather if you mean it will not mildew like cane. It is not all-weather if you mean it will look like June after five Augusts of ultraviolet. The phrase now means "we put it in the outdoor department." That is a shelf. It is not a test.

*Teak* is a tree. *Tectona grandis*. Oil in the cells. Silica that dulls a plane. Heartwood that boatbuilders trusted on decks because the deck is a patio that moves. The word got stolen by color. Pale furniture-store boards get called teak because the tag needed a warm syllable. Sometimes the board is plantation teak and the word is still botanically true and the quality is a lottery. Sometimes the board is a different species wearing teak as a finish name. If you cannot say the Latin or the country and the grade, you are buying a mood. I can respect a mood. I will not let it specify a joint.

*Wrought iron* is worse. True wrought iron is a low-carbon iron with slag fibers. Blacksmiths worked it. It is almost gone as a commercial material. What you are offered on a patio is usually mild steel, bent and welded, painted or powder-coated, sometimes with a rust-through that starts at a grind mark the finisher missed. Cast iron is a different metal — poured, brittle, heavy, honest about its weight. People say wrought when they mean "scrollwork" and they say cast when they mean "heavy." Those are silhouettes. Ask which metal, how it was joined, and what covers it. If the answer is a vibe, the rust will write a clearer sentence later.

*Patio set* is the most American theft. A set is a factory's packing list: four chairs, a table, sometimes an umbrella that was specified by a different company. Historic garden sitting was rarely a matched carton. The English bench was a bench. The Coalbrookdale seat was a casting. The porch rocker was a rocker that happened to live outside. Matching is a midcentury retail habit that leaked backward onto the past. I do not hate a set. I hate the assumption that the table and the chairs share a winter because they share a photograph. Aluminum chairs with a wood-look top are two materials and two failures waiting on different calendars.

A few cousins while we are here.

*Wicker* is a method of weaving, not a plant. Rattan is a vine. Cane is a skin. Lloyd Loom is paper twisted on wire. If a tag says wicker and the seat is a resin extrusion, the method has been outsourced to a die.

*Cedar* in American outdoor talk is often western red cedar, sometimes eastern red, sometimes a juniper someone loved. Smell is not a grade.

*Powder coat* is a baked film. It is not the metal. When it chips, you meet the metal.

I police these words the way I police *handmade* and *custom* on the indoor side. Stolen words are how a room gets the wrong object. A designer who writes "wrought teak all-weather set" has written a poem. I cannot bid a poem. I can bid a climate, a species, a joinery, a film, and a fastener.

BBF collection language has to survive the essay next to it. If a page says bench, it should be a bench. If it borrows garden language for an indoor piece — a "patio dining" photograph of an indoor table staged outside — say it is a stage. Adjacent does not mean we launder a climate.

Take the words back. All-weather is a test you describe. Teak is a tree. Wrought is a metal almost nobody is selling you. A set is a carton. After that, history is allowed to start, because the old objects still used the old words and the old words still had jobs.
