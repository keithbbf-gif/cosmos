The tag on the end of the board is doing more work than the Latin.

Pressure-treated lumber is pine or a similar softwood that was put in a cylinder and forced to drink a preservative. For a long late-twentieth-century stretch the drink was CCA — chromated copper arsenate. It worked. It also became a public argument. The EPA's residential phaseout landed at the end of 2003 into 2004. What replaced it for a lot of ground-contact and decking stock were copper systems — ACQ, copper azole, and their cousins. I am not your toxicologist. I am a person who has watched fasteners melt in new treated wood because someone used the old nail habit on the new chemistry.

Copper loves some steels in a way you will not love back. The newer treatments were harder on ordinary fasteners than many people expected. Hot-dip galvanized or stainless, rated for the treatment, is the adult sentence. Electrogalvanized close enough is how a deck gets a rust rash and a chair gets a loose joint at year three.

Treated wood is rarely furniture-grade in the sense this shop uses the word. It is often wet when you buy it. It warps as it dries. It has incising marks. It smells after rain. It will take paint badly if you rush. It can grow a coppery green that people think is mold and other people think is a finish. I will use it for a hidden sleeper or a cleat against soil. I will argue with you if you want a dining table out of it because the price per foot won. Price per foot is how you get a table that moves like a live animal for a year and then sits crooked.

There is a furniture market in "treated pine patio," especially Adirondacks and picnic tables. Some of it is honest and painted. Some of it is still dripping when it is assembled. If you assemble wet treated stock, you are tightening joints that will shrink. The chair will loosen. That is not a mystery. That is moisture content.

I will not tell you treated wood is evil. It kept a lot of American outdoor structure from becoming compost on a schedule the household could not afford. I will tell you it is a process, not a species. "What wood is it?" "Treated." That is an answer about death, not about grain. Ask southern yellow pine or whatever the mill actually used. Ask above-ground or ground-contact. Ask if it has dried.

Cut ends. When you cut treated stock, you open untreated heart. Field treatments exist. People skip them. The cut end is then the first drink. If you build a bench from treated 2x material, treat the cuts or stop acting surprised when the fresh end goes first. This is deck knowledge that furniture borrows and then forgets because the object is pretty.

This shop does not build BBF collections in treated pine. Adjacent means I still have to talk about it because clients bring patio photographs of picnic tables and ask for "that, but nicer." Nicer is a different process or a different tree. It is not a stain on wet stock. We can talk about a proper outdoor wood or a metal. We cannot launder a deck board into a showroom finish.

If you make furniture, weigh the board and wait. If you buy furniture, ask when it was built relative to when the wood was treated. If you are a designer, do not specify treated pine for a sit that touches bare legs unless you like the feel of a chemical board after a hot day. Specify a finish system and a fastener schedule, or specify something else.

Joinery is the last wood draft because the tree does not save a joint that holds a puddle. Rain is a critic of every tenon we brag about indoors.
