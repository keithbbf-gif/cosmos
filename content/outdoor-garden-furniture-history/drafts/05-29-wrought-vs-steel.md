Ask for the metal, not the curl.

True wrought iron — the old stuff — is scarce as a commercial furniture metal. It had fibers of slag. Smiths liked it. It is not what comes off a truck from a patio importer at a price that would insult a smith. What comes off that truck is mild steel. Bent. Welded. Ground. Coated. Sometimes with a rust-through that starts where the grinder went through the coat and nobody touched it up.

I do not hate mild steel. I hate the stolen word. Mild steel can make a good garden frame if the tube or bar is thick enough, the welds are real, the water cannot sit in a tube, and the film is maintained. It can also make a lace of rust in four winters if the tube is thin, undrained, and the powder coat was a suggestion.

Woodard and other American shops made serious steel and iron outdoor furniture in the twentieth century — mesh, frames, a quality you can still feel in old pieces. The department-store cousins used the silhouette. Same story as aluminum. Gauge is the moral.

Tubes hold water. Drill a drain or cap with a plan. A welded scroll that creates a cup will rust from the cup out. Look at the lowest point after a rain in your head before you buy. I do that with wood slats. I do it with steel.

Welds. A pretty ground weld that is only pretty is a thin place. A weld that was not cleaned before coating is a rust seed under a film. You do not need to be a welder to see lumps, skips, and a coat that already maps the heat.

Galvanizing plus a film is a system some good outdoor steel uses. Bare galvanizing is another look. Paint over rust without knocking the scale back is a winter project in April. I would rather see a household pick one system and keep it than buy "wrought" every five years because the word felt historic.

A gate and a chair are not the same steel job. A gate can be heavier and uglier and still be right. A chair has to take a sit without a sharp scroll in the kidney. I have seen "garden wrought" that was really a railing language turned into a seat. Railing language cuts people. Chair language holds them. If the object started life as a railing sketch, it is not a chair yet. It is a fence you sat on.

Mesh on a steel frame — the old Woodard language — is a sit that drains and a metal that still needs a coat. I respect it more than a solid steel seat that holds a puddle and cooks a thigh. Mesh also collects maple seeds and hose water at the rim. Look at the rim. The rim is where the coat chips and the rust starts a lace.

This shop works wood. We hire metal when a brief needs it. Adjacent means I will not let a designer write wrought on a drawing as if that specified a corrosion calendar. Write mild steel, wall thickness, coating, drain. BBF collections stay out of fake smithing captions unless we actually hired a smith and the object is the object.

A scroll is not a specification. I can draw a scroll in two minutes. I cannot invent wall thickness in two minutes after the piece has failed. If the client wants the grandmother's word, they can have it in the conversation. They cannot have it on the purchase order without the metal under it. Purchase orders are how winters get honest. Conversations are how winters get postponed.

If you make furniture, do not hide an undrained tube behind a scroll. If you buy furniture, look at the feet and the underside of every curl. If you are a designer, strike the word wrought unless you have a mill certificate that would surprise me. Then we can celebrate.

Aluminum we have met as history. As a material it still needs a short shop draft: heat, fatigue, coat, and the difference between cast and tube.
