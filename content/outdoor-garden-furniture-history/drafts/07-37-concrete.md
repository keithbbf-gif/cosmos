A mix is not a rock. It is a recipe.

Concrete garden furniture is the twentieth century saying stone is too expensive and too aristocratic, so here is a mineral we can pour. Benches in parks. Tables with umbrella holes. Planters that are also seats if you are young. Some of it is beautiful in a brutal way. Some of it is a tub that lost its corners because water got to the steel inside.

Reinforcement. If there is rebar or mesh, it is a hidden metal with a rust calendar. Cover — the thickness of concrete over the steel — is the difference between a bench and a rust bloom that splits the face. I have seen pretty terrazzo-adjacent tops with a brown line that is the steel writing through. That line is information. It is not a stain you mop once.

Freeze-thaw is the Midwestern exam. A mix that is too wet, too porous, too thin, will spall. De-icing salt from a nearby walk will help it fail. If the bench is on a path that gets salt, you have designed a chemistry experiment. I would rather put wood or metal there, or a stone that was chosen for it.

Weight, again. Concrete is cheap until you move it. Delivery is part of the object. So is the fact that you will not bring it in for winter. Detail it like something that stays: pitch, drain, feet that do not sit in a saucer of ice.

Sealing is a film conversation. Some sealers help staining and do not help freeze. Some make a slick sit when wet. I do not specify a sealer as a personality. I specify whether the household wants a dark wet look or a mineral look, and I tell them both will change.

A slick sit after a sealer is how someone stands up too fast with a glass in hand. If the household includes children or a wet pool path, I would rather have a mineral texture than a showroom sheen on a garden mix. Sheen is indoor talk. Traction is outdoor talk.

Umbrella holes in concrete tables collect water and then ice if the sleeve is a cup. Drain the sleeve or skip the hole. I keep saying this across metals and minerals because the umbrella is a sail and the hole is an afterthought. Afterthoughts freeze.

This shop does not pour furniture concrete. Adjacent is rare and useful: we understand heavy objects and floors. We will not caption a wood table as concrete-modern because the edges are chunky. Chunk is not a mix.

GFRC and lighter modern mixes exist so people can have the look without the hernia. Lighter can be good. Lighter can also be a skin over a foam or a steel that you have not met. Ask what is inside. If the inside is a surprise, the winter may be a surprise. I would rather lift a known heavy thing than baby a mystery sandwich that sounds hollow when I knock. Knock. Hollow is information. Information is cheaper than a cracked face in March.

If you make furniture, you are probably not pouring. If you specify, ask about mix, cover, and whether the piece is rated for your freeze. If you buy, look at corners and at any rust map. If you are a designer, do not put a pale concrete bench under a mulberry and promise a wedding.

Corners are where carts clip and where freeze starts a chip. A radiused corner lives longer than a crisp rendering corner. I will take a slightly soft edge on a garden mix over a knife edge that photographs well and then sheds its face. Photographs do not sit. Corners do.

Sling and mesh are the seats that decided water should fall through a person instead of sitting in a slat.
