The museum will lie to you by being honest about what it has.

Walk the classical galleries and you will believe the Romans sat on stone. They did. They also sat on wood. Wood does not ship well across two thousand years of damp. Bronze sat outdoors and indoors; most of it went into later cannons, bells, and coins. What you are looking at is a survival contest, not a catalog. If you design a garden from the objects that won that contest, you will specify marble benches and think you have done history. You have done geology.

Marble is a truthful outdoor material in a dry or well-drained place. It is cold. It is heavy. It does not ask for a spring ritual with a rag and a can of oil. It will sugary-weather in acid rain. It will stain under iron and under leaves. It will break if you cantilever it like a dining table and then let freeze get into a crack. Those are duties, not moods. A marble seat is a commitment to leaving the object in the weather as architecture. That is why villas used it and why a suburban patio copies it at half thickness and then wonders why the span sounds hollow.

Bronze is the metal I respect and almost never see in a garden that was priced last Tuesday. Cast, heavy, it can take a body and a century. The patina is a film the air grows. People pay extra to fake that film in a week. The historical problem is simple: bronze was always money sitting in the shape of a chair. When money got scarce, the chair became money again. So the public record of outdoor bronze is thinner than the private record of outdoor wood, and both are thinner than stone.

Wood is the missing middle. We know it from wall painting, from carbonized rooms, from a few waterlogged pieces, from the fact that people are not masochists — they sat on something that was not always 55 degrees. Joints, slats, cushions that were textile and stuffing. The cushions are even more gone than the frames. Every modern patio cushion is trying to replace a perishable layer the ancients also replaced. That should make you kinder to cushions and harsher to the idea that they are the furniture.

Survival bias is a shop problem, not just a historian's. Indoor, I can look at a Federal secretary and learn a joint because the joint is still there. Outdoor, the joints that failed are mulch. The joints that lived are stone, or a metal someone forgot to melt, or a teak slab someone kept oiling on a boat. If you only copy survivors, you copy weight and mineral. If you only copy catalogs, you copy last year's resin. The work is to admit the missing wood without inventing a fantasy Greco-Roman lounge collection in "aged oak."

I will not sell you a marble garden bench from this shop. We do not run a stone yard. I will tell you that when a client shows me a photograph of a Roman seat and asks for it in mahogany with a film finish, they have asked for a museum object and a porch object at the same time. Pick one. The marble wants to be left. The mahogany wants a roof or a species change.

If you make furniture, study what is gone. Design for the thing weather eats — the end grain, the unseen fastener, the finish on the top slat — not for the thing a camera loves. If you buy furniture, ask what the piece is supposed to become in ten years. Marble becomes more marble, with stains. Painted steel becomes a restoration project. Teak becomes silver. Resin becomes chalk. Those are four honest futures. A photograph is not a fifth.

The next climate that still teaches is the courtyard that treated shade as law — the Islamic garden, where sitting outdoors was a thermal problem before it was a style problem.
