Cyrus Wakefield figured out that a vine from across an ocean could furnish an American porch.

Rattan — the climbing palm, not a tree in the oak sense — came into New England as ballast and as a material. Wakefield's company in Massachusetts, later merged with Heywood Brothers as Heywood-Wakefield, turned woven porch furniture into a business you could find in a catalog and on a street of painted houses. The chairs were light. They took paint. They looked like summer. They were not meant to be the same object as a Coalbrookdale bench left in the rain from April to November.

Wicker is the method. We will say that again in the materials stage until it sticks. Here the history is social. The porch needed furniture you could rearrange for a crowd, carry with one good hand, and not treat like an heirloom chest. Wicker was cheap relative to carved walnut and cheerful relative to oak settles. It also hid a lot of cheap frames under a good weave and a lot of good frames under a paint that sealed dirt.

Painted wicker is a film on a texture. Every crossing is a place the film is thin. Hosing drives water into the crossings. The porch roof helps. A pressure washer is an enemy. I have watched people clean wicker the way they clean siding and then wonder why the cane went gray and the nails rusted through the paint. Clean it like furniture. Because it is.

Winter storage is the other half of the history. Households put the wicker in an attic or a shed. Sometimes they didn't, and the photographs of sagging porch sets in March are also history. The object taught a ritual. When we replaced the ritual with "all-weather wicker" we were not improving morals. We were buying out of the attic trip. That buy-out has a cost in ultraviolet and in a plasticizer that chalks. Different draft. Different honesty.

Paint color on historic wicker was often light — cream, pale green — because summer wanted to look like summer. Dirt shows. That is why the hose temptation exists. Resist it. A damp cloth and a dry day will do more for a painted weave than a blade of water. If the paint is already failing, that is a scrape and a coat, not a pressure-washer confession.

I will not pretend every Heywood piece was a masterpiece. Industry made a lot of chairs. Some were tight. Some were already tired in the warehouse. The lesson is the assignment: summer skin, roof, paint, put-away. If your household will not put away, do not buy a historic material and then write a one-star review in April.

This shop does not weave rattan. Adjacent means I know a skin from a carcass. Indoor, a cane seat in a dining chair is a skin with a climate. Outdoor porch wicker is that knowledge in weather. BBF collections can show cane as an indoor craft. This essay sits beside it so we do not call a resin lounge "the new Heywood" as if the attic never existed.

If you make furniture, look at how the weave attaches to the frame. That attachment is the failure. If you buy furniture, pick up the chair. Light is the point. If it is heavy as iron and called wicker, someone has lied about the method or stuffed the frame with a surprise. If you are a designer, specify storage square footage for seasonal wicker the way you specify a linen closet. If there is no closet, there is no historic wicker. There is a different material.

The ranch house will now move the sitting to the back and take the roof off. The patio is an American invention of floor, grill, and sky, and it will demand a new metal.
