Glass is a roof. Treat it like one.

The Victorian conservatory — and the cheaper sunrooms that followed — let a household sit among plants without sitting in the weather. That is a triumph and a category error waiting to happen. The furniture that evolved for those rooms is indoor furniture with a view: painted light chairs, wicker that would not like a week of rain, cushions, sometimes metal that is more jewelry than park bench. It belongs under glass the way a dining table belongs under plaster.

The lie is the photograph. A white wicker set in a conservatory looks "outdoor" because there are leaves behind it. A stylist then moves the cousin onto a deck. The deck has no glass. The cousin has a paper twist or a cane that drinks. July is a week of storms. August is mildew. September is a listing that says "needs TLC."

Heat is the other cheat. Glass rooms cook. Ultraviolet still comes through. A film that is fine in a north dining room can die on the south side of a sunroom. I have seen indoor tables we would be proud of asked to live against glass "because it's basically inside." Basically is how finishes chalk. If you want a piece in a glass room, say so at the start. We can talk about species, color, and whether the client will pull shades. We cannot talk after the top has gone patchy and everyone is polite.

Plants are wet neighbors. Watering is a climate. I would rather design a tile floor and a wood table with a finish that can take a drip than pretend a conservatory is a library. The old orangeries knew they were warehouses. The new sunroom wants to be a magazine. Magazines do not water ficus.

Category, then, in shop English:

Porch: roof, open sides, splash, outdoor or porch-grade objects.
Patio: sky, hard floor, outdoor objects.
Conservatory: glass roof and walls, indoor objects that can tolerate light and plant water.
Open lawn: leave-it materials or a ritual of carrying.

If a tag says "conservatory chair" I hear "not a rain chair." If a tag says "indoor-outdoor" I hear a marketing department hoping you will not pick a climate. Sometimes the object can do both — teak, certain metals, certain slings. Sometimes the object can do one. The hyphen does not add a climate.

I have been asked to finish an indoor table "a little tougher" because it will live in a glass room that "gets opened to the patio." Opening a door does not change a film into a marine system. It changes the amount of pollen and the amount of hope. If the table will spend three months with the doors stacked open and the sprinkler overshooting, say that in the first meeting. We can refuse the table or change the brief. We cannot change it after the top has gone cloudy and everyone is still being polite about the ficus.

This shop builds indoor work. A sunroom job is still indoor if the envelope is real. That is BBF-adjacent in the useful direction: we already know how to refuse a dining finish next to a humidifier and a west window. The conservatory is that refusal with more leaves. What I will not do is let a sunroom photograph authorize a patio purchase. Adjacent history is there to stop that leak.

If you make furniture, ask where the glass stops. If you buy furniture, put your hand on the ceiling. If the ceiling is sky, you are not in a conservatory. If you are a designer, do not use "indoor-outdoor fabric" as a spell that blesses an indoor frame. Fabric is one layer. The frame has its own winter.

America will now take the porch seriously as a room. That is the next climate, and it is the one I have actually sat in for a lifetime of Midwestern evenings.
