The street could see you, and that was the point.

A front porch in the American nineteenth century is not a leftover stoop. It is a room. Ceiling. Floor. Posts. A rail. Furniture that faces the walk, the neighbor, the weather that is only half allowed in. You sat there because the house was hot, because the street was a show, because courtship and gossip and the Sunday wait for someone needed a place that was not the parlor and not the road. I grew up around porches that still knew that job, even when the air conditioner had already won the interior.

This is the most important American distinction in the series. Porch furniture can be wood with paint. It can be a rocker. It can be wicker that would fail on a patio. It can be a swing on chains. The roof is doing half the work. People keep buying patio sets for porches and porch swings for patios and then writing reviews as if the object betrayed them. The climate betrayed the tag.

Screens change the room again. A screened porch is closer to a conservatory with better air. Insects were a design brief. After screens, you could have cushions that lived out more of the season. After air conditioning, many porches became storage for the objects that used to be the social life. I have walked Midwestern streets where the porch still has the swing and nobody is on it because the television is colder. That is not a moral. It is a furniture vacancy.

Depth matters. A porch that is five feet deep will take a rocker if you are careful and will not take a sofa without blocking the door. A porch that is eight feet can be a room. Measure like a hallway. Leave the path. The door swing still wins. I have said this about halls in another series. The porch is a hall that learned to sit.

Ceilings were often painted — the blue that is supposed to fool wasps or just please a painter. Floors were painted gray or left as boards that needed paint. The furniture had to live with that maintenance culture. A household that already paints a floor can paint a rocker. A household that wants "maintenance-free" is asking for a different room, usually a patio, usually a resin.

I will not romanticize every porch as a better America. Some were the only cool place a person had. Some were performance for the street. Some now are stage sets for a listing. What I will romanticize, if you want to call it that, is the roof. The roof is why this shop's indoor habits almost apply. A table on a porch can be a cousin of a kitchen table if the splash is modest and the finish is chosen with the draft in mind. A table on a patio cannot borrow that cousinship without changing species or metal.

BBF collections showing a bench or a dining form on a covered porch are adjacent in a fair way. Covered is the word. If the photograph is a porch and the caption says outdoor, add the roof. If the photograph is a slab and the caption says porch, that is a lie.

If you make furniture, design the porch piece to be moved by two people and cleaned with a mop, not a pressure washer at close range. If you buy furniture, stand on the porch and look at the ceiling before you look at a catalog. If you are a designer, write "roofed" or "unroofed" on every outdoor line. That one word saves a winter.

The rocker earned this room. It gets its own draft, because a chair that moves is a different machine than a chair that only sits.
