Heat is a more honest critic than taste.

In a courtyard tradition that runs from Andalusia through North Africa into Iran and beyond, the garden is not a lawn you cross with a tray. It is a cooled interior that happens to have sky. Walls. A basin. Trees that are HVAC. Seats that are stone, tile, built-in masonry, sometimes wood under a portico. The furniture is often the building. That sentence should sound familiar if you just walked a peristyle. Different theology, same physics.

I care about this because American outdoor copy stole the lantern and left the shade. You get a metal table powder-coated "Marrakech," four chairs that hold heat like a skillet, a fire bowl for a climate that already has too much fire, and a rug that will mildew. The postcard had water and a roofed walk. The order has a SKU.

Low sitting is not a personality. It is a relationship to a carpet, a tray, and a wall you can lean on. When a Western catalog translates that into a six-inch pouf in olefin on a south-facing deck, it has translated the photograph and not the room. The room had a thermal mass. The pouf has a foam that holds last night's rain if the cover is cute and not sealed.

Water again. A four-fold garden, a cross axis, a rill — these are plans. They humidify and they organize walking. They also demand maintenance that a weekend house will not give. If you cannot keep water clean, do not specify a rill because a magazine did. Specify a tree. A tree is furniture that bills you in leaves instead of pumps.

Hard seats. Tile and stone stay outside without asking for a cover ritual. They are cold in the morning and hot at noon unless the shade is real. Built-in benches along a wall are the honest form: they belong to the masonry, they cannot be stolen easily, they cannot be put on our truck. That last clause is the BBF boundary. We build objects that can leave a shop. A courtyard bench that is a wall is architecture. Adjacent history still matters because designers ask furniture shops to fake a wall with a sofa. A sofa is not a wall. A sofa on a hot courtyard without a portico is a cushioned apology.

Wood, when it appears, stays under cover — a mashrabiya's cousin, a ceiling, a lattice that breaks glare. Lattice is furniture. It is also a finish problem and a rot problem if you build it like a porch rail and then forget the end grain. I have seen "Moroccan screens" in interior plywood put outside for a wedding. That is not culture. That is a prop.

I will not lecture you on a religion I do not speak from. I will lecture you on climate. If your site is a blast of sun and a hard floor, you are closer to this assignment than you are to an English lawn bench, even if your Pinterest folder says otherwise. Build shade. Cool the air if you can. Give the body a seat it can leave. Do not start with a deep-cushion sectional and hope the lanterns will lower the temperature.

If you make furniture, notice how much of the sitting is built-in. That is a refusal of the carton. If you buy furniture, buy the portico first. If you are a designer, write "shade source" above the chair schedule. If the cell is empty, you do not have an outdoor room. You have a bill for replacement foam.
