Eleven boards and a sit you have to commit to.

In 1903 Thomas Lee, in Westport, New York, on Lake Champlain, built a chair from wide boards so guests would have something to sit on that was not parlor furniture taken hostage by a lawn. Harry Bunnell, a carpenter and friend, patented it in 1905 as the Westport chair. The later name — Adirondack — is geography leaking onto a form. The form is a plank machine: a seat that leans you back, a back of slats, wide arms that are really tables for a glass, legs that are not trying to look like Hepplewhite.

I start with the patent year because the internet will tell you the chair is ancient forest wisdom. It is early-twentieth-century American outdoor carpentry. It is closer to a packing crate that learned comfort than to a Windsor. That is a compliment. The crate logic is why amateurs can build a bad one and why a good one is still a drawing problem.

The sit is low. Getting out is work. If your client is not interested in that work, do not specify the form because the paint is cute. I have watched people love the photograph and hate the stand-up. Design is not a postcard.

The seat angle is the invention. You are in a lounge, not at a table. Putting Adirondacks around a 29-inch dining table is a common patio crime. The arms hit the apron. The person is in a recliner trying to eat salad. If you want dining, buy a dining chair. If you want sunset, buy the plank chair and a low table. Do not make one object do both just because they are both "outdoor."

Slats. Water has to leave. A cupped slat is a canoe. A tight array without gaps holds wet leaves. Fasteners stain. The original idea is generous boards, not a millwork grill. When manufacturers skinny the slats to save wood, they also skinny the comfort and add more edges for splinters.

Wood. Pine painted. Hemlock. Later cedar, cypress, teak, HDPE that wants to be wood. Paint was always a fair answer. Bare pine is a short story. If you leave a painted Adirondack unfinished at the end grain of every slat, you have left a hundred little mouths. I have more respect for a scuffed painted chair that gets a spring coat than for a "natural" pine that went black in the end grain by August.

The wide arm is a table. People will set a sweating glass on it. Finish the arm like a table or admit the rings. This is indoor knowledge wearing boots.

I will not sell you an Adirondack as a Keith Fritz line. We do not. Adjacent means I can read a chair drawing. The Westport chair is a drawing. Copy it with respect or change it with a reason. Do not add a cupholder CNC'd like a boat console and call it heritage. Do not steepen the sit to "dining height" and keep the name. Names are duties.

Paint color is not the form. Cottage red and sage green sold a lot of chairs. The sit is still low. If you change the color and keep a bad seat angle, you have changed a photograph. Lee's problem was guests and a lawn. Your problem might be a deck and a table. Those are different drawings. Call yours something else if you change the sit.

If you make furniture, build a mockup and get out of it fifty times. If you buy furniture, sit, then stand, then decide. If you are a designer, keep these off the dining patio or lower the table and change the evening. The chair is a lounge. Let it lounge.

Craftsman taste will try to take the porch more seriously as design. Mission outdoor is a shorter chapter than the catalogs want, and it still has something to teach about oak and a roof.
