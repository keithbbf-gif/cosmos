The plane will tell you before the tag will.

Teak is *Tectona grandis*. Heartwood with oil in the cells. Silica enough to take the edge off a tool the way a sandy board takes the edge off a weekend. Boatbuilders put it on decks because a deck is a patio that has to work while wet and while the sun is a vertical insult. English garden makers — Barlow Tyrie from 1920, Lister, the benches that wanted to be left — borrowed the navy's tree. That is the honest lineage. The dishonest lineage is a stain named teak on a species that never saw a monsoon.

I care about the silica because it is a shop fact you can feel. If you have dressed teak, you know the dulling. If you have only bought a table, you still need the fact: this wood is not basswood. Fasteners and tools and sanding belts have opinions. So do lungs — silica dust is not a scented candle. Work it like a material, not like a lifestyle.

The oil is why people think they can ignore a film. Sometimes they can. Teak left bare will go silver. That silver is a surface, not a rot. Under it the heartwood can still be working. I like silvered teak. I like it the way I like a zinc counter that has already admitted use. What I do not like is the panic that sends a household to a can labeled teak oil every April, followed by a sticky table that collects pollen and then mildew. Many of those cans are linseed or a varnish-oil hybrid that wanted a sale. The tree already brought oil. Extra oil is a decision, not a sacrament.

If you want the brown, you are signing up for a ritual: clean, brighten if you must, oil or a penetrating finish, and the knowledge that the brown is a look you are buying against the sun. If you want the silver, you are signing up for a different ritual: clean the mildew, accept the color, keep the joints free of sitting water. Both are adult. The child choice is "I want it to look like the showroom forever and I do not want to touch it." That child is how film finishes get put on teak and then peel in maps.

Grade and origin matter and I will not fake a certificate I did not see. Plantation teak can be excellent or pale and soapy depending on age and cut. Old-growth stories are mostly stories at retail. Ask for heartwood, for thickness that is not veneer on a mystery core, for joinery that is not a dowel in end grain hoping for the best. FSC and legality are real papers. They are not a sit.

Veneer teak on a patio table is a short play. The sun cooks the glue line. The edge takes a chip. Then you have a photograph of a tree on a core that is already tired. I have more respect for a thinner solid slat than a thick-looking sandwich. Thickness you can measure from the edge with a thumbnail is the only thickness I believe.

This shop does not run a teak patio line. Adjacent means I know oil and silica from indoor exotics we have actually dressed, and I know a veneer when a client shows me a "teak" top that is a photograph. BBF collections stay indoor unless a page says otherwise. Do not let a warm brown chip launder a tree.

If you make furniture, cut drip edges and keep end grain off the slab. If you buy furniture, scrape a hidden underside with a thumbnail. Solid teak dusts gold-brown. A printed film pills. If you are a designer, write silver or brown on the sheet. If you leave it blank, the sun will write it, and the client will call it a defect.

Cedar and cypress are the American trees that tried to do some of this work without a monsoon and without a navy.
