Look for the evenness. Nature is not that polite.

In 1917 Marshall B. Lloyd, in Menominee, Michigan, patented a way to twist kraft paper around a wire and weave it on a loom into a sheet you could tack to a frame. Heywood-Wakefield bought the American rights. British and European makers took the idea and made a midcentury language of it. The chairs are light. The pattern is regular. The paint loves it because the surface is more even than a rattan mountain range. People still call it wicker. It is a factory textile that learned furniture.

Paper drinks. Wire rusts if the paint and the paper fail. A Lloyd Loom cousin left in a real rain is a sad object: swollen paper, rust halo, a sag. Porch or sunroom, yes, with paint. Patio, no, unless you like experiments. I have watched people buy "vintage wicker" that is this material and then hose it. The hose is how you find the paper.

The regularity is why designers like it. It photographs as calm. Historic rattan photographs as talk. Both can be good. They are not interchangeable in a wet climate. If you specify Lloyd Loom for a covered porch, you are in a tradition. If you specify it for a pool deck, you are in a wish.

The wire inside is why a torn chair can rust-halo through a cream paint. That halo is a diagnosis, not dirt. If you see it, water has been in the paper. Stop hosing. Dry it. Paint is a system after the dry, not before.

Indoor, this stuff can live a long time. It is part of a twentieth-century sitting room story as much as a porch story. Adjacent to our work: it is a skin on a frame, like cane, like a panel. We do not make it. We can respect a client who already owns it and wants a table that does not bully it. BBF collections should not caption a carved lattice as Lloyd Loom. The loom is a machine and a patent year.

I like these chairs when they are what they are: light, regular, painted, under a roof. I dislike them when a listing says "all-weather vintage wicker" and the photograph is a lawn. Vintage is not a climate. A 1917 method is a climate warning. Paper was never a raincoat. The loom made paper polite. It did not make paper waterproof. Polite and waterproof are different compliments.

Reproductions exist. Some use the method. Some use resin and the name. Ask. The name without the paper-and-wire is a perfume.

Paint on paper-and-wire is load-bearing. It is not a color whim. A bare worn patch is a drink. Touch it before the season, not after the first storm. I would rather see a chair that looks slightly over-painted than a chair that looks "natural" and is already a wick.

If you make furniture, you probably will not. If you restore it, keep water off and paint as a system. If you buy furniture, run a fingernail. Paper and resin feel different at the nick. If you are a designer, write 1917 method or write resin. The client deserves to know whether they bought a vine, a paper, or a die.

A nick that pills is paper. A nick that shreds shiny is polymer. A nick that shows a hair of metal is the wire announcing itself. Three nicks, three winters. I would rather look slightly rude at a flea market than look surprised in April. April is a bad time to learn materials. The chair already learned.

Mineral seats next. Stone that stays, concrete that tried to be modern, and the cold that nobody puts in the rendering.
