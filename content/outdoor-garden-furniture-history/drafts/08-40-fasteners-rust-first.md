I look at the screw before I look at the silhouette.

Indoor, a drywall screw in a showpiece is an insult I can see from the door. Outdoor, a drywall screw is a timer. The head rusts, the joint loosens, the stain writes a comet on the slat, and the review says the wood failed. The wood was a witness. The fastener was the crime.

Stainless. 304 is common. 316 is the marine cousin people mention when they are near salt. I will not recite a metallurgy course I did not teach. I will say: stainless is a family, it can still stain, it can still gall, and it is still better than a bucket of black phosphated screws from a drawer. If the site is a pool with chemicals or a coast, upgrade the conversation. If the site is a Midwestern porch, still do not use interior screws and hope.

Galvanic pairs. Aluminum frames with steel screws. Copper-treated wood with uncoated steel. Oak tannin with cheap iron. Bronze on teak is an old boat habit that still makes sense. Mix metals on purpose or isolate them. Mix them as a leftover from the hardware aisle and you have built a battery. Batteries eat furniture slowly and then all at once.

Staples in slings and weaves rust and then saw the fiber. Hidden fasteners in resin wicker hide the rust until the stain shows at a foot. I flip things. You should flip things.

Retightening is a feature. Outdoor wood moves. A bolted teak table that can be snugged in year three is a better object than a glued dowel shrine that cannot. Indoor, I chase a certain invisibility. Outdoor, I will let a cap nut show if it means the piece can be maintained. Adjacent pride has to bend.

A cap nut also keeps a thread from eating a thigh. I have sat on more than one picnic bench that was a hardware-store bolt with a proud hex. That is not rustic. That is a bruise. If the fastener shows, finish the showing: cap, dome, or a seat that never meets the metal. Indoor we already hide a screw from a bare arm. Outdoor the arm is more often bare. Be kinder than a hex head.

Staples and brads in outdoor slats are how a factory hides a count. I pry when I am allowed. If the slat is only stapled, the staple is the joint. The joint will narrate. Prefer a screw or a bolt you can face. Prefer a slat you can replace without destroying the next slat. Factory speed is not a climate.

This shop's indoor hardware is specified like we mean it — hinges, slides, the ghost of a cheap knob. The garden is the same sentence louder. BBF collections should not show decorative interior screws as if they were outdoor-rated. The essay is the warning label.

I carry the magnet habit from looking at "solid brass" indoor hardware that was steel in a costume. Outdoor the costume fails faster. A magnet will not catch every lie — some stainless is barely magnetic, some platings confuse you — but a rust bloom around a pretty head is not confused. Believe the bloom. Replace the family of fasteners, not just the one you can see from the camera side. The camera side is never the only one that ate water.

If you make furniture, put the fastener schedule on the shop drawing, not in your head. If you buy furniture, carry a magnet and a skeptical eye. If you are a designer, write the metal. "Stainless look" is a plating. Plating is a film. Films die.

I have backed out a pretty black screw that was already orange under the head. The head was the costume. The thread was the winter. Replace the family. Coat the new heads if the look needs to match. Do not leave cousins in the same joint. Cousins start batteries. Batteries eat quietly and then all at once.

Finish next. Outdoor finish has to fail in public. Pick a failure you can live with.
