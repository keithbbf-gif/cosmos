If you wanted heavy, you wanted a different metal.

Aluminum's outdoor gift is that it does not make the red rust coin steel makes. Its outdoor bill is everything else. Light chairs blow. Light tables walk. Thin tube ovals. Castings can crack at a sharp inside corner if someone designed them like a drawing, not like a pour. Oxidation is white and powdery when it shows. Heat on a dark casting is a branding iron.

Cast versus tube. Cast aluminum dining chairs that want to look like iron are buying weight back on purpose. Good, if the wind is real and the casting is decent. They are not "the romance of iron" and they will not rust-stain the same way. They can still pit. They can still get a coat that maps. Tube is the stacking, restaurant, sling world. Tube is also the world of cheap gauges. Flex the arm. If the arm is a noodle, the weld will be the historian.

Fatigue. Aluminum can work-harden and then work-crack in a joint that flexes every sit. Steel often warns you by bending. Aluminum can go from fine to a crack. Overbuild the joint. Do not design a spring.

Dissimilar metals. Stainless screws in aluminum can be fine with isolation or a thought. Cheap steel screws in aluminum in a wet climate can make a mess. Think like a boat again. The patio is a slow boat that never leaves the dock.

Anodizing and powder coat are films or conversions, not the soul of the metal. Bare architectural aluminum is a look. Furniture-store bare aluminum is often a cost cut that will water-spot and oxidize into a dull map. Pick a system.

Umbrella holes in aluminum tables are a special little failure. The hole is a drain if you are lucky and a saw if the insert is sharp. The umbrella is a sail. The light table becomes a weather vane. I have seen tabletops lift and take a glass with them. Weight the base, tie the sail, or skip the umbrella and build shade that belongs to the building. A hole is not a peristyle.

I already told you I will not invent a patio extrusion line. Adjacent is the feel of lightness. Indoor, I chase a certain gravity in a table when you put a hand on it. Outdoor, I will accept a light chair if the brief is hail and a garage. I will not accept a light table that walks every time a storm comes through and then chips its coat on the house wall. Weight the base or change the metal or plan the storage.

Cast aluminum arms that imitate iron scrolls will still be aluminum in July. The scroll does not cool the metal. If the household sits in shorts, specify a sling, a strap, or a wood cap, or specify shade. I will not let a "traditional" silhouette exempt a forearm from physics. Tradition is a pattern. Heat is a measurement.

If you make furniture, radius the inside corners of a casting in the drawing. If you buy furniture, sit and twist. If you hear a tick, find it now. If you are a designer, write cast or tube, and write whether the site is a wind tunnel. A fifteenth-floor balcony is not a courtyard.

Feet on aluminum will walk on a smooth slab. Caps help. They also hold water if they are cups. I want a foot that grips and drains. That is a small part and it is how a light table stays where you put it without becoming a boat.

Powder coat is the film most of these metals are wearing. It is not the metal. When it fails, you meet the truth underneath.
