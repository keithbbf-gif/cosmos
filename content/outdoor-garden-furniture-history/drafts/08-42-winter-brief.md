Draw the snow on the seat while you still have a choice.

Indiana winters are not a rumor. Water in a joint becomes a wedge. Foam becomes a brick and then a smell. A cover becomes a drum for ice and then a tear. A stack becomes a press that ovals a tube. I live here. I have seen April reveal what September pretended not to know.

Leave-it materials: stone, some concrete, teak that was detailed, certain metals with a film you accept, slings that can take a sag and a freeze if the fabric was honest. Leaving is a specification. It is not laziness. It is a climate choice that has to match the object.

Bring-in materials: rattan, Lloyd Loom, most cushions, indoor cousins, thin aluminum you do not want the wind to own, anything with a cup that holds ice. Bringing in is a labor choice. Labor needs a path and a room. If the path is a spiral stair and the room is already full of Christmas, you did not specify bring-in. You specified abandonment.

Covers. A good cover breathes or is used dry. A bad cover is a greenhouse taped to a wet sectional. I would rather see no cover and a clean snow sitting on a slat bench than a vinyl tomb. If you cover, vent. If you cannot vent, skip it.

Stacking we already praised. Winter stacking is only praise if the faces will not weld their coats together and the garage is not a pool. Put something between coats if you have to.

Salt. Walks get salted. Furniture near walks gets salted. Salt plus metal is a faster calendar. Move the bench or change the metal or stop pretending the city truck is not part of your design team.

Mice and garages. Textile stacks are housing. I have opened January bins that were a nest. That is not a joke about country life. That is a lid and a dry bin and a decision not to store bread with the cushions. Design the bin if you design the foam.

This shop's indoor work spends winter indoors. That sounds stupid and it is the point. We design for a climate we control. Outdoor adjacent is the refusal to forget January because a July photograph was pretty. BBF collections should not stage indoor chairs in a snowless rendering and call it year-round outdoor. The essay is January's representative in the file.

Ice on a sling can stretch a sit that was already tired. Ice in a tube can split a weld if water sat there all fall. Drain in November is a five-minute job that saves a March replacement. I will nag about drain holes until I am boring. Boring winters are the goal. Dramatic winters are how a light chair becomes a story you tell at dinner about the year the set died. I would rather you tell a story about the food.

If you make furniture, include the winter sentence on the shop ticket. If you buy furniture, walk the storage route with the actual chair in your hands, or admit you cannot. If you are a designer, put January on the mood board. One picture of the garage is worth six pictures of lemons.

I have watched a household buy a set that could not make the turn at the basement stair. The set then wintered on the patio under a cover that became a pond. The stair was the specification they skipped. Measure the stair as if it were a door on an indoor case. We already do that for a secretary. The garden chair does not get a pass because it is "just outdoor." Just outdoor still has to fit the house.

Now we read a patio the way we read a dining room — tape, path, climate, refusal — before anyone draws a set.
