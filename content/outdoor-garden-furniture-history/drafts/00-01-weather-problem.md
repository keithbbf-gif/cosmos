Weather is the client I cannot put on a conference call.

If I start with a lifestyle photograph — linen, lemon water, a hedge that never drops a leaf — you will look at the cushions, and that is a pleasant way to waste a Sunday and a bad way to understand a bench. The cushions are the easy part. The hard part is the object under them. The object has to take ultraviolet light that cooks a film, water that swells a joint, freeze that splits a poorly dried seat, and a person who sits down wet and stands up without wiping anything.

Outdoor furniture is a weather problem. I mean that as a compliment. Weather problems have seasons. They have fasteners that rust first. They have woods that carry their own oil and woods that only carry a warranty paragraph. Lifestyle problems have adjectives. I cannot hinge an adjective to a patio.

This shop makes indoor casegoods. Tables, cabinets, desks, the quiet work that leaves Ferdinand on our own trucks. I am not going to invent a garden catalog we do not run. I am going to talk about the cousin. The cousin has the same duties — hold a body, hold a plate, not lie about its joints — and a climate that does not care about our showroom lighting. Indoor furniture fails in private. Outdoor furniture fails where the neighbors can see it. That is why the history of garden sitting is a history of materials that either confessed the weather or pretended they were still in the parlor.

People will tell you outdoor furniture is about living outside. Living outside is a behavior. The furniture is an object that either helps the behavior or teaches you chemistry. A good bench makes the second sit as honest as the first. A bad bench leaves a rust tattoo on a white dress, or a wobble after August, or a conversation about who left the cover off in January.

There is history in the object, and we are going to walk it. Not because I want you to live in a peristyle. Because the old names are still hiding in the new cart pages. A porch is a room with a roof and a draft. A patio is a floor that forgot the roof. Teak is a tree with oil and silica, not a finish color. Wicker is a method. Wrought iron, on most of the pieces you will be offered, is mild steel wearing a grandmother's name. If you do not know which of those you asked for, you will buy the language of one and the problems of another.

I have watched drawings arrive with the word *outdoor* and a picture of an English garden that has a staff. I have watched drawings arrive with the word *teak* and a picture of a pale board that never saw Burma. I have watched drawings arrive with no word at all, just a rectangle and a note that says "something all-weather." All-weather is how you get a piece that looks right in May and wrong in March.

So this series is going to be rude in a useful way. We will name the climate. We will name the century when sitting outdoors stopped being architecture and started being a SKU. We will name the shop habits that keep a joint from teaching you physics after a thunderstorm. We will not invent a famous patio this shop built. We will not sell you an oil. We will not end with a coupon.

If you make furniture, this is a map of a case that looks simple because it has no back against a wall. If you buy furniture, it is a map of why two chairs that share a photograph do not share a winter. If you just like history, it is a furniture history that never got a clean textbook because the people who lived it were either serving tea on a lawn or hosing mildew off a cushion.

I want you to leave the first episode able to say one sentence without blushing: this is a seat that has to die in public, and the seat has duties. After that we can talk about Rome, and Coalbrookdale, and Westport, New York, and why a powder coat is a film, not a metal.

The lemon water can wait. The weather cannot.
