Stickley did not design a pool collection.

Gustav Stickley and the shops around American Craftsman made indoor furniture that looked like it could take a hit: oak, visible joinery, settles, straight chairs, a moral about honesty that was also a look. Some of that furniture lived on porches because the porch was a room and the room wanted the same moral. A settle on a porch is a good object. A settle in a puddle is a science experiment.

The catalogs will now sell you "Mission outdoor" as a finish color — dark, straight, maybe a slat back — in aluminum or resin or a wood that never saw a Stickley shop. The silhouette is cheap to print. The duty is not. Historic Mission outdoor, such as it was, is porch-grade oak with a finish that expected maintenance, under a roof, in a culture that already believed furniture should look like work. It is not stainless mesh. It is not a sectional.

Oak outdoors is a later draft. Here I only need this: quartersawn white oak is not a raincoat. Tyloses help heartwood white oak resist rot relative to red oak. Help is not a spell. End grain in a post foot standing in a planter saucer will still drink. I have more faith in a porch settle with a gap under the foot than in a "Mission" dining set whose legs are in the same puddle as the downspout.

Honesty as a look can become a lie. Exposed through-tenons on an outdoor table are beautiful and they are extra end grain. If you show the joint, you own the sealing of the joint. Indoor, I like a through-tenon because the room is polite. Outdoor, I like it if the species and the detail were chosen for water. Otherwise you have built a fountain into the apron.

I am closer to this taste than to fern-backed iron, so I have to watch myself. Preference is not climate. I would rather build you an indoor Mission table and tell you to keep it indoors than build a costume for the patio and hope the moral keeps the rain off. BBF collections that show Craftsman-adjacent indoor work are the home of this language. The porch can borrow it. The open slab should pay for a different material.

Hardware on a porch settle gets touched by wet hands and by people who use the arm as a rail. Specify it. Indoor Mission hardware can be iron that spots. Outdoor-adjacent hardware should not be interior steel with a story. I have replaced more than one "honest" dark metal pull that was honest only about rust.

Settles and benches again. A porch settle with cushions is a sofa that can be stripped. Take the cushions in. The oak can stay if the roof is real. That ritual is the Craftsman version of the villa. Staff or no staff, the textile still moves.

A through-tenon that is proud of the post will catch a coat and a hose. Ease it. Indoor, a proud tenon is a brag. On a porch it is also a snag and a drip. I like a brag I can live with. I do not like a brag that takes a sleeve every time someone stands up in a hurry.

If you make furniture, do not dark-stain a softwood Adirondack and file it under Mission. If you buy furniture, ask whether the piece is oak or "oak." If you are a designer, use Mission as a joinery conversation, not as a stain chip on an aluminum chair. The chair will not become more honest because the powder coat is brown.

Wicker is the other American porch language — lighter, talkier, easier to move, easier to ruin. It gets the next draft because the porch made wicker famous and the patio is still trying to steal the fame with plastic.
