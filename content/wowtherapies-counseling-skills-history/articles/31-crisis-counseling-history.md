**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

If you are in danger, call local emergency services.

In the small hours after 28 November 1942, Boston hospitals took in the burned and the bereaved from the Cocoanut Grove nightclub fire. Nearly five hundred people died; 492 is the figure most public histories print. Erich Lindemann, a psychiatrist at Massachusetts General Hospital, later reported on acute grief among the bereaved and among related groups in "Symptomatology and Management of Acute Grief," *American Journal of Psychiatry* 101 (1944): 141–148. Stanley Cobb and Lindemann had already published neuropsychiatric observations from the MGH census in *Annals of Surgery* in 1943. Alexandra Adler, working from Boston City Hospital's survivors, published a parallel 1943 JAMA paper. Two hospitals. One night. A literature of sudden loss.

This essay is a short-form history of crisis counseling as a *time-limited* skill package. It will not print a safety-planning protocol. It will not print victim photographs. The 1944 paper is a document. The doorway is not a picture of the dead.

## What Lindemann put on the page in 1944

Lindemann described acute grief as a syndrome with psychological and bodily features: tightness in the throat, shortness of breath, sighing, weakness, guilt, unreality. He argued that the picture might come at once, or delayed, or distorted, and that a psychiatric management of grief might reduce later complications. The paper's census mixed disaster-bereaved people with other losses, including wartime bereavement. Cocoanut Grove is the public name that attached to the work. It was not the only source of the observations.

The skill claim, for a later counseling profession, was that a helper could meet *acute* grief as a recognizable, time-bound state rather than as a personality or a lifelong neurosis. That claim is the ancestor of "crisis intervention" as a short form. It is also a medical claim that later grief researchers would argue with: what is "normal," what is "distorted," who decides. Those later arguments (including debates over grief diagnoses in successive DSM editions) are neighboring history. They are not a protocol on this page.

No stills of the club, the bodies, or the survivors as decoration. If a graphics pass needs an object, a 1944 title page is enough.

## Caplan 1964, Wellesley, and a preventive vocabulary

Gerald Caplan's *Principles of Preventive Psychiatry* (Basic Books, 1964) is the book this bibliography keeps. Primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention — words borrowed from public health — became a way to talk about intervening before a crisis, during it, and after it had become a disorder. Caplan and Lindemann had already been associated with the Wellesley Human Relations Service / Wellesley Project from the late 1940s, a community experiment in consultation and early intervention. *An Approach to Community Mental Health* (1961) is a neighboring Caplan title. The 1964 book is the one this file will carry.

Crisis, in that vocabulary, is a turning point with a limited duration, a precipitating event, a disturbance of coping, and a chance that the person will come out at a different level of functioning. The helper's job, as later crisis-intervention textbooks flattened it, was to be available, brief, focused on the present hazard, and linked to the person's ordinary supports. Flattening is what textbooks do. Caplan's own emphasis on consultation — working with other professionals rather than capturing every client — is the part the flattening often drops.

The Community Mental Health Centers Act (Public Law 88-164), signed in 1963, made 24-hour emergency services one of the expected functions of a new American clinic system. Crisis counseling as a *public* modality — not only a private-office craft — has that statute in its lineage. The centers that were built, underfunded, or closed are a later political history. The 1963 expectation is the dated object: crisis work as a community function, not a luxury.

## Telephones, 1958, and the volunteer ear

The Los Angeles Suicide Prevention Center opened in 1958, associated with Edwin Shneidman, Norman Farberow, and Robert Litman. A 24-hour telephone, trained listeners including nonprofessional volunteers, a link to hospitals and police, a research interest in suicide as a studyable act: that is the public description the founders later wrote. Other U.S. centers multiplied in the 1960s. Britain's Samaritans are a related volunteer history with a different passport. This file will not write a hotline script. It will say that the telephone became a crisis instrument, and that the skill of the unfilled second (essay 05) had to live in a receiver.

Volunteer labor is part of the skill package's class history. The professionalization fight — who may listen, under what license, with what liability — never ended. Essay 17 (counseling skills outside the counseling license) is the companion. Crisis work is one of the places the license question is sharpest, which is why this page stays historical and leaves the emergency sentence at the top.

## Debriefing as a later object, and the quarrel

Jeffrey T. Mitchell's critical incident stress debriefing (CISD), described for emergency workers in a 1983 *Journal of Emergency Medical Services* paper and later expanded as CISM, is a subsequent named package. It is not Lindemann 1944. It is not Caplan 1964. It is a 1980s occupational ritual that some departments made mandatory. Later reviews, including Cochrane-style assessments of psychological debriefing, questioned whether a single structured retelling prevented later disorder and whether it might harm some people. Psychological First Aid, as named in later NCTSN / National Center for PTSD field guides after the early 2000s, is another subsequent object, written partly against mandatory debriefing.

This series will not teach debriefing. It will not teach Psychological First Aid as a field protocol. It will say that crisis counseling's history includes a 1980s–2000s fight about whether a helper should *require* a telling, and that the fight is part of the short-form tradition's self-criticism. A speech site that printed the protocol would be doing the thing this series exists not to do.

## Short form, not a personality theory

Compared with the other modalities in this wave, crisis work is oddly uninterested in a theory of personality. That is its point. Lindemann offered a syndrome. Caplan offered a preventive frame and a time-limited crisis. Hotlines offered a clock and a phone. The package can be misused as a way of not staying with anyone. It can also be the only honest hour when the presenting fact is a fire, a death, a collapse, a night.

Psychodynamic listening wanted time. Cognitive manuals wanted records. Reality therapy wanted a plan. Crisis counseling, in this short history, wanted the next day to be survivable. The want is modest and easy to advertise. The 1944 paper is not modest about bodily grief. Keep the body in the history. A "check-in" that ignores the throat and the breath has already left Lindemann.

## A speech clinic, bad news, no protocol

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. It is not a crisis center. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice locates counseling in communication, feeding, and swallowing. Some CSD hours still sit next to acute news: a new diagnosis, a lost voice, a swallow that will not be as it was. Luterman's 1979 parent book and Holland's later wellness frame are this pack's nearer guides to those hours. They are not Caplan. They share with 1944 an interest in acute reaction as something a professional might meet without pretending it is a lifelong personality.

The rest is dates: 28 November 1942, Lindemann 1944, Wellesley as a late-1940s project, Caplan 1964, the 1963 statute, Los Angeles 1958, Mitchell 1983 as a later quarrel, and a refusal to print a plan you could mistake for care.
