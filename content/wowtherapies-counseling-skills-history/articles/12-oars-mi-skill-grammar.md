**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In April 1983 the British journal *Behavioural Psychotherapy* printed William R. Miller's "Motivational Interviewing with Problem Drinkers," twenty-six pages from the University of New Mexico that refused to treat "denial" as a drinker's personality. The reprint address was Albuquerque. The argument was older than the title: motivation, Miller wrote, was an interpersonal process, not a trait you waited for at the bottom of a bottle. Counselors who took credit for successes and blamed clients for failures were practicing a comfortable attribution. Clients who then heard themselves argue the other side of the counselor's case were practicing something worse. They were becoming, in the hour, the very "unmotivated" person the clinic had already named.

This essay is not a motivational-interviewing manual, and it is not the later modality piece in this pack. It is the history of a *named grammar* — four classes of counselor talk that trainers later bundled under the acronym OARS: open questions, affirmations, reflective listening, summaries. The letters are a teaching object. They are not a session. They will not be printed here as stems, drills, or a thing to try on a relative who drinks.

## Albuquerque, 1983: denial as a two-person event

Miller was already a psychologist of problem drinking, not a founder-myth waiting to be cast. The 1983 paper sat in a *clinical* section of a behavioural journal and borrowed from experimental social psychology: attribution, cognitive dissonance, self-efficacy. It also borrowed from Carl Rogers. Empathic process, the paper said, belonged in the same room as assessment feedback. The mix was the scandal. A Rogers hour was not supposed to aim at a drinking outcome. A behavioural hour was not supposed to refuse confrontation. Miller's hour did both, on purpose.

The paper's most durable historical claim is negative. "Denial," as alcoholism counselors used the word, was not a finding on a personality inventory. It was two kinds of client sentence — I am not an alcoholic; I do not have to abstain forever — produced inside a particular kind of staff conversation. If the counselor argued the disease model, the client learned the other brief. Social psychology had a name for that: people come to believe what they hear themselves say. Direct argument, the paper said, was a poor way to change anyone's opinion. That sentence is a research claim from 1983. It is not a license for a reader to interview a spouse.

A schematic diagram of a motivational process appeared in the article, and a six-step sequence for implementing the method. Later trainers would treat those pages as a skeleton they could rename. This series will not reprint the sequence as a protocol. The historical fact is that a *sequence* was already thinkable in 1983, before the four-letter grammar had a nickname. The mouth had jobs. They had not yet been alphabetized for a laminated card.

Prochaska and DiClemente's stages of change, already circulating in the early 1980s, gave Miller a developmental frame: contemplation and determination as early steps a therapist might influence. The paper does not invent those stages. It uses them. A later industry would plaster the stages on posters and forget that Miller's 1983 problem was the *hour*, not the poster.

## Guilford, 1991: a grammar finds a co-author

Stephen Rollnick, working in the United Kingdom, had been teaching the 1983 method and wanted a fuller account. Miller's later public history of the collaboration — "The Evolution of Motivational Interviewing," a 2023 *Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapy* essay that looks back at the same journal — describes a year of talk that became a book. *Motivational Interviewing: Preparing People to Change Addictive Behavior* (Guilford, 1991) is the dated object. The subtitle still said addictive behavior. The method was already leaking into other rooms.

What the 1991 book did for skill history was to treat certain counselor acts as *classes*. Open questions were distinguished from closed ones, not as a personality but as a syntactic choice that changed who talked next. Affirmations were distinguished from praise-as-control: a recognition of the other person's labor, not a sticker. Reflective listening inherited the restatement problem already told in this pack — the difference between a mirror and a steered restatement. Summaries were named as a special case of reflection, a pile at a turning point. Four classes. Four jobs for the mouth. Trainers needed a way to remember them in a morning workshop. They got an acronym.

OARS is therefore younger, as a *word*, than the method it labels. Workshop handouts adapted from David Rosengren and from Miller and Rollnick's second edition (2002) treated "the basics, OARS" as early-and-often technique. That is a pedagogical event. It is not a discovery of four new human behaviors. Open questions are older than Albuquerque. Affirmation is older than Guilford. Reflection is the mid-century reel. Summary is what a clerk does at the end of a charge. The historical claim is narrower: these four were bundled, ranked as *core*, and coded as if they were the method's grammar rather than its optional decoration.

The 2002 second edition changed the subtitle to *Preparing People for Change*. Addictive behavior dropped out of the title because the method had already been borrowed by medicine, probation, and health promotion. The 2013 third edition, *Helping People Change*, rearranged the house: four processes (engaging, focusing, evoking, planning) and a fuller account of "spirit" — partnership, acceptance, evocation, and, newly named, compassion, added because the same techniques could be used in self-serving ways. The 2023 fourth edition, *Helping People Change and Grow*, is another revision of the same grammar, not a new alphabet. A reader who meets OARS on a laminated card in 2026 is meeting a teaching fossil. The fossil has a publication history. It does not have a soul.

Miller and Rollnick's 2013 text also shifted the verbs. Asking, affirming, reflecting, summarizing: gerunds, to stress that the skills were acts in time. Rosengren's practitioner workbook kept OARS because an acronym sticks in a trainee's pocket. That quarrel — process language versus pocket language — is part of the grammar's history. It is not a reason to print the pocket.

## MITI: the grammar becomes a code

Once a method can be taught in a morning, funders ask whether the morning "took." The Motivational Interviewing Skill Code (MISC), developed by Miller around 2000 at what was then the Center on Alcoholism, Substance Abuse, and Addictions (CASAA) at the University of New Mexico, tried to hear both clinician and client language. It was long. Trials needed something shorter.

The Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) code, first publicly released as version 2.0 in 2005, is the historical object that matters for OARS. Theresa B. Moyers and colleagues described a one-pass system for a twenty-minute slice. A 2005 *Journal of Substance Abuse Treatment* paper — Moyers, Martin, Manuel, Hendrickson, and Miller, "Assessing competence in the use of motivational interviewing" — made the competence claim researchable. Later manuals (3.0, 3.1, then 4.0 / 4.1 / 4.2.1, the last of those circulating as an unpublished 2014 manual with later FAQ revisions) changed globals and behavior counts. CASAA's archive still holds the older sheets. They are coding instruments. They are not homework.

What MITI did to OARS was to make the grammar *countable in someone else's ear*. A reflection could be simple or complex. A question could be open or closed. Affirmations and summaries had codes. Ratios — reflections to questions, open questions to closed — became numbers a trial could print. The numbers are a historical event in the same family as Truax's 1961 Accurate Empathy Scale. A rater hears a slice. The slice talks about fidelity. Critics of MITI, like critics of Truax, said the house dialect was being scored as if it were the universe. The charge is old. The manual is specific.

This series will not reproduce MITI categories as a self-audit. A code that exists to keep a clinical trial honest is not a form for a reader to fill in. The existence of the code is the point: by the mid-2000s, OARS was no longer only a workshop mnemonic. It was a thing you could fail on paper.

## What the letters hide

Each letter smuggles a fight this pack has already named.

Open questions inherit the Socratic problem: a question can be a door or a trap. Motivational interviewing's preference for open over closed was a bet that the speaker should supply the next sentence. Closed questions still occur. The grammar never banned them. It ranked them.

Affirmations inherit a class anxiety. Praise from a professional can sound like a parent, a coach, or a person with the keys. Miller's 1983 paper already wanted an "affirming atmosphere" that protected self-esteem while dissonance was raised. That is a technical tension, not a vibe. Later editions warned that affirmation is not cheerleading. The warning exists because the failure mode is common.

Reflective listening inherits parroting. MITI's later distinction between simple and complex reflection is an open admission that reflection is not a mirror. Complex reflection adds meaning or emphasis the speaker had not quite said. The restatement problem does not disappear because a New Mexico coding team gave it a better name.

Summaries inherit the clerk and the preacher. A summary can check hearing. It can also edit the hour toward change-talk the counselor wants to hear. The grammar's honesty, when it is honest, is that summaries *select*. Selection is a power. Cultural humility, in a later essay in this pack, will ask whose life gets selected as the "important" part of the pile.

OARS also hides what it is not. It is not the spirit of the method. It is not the four processes of the 2013 edition. It is not a diagnosis of alcohol use. It is not a speech-language treatment. Trainers who taught OARS as if it were the whole of motivational interviewing produced a generation of students who could name four letters and still argue with a drinker. The 1983 paper had already explained why that argument would fail. The acronym did not read the paper for them.

## A drinkers' grammar in other rooms

Project MATCH in the 1990s used Motivational Enhancement Therapy, a relative, as one of three conditions in a large alcohol trial. That is modality history, and it belongs in the later MI essay. For skill history the trial matters because it made a certain *sound* of counselor talk look exportable: brief, empathic, feedback-bearing, coded. Once exportable, the sound left the alcohol clinic. Primary care, HIV clinics, dietetics, probation, and, eventually, some communication-disorders classrooms borrowed the letters.

An SLP who "uses OARS" is not thereby doing motivational interviewing, and is not thereby a licensed addiction counselor. The grammar traveled because it was teachable in a morning and because it forbade a certain kind of nagging. Communication clinics have their own nagging: practice this sound, use this strategy, swallow this way. The ethical border is the same one ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice would later draw for counseling in general: about communication, feeding, and swallowing, with referral when the hour becomes something else. OARS does not widen that border. It only names four ways of talking inside it.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. These pages are heritage education. They are not a claim that the clinic practices motivational interviewing, codes MITI, or treats addictive behavior. A family who meets an SLP after a new diagnosis is not a "problem drinker" in Miller's 1983 sense. The useful inheritance is smaller and stranger: American helping professions spent forty years turning a social-psychological argument about denial into a four-letter grammar, then into a coding manual, then into a laminated card. The card is the least interesting object in the pile. The 1983 paper is still the one that bites.
