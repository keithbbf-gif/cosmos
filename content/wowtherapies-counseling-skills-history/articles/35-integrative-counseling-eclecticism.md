**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1967 Arnold A. Lazarus published a short paper in *Psychological Reports* with a title that already chose a side: "In Support of Technical Eclecticism." He had co-written a behavior-therapy techniques book with Joseph Wolpe the year before. He was not asking the field to become theoretically promiscuous. He was arguing the opposite: that a practitioner could stay loyal to a theory and still borrow a technique that had been born in someone else's house. Mix the theories, he said, and you get contradictions. Borrow the methods, and you might get a wider kit.

Nine years later Springer issued *Multimodal Behavior Therapy* (1976). The acronym that escaped the book — BASIC ID — turned a seven-part assessment claim into something a student could print on a card. This essay is about that quarrel and the later handbooks that tried to civilize it. It is not a menu for WOW Therapies. Integrative counseling, as a historical object, is a set of arguments about how skills travel. It is not a permission slip to collect techniques from this series and run them at home.

## What Lazarus was refusing

Eclecticism, in mid-century American therapy, was often an insult. It meant a practitioner without a church. Lazarus tried to split the word. *Theoretical* eclecticism — stitching Freud to Skinner to Rogers at the level of explanation — would, in his view, produce a mash of incompatible claims about what a person is. *Technical* eclecticism would let a behaviorally minded clinician use a method that had been described in a gestalt or hypnotic or cognitive manual, without converting to that manual's metaphysics.

The 1967 note is nine pages of that distinction. It is easy to misread as "use whatever works." Lazarus was more stubborn. He wanted an array of techniques *and* a home theory that could say why you reached for one of them. In later chapters he was explicit that he did not favor theoretical integration. He thought combining theories in the hope of better methods produced "divergent and contradictory notions." You can disagree with him. You cannot, if you are writing history, pretend he was selling a smoothie.

*Multimodal Behavior Therapy* (1976) then offered a map of "modalities" in a different sense from this pack's use of the word. Lazarus meant channels of human functioning: Behavior, Affect, Sensation, Imagery, Cognition, Interpersonal relationships, and Drugs/biology — BASIC I.D. (the last letter is the D of drugs/biology). He later dropped "behavior" from the brand and wrote *The Practice of Multimodal Therapy* (1981) and later editions. The 1973 papers already used "multimodal behavior therapy"; the 1976 book is the object this bibliography pins. The card is a historical object. It is not reprinted here as a self-scan. A reader who wants a multimodal assessment is looking for a clinician, not a webpage.

## Four ways of not being a school

By the 1980s the people who were tired of school loyalty had names for their refusals. John C. Norcross and colleagues, surveying and editing, sorted the traffic into types that later handbooks repeated.

Technical eclecticism: Lazarus's bet. Take techniques; leave the churches.

Theoretical integration: try to make a new theory out of two old ones. Paul Wachtel's *Psychoanalysis and Behavior Therapy* (1977) is a dated landmark of that ambition, a cyclical psychodynamics that wanted behavioral work without pretending the unconscious had clocked out. Historians can name the book. This series will not teach it.

Common factors: the older joke with a thesis. Saul Rosenzweig in 1936 pointed at ingredients that rival therapies might share. Jerome Frank's *Persuasion and Healing* (1961) made demoralization and a ritual of persuasion a comparative claim. Lambert's later "four-factor" popularizations, Luborsky's 1975 comparative paper, and Bruce Wampold's *The Great Psychotherapy Debate* (2001; 2nd ed. with Zac Imel, 2015) are the research weather. This pack's Dodo-bird essay is the place for that argument. Here the point is narrower: if common factors do most of the work, then "integrative" might mean getting the relationship and the expectancy right, not collecting more techniques. If specific techniques do more than the Dodo allows, then Lazarus's kit looks prudent. The field did not settle this. Anyone who tells you it did is selling a workshop.

Assimilative integration, named later, is a compromise the handbooks had to make room for: stay in one home theory and import a technique without converting. Lazarus thought that muddied the water. Norcross's later editions gave it a section anyway. The existence of the section is the history. The section is not a recipe.

## A society, then a handbook

Marvin Goldfried, Hans Strupp, and Paul Wachtel were among those who, in the early 1980s, kept a list of people interested in rapprochement across orientations. In the summer of 1983 an organizing committee that included Lee Birk, Jeanne Phillips, George Stricker, and Barry Wolfe helped turn the list into the Society for the Exploration of Psychotherapy Integration (SEPI). The name was careful. *Exploration*. Not a new school with a diploma. SEPI's annual meetings and its journal became the institutional address for a conversation that had been happening in hallways.

Norcross and Marvin R. Goldfried's *Handbook of Psychotherapy Integration* appeared from Basic Books in 1992. Oxford later took the book. A second edition is dated 2005; later Oxford / APA volumes followed. Cite the edition on the desk. The 1992 handbook is the first compilation of its kind that this series will treat as a dated object: technical eclecticism, theoretical integration, and common factors under one contents page, plus chapters on training and on combining psychotherapy with other modalities. Lazarus contributed to that world even while he kept saying theoretical integration was a mistake. A handbook can hold a dissenter. That is what a handbook is for.

Norcross's later career also ran "psychotherapy relationships that work" reviews — a common-factors cousin that tried to name *which* relationship elements had evidence behind them. Those APA tasking documents are historical objects in the same family. They are not a treatment plan for a reader of wowtherapies.com.

## What "integrative" did to skill teaching

Once a technique could travel without its theory, the training problem changed. Ivey's microskills, Carkhuff's five-point ratings, Egan's staged helper, Hill's exploration–insight–action sequence, Miller's later motivational interviewing — all became available as *units* a program could assign in a semester without converting the student to a church. Integrative talk made that shopping respectable. It also made it easier to forget that a skill on a rating sheet is not the same object as the same-named skill inside a theory that can say when *not* to use it.

Person-centered work as a skill package, in this series, already warned that Rogers's 1957 conditions were a claim about necessary and sufficient change, not a drawer of tools. Cognitive counseling as a skill set warned that a thought record is a historical object, not a download. An integrative syllabus that lifts "reflection" from one chapter and "a miracle question" from another, without the Milan team's ethics or de Shazer's brief-therapy constraints, is doing technical eclecticism in Lazarus's sense only if it has a home theory that can refuse. Without the refusal, it is a pile.

Counselor education after CACREP (1981) often required students to "develop an integrative personal model." That assignment is itself a historical practice. Some students wrote a genuine theoretical integration. Many wrote a tasteful eclecticism with citations. A few wrote common-factors paragraphs and called it a model. The assignment produced a generation of clinicians who could name four schools and a relationship. It did not settle which of Norcross's types they were practicing. The live question in supervision — "why that, this hour?" — is older than SEPI. Integrative language gave supervisors a vocabulary for asking it. It did not answer it.

## Not a menu, and not this clinic's brand

WOW Therapies is a Southeast Arkansas speech-language practice. Its rooms already mix tasks that a purist would file under several headings: a swallow result, a stalled sound, a family's grief, a school 504 meeting, a device that VR might fund. That mix is the job of an allied-health clinic. It is not "multimodal therapy" in Lazarus's 1976 sense, and it is not psychotherapy integration in Norcross's. Calling every mixed hour "integrative counseling" would annex a psychotherapy debate onto ASHA's 2016 counseling domain. The scope document does not do that. Neither does this page.

The useful inheritance is smaller. When a later SLP textbook lists "counseling skills" beside "techniques," it is living in the world Lazarus and Norcross made thinkable: skills can be named apart from founder biographies. The danger is the menu. This series will describe how listening was taught, how OARS became a grammar, how narrative re-authoring was packaged. It will not invite a reader to assemble those chapters into a self-administered integrative treatment. If you want care, you want a licensed clinician. If you want history, you can stand in the 1967 quarrel and watch the 1992 handbook try to keep the quarrel from becoming a new church.

Improvement, in Keith's sense for this pack, includes knowing when a word has become a shopping cart. "Integrative" is one of those words. Technical eclecticism, at least, had the honesty to admit it was borrowing. Common factors had the honesty to suspect the borrowings might not be the engine. Theoretical integration had the ambition to rebuild the engine. A speech clinic can read all three as history. It cannot, on this URL, sell them as a fourth.
