**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1951 Julian Press in New York published *Gestalt Therapy: Excitement and Growth in the Human Personality*, by Frederick S. Perls, Ralph F. Hefferline, and Paul Goodman. Three names on a spine. Two rooms inside the covers. Volume-logic later readers still use: a book of experiments, drawn from Hefferline's work with Columbia students, and a book of theory, much of it written by Goodman from Perls's notes and from a New York conversation that included Laura Perls even when the title page did not. The 1951 object is the start. Contact and awareness were not yet a workshop poster. They were an argument about how a person meets a world at a boundary.

This essay will not teach the empty chair. It will say when a chair on a stage became the public face of a 1951 book, and why that demonstration is a poor synonym for the skill package.

## PHG, and who wrote which hour

Frederick ("Fritz") and Laura Perls had left South Africa for New York in the late 1940s. *Ego, Hunger and Aggression* already existed as a Perls volume from the South African years. The 1951 book gave the work a name American readers could file: Gestalt. The German school of experimental psychology that had studied figure and ground was in the background. The clinic in the foreground was not a laboratory of optical illusions. It was a claim that *contact* — the meeting of organism and environment — could be interrupted, and that the interruption was the suffering.

Hefferline, a Columbia psychologist who had been in therapy with Laura Perls, organized the experiments: written tasks a student could do with attention, sensation, and language. Those experiments are historical objects. This series will not reprint them as homework. Goodman, poet, anarchist, later famous for *Growing Up Absurd* (1960), wrote theoretical chapters that made "self" a process at the contact boundary rather than a furniture of traits. Later Gestalt historians have spent decades arguing how much of the theory is Goodman, how much Fritz, how much Laura's uncredited labor. The argument is the authorship history. It is also a skill history: a modality that talks about contact was itself a contact among three writers who did not share a temperament.

Laura Perls's later interviews (the 1977 conversation published in *The Gestalt Journal*) placed the New York Institute's birth after the book. Forty people, she said, arrived for study at the apartment. Fritz and Laura each took a group. 1952 is the Institute's own founding year. Cleveland's institute followed in 1953 after a ten-day intensive. Isadore From became a traveling teacher. The New York apartment is the room this essay prefers to Esalen's later lawn, because the apartment is where the 1951 book first became a school.

## Contact, awareness, here-and-now — as claims, not drills

"Contact" in PHG is not a synonym for eye contact. (Ivey's 1968 attending behaviors, in this pack's microskills essay, are a different technology.) Contact is the quality of meeting: figure forming against ground, a need becoming action, a boundary that can be lively or dull. "Awareness" is not a mindfulness brand of the 2000s. It is the 1951 bet that a person can notice what is happening in the present — sensation, interrupting, the unfinished — without immediately explaining it from childhood. "Here-and-now" became the slogan that survived. Slogans are what workshops need. The 1951 text is slower than the slogan.

Psychodynamic listening, two files back, treated the past as a structure in the present. Gestalt contact skills treated the present as enough surface to work. The fight with analysis was explicit. So was a quieter fight with Rogerian reflection. A Gestalt teacher who "stayed with" a sensation was not trying to restate a feeling-word at Carkhuff Level 3. They were trying to keep a figure from being talked to death. Supervisors who later mixed the traditions produced students who reflected a feeling and then asked the student to "be" the feeling in an empty chair. That mix is a late-century casserole. It is not 1951.

## The empty chair as a demonstration, and the room that made it famous

Two-chair work has cousins in Moreno's psychodrama, which is older. Fritz Perls's later public demonstrations — a person, a chair, an absent other, a voice thrown across the gap — became what television and training films could show. The move is a historical demonstration. This series will not give the setup, the sequence, or a prompt to occupy an absent person's chair. A demonstration a reader could run alone is a protocol. The protocol stays off this page.

The room that made the chair famous was not the 1952 apartment. It was Esalen Institute, Big Sur, founded in 1962 by Michael Murphy and Dick Price. Perls taught there from the mid-1960s until he left for British Columbia in 1969. He died in Chicago on 14 March 1970. The "hot seat" — one person up, a group watching, Fritz conducting — is the image American counseling students still borrow when they say "Gestalt." Laura Perls did not run that circus. She stayed with the New York Institute for decades after Fritz left the city in the mid-1950s. The two Gestalt cultures, New York and Esalen, are not one modality. Students who learned only the film learned the louder culture.

Showmanship is the fight inside the skill. Contact requires a helper who is actually there. A guru who performs contact for an audience can become the figure that eats the field. Critics said Fritz had become the method. Friends said the method had always included experiment and surprise. Both can be true about a man and still leave Laura's quieter New York practice as the other half of the 1951 book. A counseling-skills history that only has the hot seat has failed the title page.

## What trainers later listed as "Gestalt skills"

Counselor-education theories surveys, by the 1980s, listed a menu: now-language, experiments, dream work as a here-and-now event, polarities, unfinished business, the empty chair. Menus are how master's programs survive. They are also how a 1951 argument about the contact boundary becomes a bag of tricks. Ivey's microskills tradition could film a "Gestalt confrontation." Egan's skilled-helper stages could absorb an "experiment" as an action stage. The package leaked. Leakage is not theft. It is how modalities become the furniture of a field that no longer remembers Julian Press.

Cross-cultural criticism arrived on the "now." A demand to speak in present-tense English, to emote on cue, to occupy a chair in a group of strangers, is a North American workshop aesthetic. It is not a human universal of contact. Gestalt writers after the 1970s, including people who stayed with the New York Institute's field-and-contact vocabulary, tried to write that warning back in. The first famous films were less careful.

Feminist readers asked who got interrupted, and whose "excitement and growth" the 1951 subtitle named. A man conducting a woman's anger in public, in 1968, is a dated scene. It does not automatically condemn every later Gestalt clinician. It does require the history to keep the scene.

## A speech clinic, a boundary, no chair

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. It does not run hot-seat groups. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice places counseling in communication, feeding, and swallowing. The CSD hour already knows a version of contact: a person whose speech or swallow is the figure, a family in the ground, a clinician who can either rush to a drill or stay with the news. Luterman's parent work and Holland's wellness frame are this pack's CSD inheritance. They do not need Fritz's chair.

They might still need the 1951 warning that talking *about* an experience can be a way of not having it. A parent narrating a diagnosis at high speed is not failing a Gestalt test. They may be surviving a Tuesday. A clinician who has only the Esalen film may push for "feeling" when the ethical task is information, silence, or a drink of water. Modalities are greedy. Heritage education on a speech site can name Gestalt contact skills as a mid-century package without putting a second chair in the treatment room.

The therapy-history pack may keep Fritz as a founder with a cigar and a myth. This pack keeps PHG, the 1952 apartment, Laura's long New York, the Esalen demonstration as a warning, and the refusal to teach the chair. Contact is a word that survives because it is better than the poster. Awareness is a word that dies when it becomes a homework app. The 1951 book is stranger than both posters. Strangeness is what a history is for.
