**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Library catalogs list David Luterman as born in 1934. The more useful date for a speech clinic is 1979, when Little, Brown issued *Counseling Parents of Hearing-Impaired Children*, with a foreword by Mark Ross. The book is a professional object you can still pull from a stack. It told a generation of audiologists and speech-language pathologists that the first job with a family might not be a packet of facts. The facts would still be needed. They would not be enough.

Luterman is living at last verification (2026-09-14). This page uses public books, public teaching posts, and public awards. It does not discuss private health or private life. It does not teach his method as a script. The CSD counseling essays in this pack already named the problem he made unavoidable. This one is the teacher who wrote the desk book.

## Emerson, and a nursery that was a method

Luterman taught for decades at Emerson College in Boston and is professor emeritus there. Public biographical notes from professional organizations describe a full-time teaching career of about forty years and a counseling course that continued long after the formal retirement — a rare administrative fact, not a personality profile. He also directed a family-centered nursery for hearing-impaired children associated with that work. The nursery is the better clue to the books than a CV line. A graduate student could watch what happened when information was delivered as if the parent were a storage device. The room answered.

He holds the CCC-SLP, is a Fellow of the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, and received ASHA's Frank Kleffner Clinical Achievement Award in 2011. Those are public honors. They locate him in the speech-language profession, not in counselor licensure. The distinction matters on a WOW Therapies page. Luterman taught counseling *as a CSD problem*. He did not, by writing, turn SLPs into LPCs.

## 1979, 1984, and a retitled life

*Counseling Parents of Hearing-Impaired Children* (Little, Brown, 1979; ISBN in catalogs 0-316-53750-0) is the first door. The title is dated in a way later editions would repair: "hearing-impaired" as the era's professional English. The contents were already arguing with the information-dump. Parents were not a delivery system for the audiogram.

Five years later Little, Brown issued *Counseling the Communicatively Disordered and Their Families* (1984). The census widened from parents of deaf and hard-of-hearing children to the broader communication-disorders caseload. A 1985 review in a Canadian speech journal summarized the book's own story of the author's shift: from a professional who mostly provided information to one who would let affect into the room, on the bet that the information would then have somewhere to land. That is a reviewer's paraphrase of a published argument. It is not a quote from a session.

Later printings, under PRO-ED, took the title *Counseling Persons with Communication Disorders and Their Families*. Person-first English replaced "the communicatively disordered." ERIC ED583622 records a sixth edition in 2017 (ISBN 978-1-4164-1057-7) and notes a 1979 first-publication claim for the lineage — the 1979 parent book counted as the start even though the 1984 title is the direct ancestor of the later name. Some professional podcasts still say "fifth edition" in passing. The object on the 2017 desk is the sixth. [VERIFY the numbering against the copy you hold on upload week.] A new chapter in that sixth edition, per ERIC's abstract, summed a fifty-year career. Barry M. Prizant wrote a foreword.

The through-line is stubborn enough to survive the retitling. Diagnosis in CSD produces grief, anger, relief, and a rearrangement of a household. A professional who only explains the test has not finished. A professional who only "reflects feeling" and withholds the audiogram has not finished either. Luterman's published claim was that affect is not a detour around information. It is the condition under which information can be heard. The measurement tradition in this pack — Truax's slices, Ivey's attending units — did not build its scales in that nursery. The two traditions grew up in the same decades and did not always share a hallway. That is why Luterman has a figure essay here and not only a mention in the SLP counseling skill piece.

## What he would not let the field become

CSD training, especially in audiology, can sound like a pipeline: test, explain, recommend a device, schedule the follow-up. The pipeline is not wicked. It is incomplete. Luterman's books kept returning to locus of control, to the difference between a professional who needs to be the expert in the room and a family that has to live the diagnosis at 7 p.m. He read humanistic and existential sources in public — Kierkegaard and Erikson appear in the 1984 contents as the reviewer listed them — and he did not pretend those sources were speech science. He used them to name a counseling stance inside a technical profession.

He also argued, in later public teaching associated with the Stuttering Foundation and similar professional venues, against the clinician as miracle-worker. The phrase is a published teaching theme, not a session transcript. A clinician who needs to be the one who "fixes" the child can crowd out the parent's competence. Family-centered, in that literature, is not a poster. It is a claim about who holds the life. This series will not turn that claim into a worksheet of questions to ask a parent. The historical point is that CSD counseling had to be argued *against* the field's own expertise-performance.

Grief, in this lineage, is not a five-stage poster. The grief-and-adjustment essay in this pack is the place for that quarrel. Luterman's contribution was to insist that communication diagnosis is one of the events that can start it, and that the SLP or audiologist is often the person in the room when it starts. Ignoring it is not neutrality. It is a technique of its own.

## What this page will not do

It will not invent a family. It will not composite a child in a Southeast Arkansas clinic. It will not print parent-session homework. Luterman's later editions include chapters whose titles (on the 2017 contents: diagnostic process, techniques of counseling, group process, working with families) look like they could be copied as homework. They are textbook chapters for students under supervision. They are not a consumer protocol.

He remains a living teacher of a living book. Public interviews and podcasts through professional nonprofits are part of the record; they are not a license to speculate about his health or his household. If a later editor re-opens this file, the first check is whether he is still living and whether the edition on the desk is still the sixth.

## Why a small-city speech practice still keeps the 1979 date

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. Its people deliver results that rearrange evenings: a child is not "just shy"; a swallow is not safe; a hearing test is not a formality. The temptation, in a rural clinic that already does too much in one hour, is to talk faster. Luterman's public argument is that speed is not the virtue the family needs. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice named counseling as a service-delivery domain about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It did not name Luterman. The inheritance is older than the PDF: once CSD admitted that information without affect is a failed delivery, every later syllabus had to decide whether to teach that admission or to hide it under "bedside manner."

The SLPWOW speech-pathology pack can keep the audiogram and the device. This pack keeps the teacher who made the other chair count. Complementary, not a reprint. The 1979 Little, Brown book is a small object. It changed what a communication-disorders student could be told was part of the job.
