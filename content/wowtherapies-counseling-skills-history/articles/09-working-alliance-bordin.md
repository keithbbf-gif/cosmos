**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1934 the *International Journal of Psycho-Analysis* printed Richard Sterba's "The Fate of the Ego in Analytic Therapy." Sterba described an alliance between the analyst and the patient's rational ego — a split in which a reasonable part of the person side with the work against the pull of the neurosis. The paper is an institute object. It is not a questionnaire. Forty-five years later Edward S. Bordin, in *Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice* (1979, volume 16, number 3), would take that psychoanalytic cluster, fuse it with later talk of a "real relationship," and say the resulting working alliance could be stated in forms generalizable to *all* psychotherapies. The 1979 paper's three features are still the ones a master's student can recite: agreement on goals, an assignment of tasks, the development of bonds. Reciting is not destiny. This essay is about how a 1934 ego-split became a 1989 inventory and then a meta-analytic industry — and about the fights that tried, and failed, to keep the alliance inside psychoanalysis.

## Institute words, American weather

Elizabeth R. Zetzel's "Current Concepts of Transference" (*International Journal of Psycho-Analysis*, 1956) is the usual English door to the phrase *therapeutic alliance*. Zetzel, working in a Boston analytic world still arguing Klein, ego psychology, and what a patient must already be able to do, treated the alliance as a non-neurotic joining: the healthy part of the ego linking with the analyst so that the work, including the later transference neurosis, could be borne. The developmental claim inside the paper — that early maternal reliability shows up as a later capacity to ally — is not a skills manual. It is a theory of who can even begin.

Ralph R. Greenson's "The Working Alliance and the Transference Neurosis" (*Psychoanalytic Quarterly*, 1965) and *The Technique and Practice of Psychoanalysis* (International Universities Press, 1967) split the vocabulary again. The working alliance, for Greenson, was a relatively rational, reality-based collaboration, not identical with the transference neurosis that analysis also required. He was willing to speak to the reality of the relationship — including, in examples that became famous inside institutes, acknowledging a justified criticism rather than interpreting it away. Some American analysts heard a leak in neutrality. Jacob Arlow, Charles Brenner, and later Mark Kanzer are among those who worried, in print, that "alliance" talk was a way of being extra-analytic: too real, too kind, too much of a two-person support. The fight is the history. A method that had defined itself against suggestion had to explain why two people were cooperating at all.

Karl Menninger's 1958 emphasis on the therapeutic contract, which Bordin cites, sits on the same shelf: work as an agreement, not only as a transference storm. Luborsky's later "helping alliance" measures (the 1976 line, then the Helping Alliance Questionnaire with Alexander in the 1980s) kept a psychodynamic accent while becoming countable. Vanderbilt process scales, another 1980s object, did similar work from a different shop. By the time Bordin wrote, the profession was already trying to get the relationship onto a form.

## Bordin 1979: three parts, many therapies

Bordin's opening is a period piece. He complains that the 1960s and 1970s had proliferated psychotherapies — Harper's 1959 list, Harper's 1975 supplement — toward a solipsism of methods. Research, he says, had suffered a matching lack of convergence. Donald Campbell's 1976 exhortation to practitioners to follow up their own cases is in the first paragraph. Then the thesis: the terms of the therapeutic working alliance have a psychoanalytic origin but can be stated so as to travel.

Two foundations, Bordin writes. One is Sterba (1934) on the rational-ego alliance, plus Menninger (1958) on the contract. The other is Zetzel (1956) and especially Greenson (1967) on the significance of the real relationship. Fusing them yields three features: agreement on goals, assignment of a task or series of tasks, development of bonds. He is explicit that he will treat these in psychoanalysis *and* try to show their application to other forms. He is also explicit that research should test that generalizability. The paper is a proposal, not a completed law.

The three features are easy to sloganize and hard to keep distinct. Goals are the shared picture of what would count as having done the work. Tasks are the specific actions the method requires — free association, thought records, exposure, a weekly hour of sitting. Bonds are the human attachment that makes the tasks tolerable. Bordin argued that different therapies would demand different alliance *profiles*. A behavioral hour and an analytic hour are not the same job, even if both need a bond. Strength of alliance, not kind, he guessed, would be the major factor in change. That guess is the sentence later meta-analyses were built to caress.

It is also the sentence that can be misunderstood as "relationship is everything, technique is nothing." Bordin did not empty the tasks. He made tasks one of three legs. A warm bond around a task nobody understands is, on his own scheme, a weak alliance. So is a brilliant protocol around a goal the client never signed. The 1979 paper is a theory of *fit* — of personalities to the demands of a particular working alliance — as much as it is a theory of warmth.

## Horvath 1989: the form that ate the field

Adam O. Horvath and Leslie S. Greenberg's "Development and Validation of the Working Alliance Inventory" (*Journal of Counseling Psychology*, 1989, volume 36, number 2) turned Bordin's three features into a 36-item self-report, with versions for client, therapist, and, in the wider literature, observers. Tracey and Kokotovic's short form, also 1989, made the instrument even more portable. The WAI is a historical object in this series, not a homework sheet. This pack will not print items. The dated fact is that after 1989 a graduate student could measure "alliance" on a seven-point scale and put the number in a thesis.

Once the number existed, it began to behave like a cause. Early alliance (often sessions three to five, in the later reviews) predicted outcome at a moderate level in study after study. Horvath and others spent the 1990s arguing about whose rating mattered most — the client's, usually — and whether alliance was a confound for technique or a mechanism of its own. The argument is not settled in this essay because it is not settled in the literature. What is settled as history is the instrument's success. Flückiger, Del Re, Wampold, and Horvath, in their 2018 synthesis in *Psychotherapy* (volume 55, number 4), noted that WAI-based inventories accounted for a large share of alliance–outcome studies, with a long tendency toward shorter forms. The 2018 paper, and its updates, are treated here as historical objects: dated attempts to summarize a pile of correlations. They are not a shopping list for a clinic, and they are not a promise that a good score will save a bad method.

Critics of the alliance literature — from within research, not only from analytic purists — pointed at reverse causation (people who are already improving like their therapist more), at therapist effects (some helpers pull better alliances across clients), and at the tautology problem (alliance items that already sound like outcome). Bordin had asked for tests of generalizability across modalities. The literature often answered with a single number pooled across modalities, which is a different question. Pooling is how meta-analysis gets a headline. Headlines are how a 1934 ego-split becomes a LinkedIn post about "the relationship."

## Not destiny

Alliance is not a personality transplant. It is not a guarantee. It is not, in Bordin's own pages, a substitute for tasks. Analytic writers who feared that "alliance" meant being nice were not only being snobs. They had watched suggestion return in American clothes. Research writers who treated the WAI as a sufficient account of healing were not only being sloppy. They had watched technique wars fail to produce the winnowing Bordin's first paragraph wanted, and they reached for the one construct that kept correlating.

Common-factors arguments, from Rosenzweig's 1936 joke-thesis through Frank's *Persuasion and Healing* (1961) to Wampold's later books, sit next door in this pack. The alliance literature is their most successful measurement campaign. Success has a cost. A construct that predicts a little in every school can be used to stop arguing about schools. Sometimes that is wisdom. Sometimes it is a way of not noticing that the *tasks* two people agreed to are idle, coercive, or a poor fit for the problem in the room. Bordin's three-part scheme, read honestly, still allows that notice. A bond cannot launder a task.

There is a political history too. Who gets to agree on goals? A child in a school-counseling office, a court-mandated client, a parent who brought a teenager, a person whose insurance will pay only for a named diagnosis — these are not the 1979 dyad of two volunteers. Feminist and multicultural critics said the "agreement" was often a translation of the helper's goals into the client's mouth. Rehabilitation counseling, with its statutory vocational aims, has always had tasks that arrive from outside the pair. The WAI can still be filled out in those rooms. The number will not tell you whether the goal was the person's.

## A communication clinic and a three-part word

Speech-language work is full of tasks. A swallow maneuver is a task. A fluency hierarchy is a task. A home program is a task. Families either share a goal — eat safely, be understood at school, keep the voice through a teaching year — or they sit in a polite war about whether anything is wrong. Bordin's vocabulary fits those rooms without turning an SLP into a licensed professional counselor. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice already named counseling as a service-delivery domain about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It did not name Sterba. The inheritance that earns its keep in Southeast Arkansas is plainer. When a family and a clinician do not agree on the goal, no amount of warmth will make the tasks make sense. When the tasks are clear and the bond is contempt, the child still has to produce a sound in a room that feels unsafe. That is alliance talk without a WAI. It is also why this series will not tell a reader that "the relationship heals." Healing, in Bordin 1979, was never a bond alone.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice, not an institute and not a psychotherapy outcome lab. Its education pages can afford to keep the 1934–1979–1989–2018 sequence as history: Sterba's ego alliance, Zetzel's therapeutic alliance, Greenson's working alliance versus transference neurosis, Bordin's three traveling parts, Horvath and Greenberg's inventory, Flückiger and colleagues' syntheses as dated piles of evidence. The sequence is a skill-and-measurement story. It is not a fate. People leave helpers they like. People improve with helpers they barely warm to, if the tasks fit. The literature's moderate correlations are the opposite of magic. They are a modest number that got famous.
