**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Charles B. Truax and Robert R. Carkhuff put *Toward Effective Counseling and Psychotherapy: Training and Practice* into Aldine's 1967 Chicago list, and a rating technology left the Wisconsin weather for the textbook market. The Accurate Empathy Scale had been Truax's 1961 grant object. Unconditional positive regard and genuineness scales followed. The 1967 book bound the claim: you could rate a slice of tape, and the rating would talk about outcome. Critics later said the slices were too short and the raters too trained in the house style. The critics did not put the reel back in the box.

## Wisconsin, then a two-volume primer

Carkhuff came out of the same late-1950s and early-1960s Wisconsin project that tried to take Rogers's conditions into a hospital population that did not share the university clinic's manners. Truax was the scale-maker of that weather. Carkhuff became the trainer who translated the scales into a curriculum other departments could run. *Sources of Gain in Counseling and Psychotherapy* (with Bernard G. Berenson, 1967) and *Beyond Counseling and Therapy* (also 1967, with Berenson) sit in that burst year. Three Carkhuff-related books in 1967 is not a coincidence. It is the moment a grant-era rating culture tried to become a literature. The Aldine book with Truax is the one this pack keeps citing because it is where the rating claim met a wide audience.

*Helping and Human Relations: A Primer for Lay and Professional Helpers* (Holt, Rinehart and Winston, 1969) came in two volumes: *Selection and Training* and *Practice and Research*. The subtitle's word *lay* is a historical tell. Carkhuff was already arguing that the conditions of helping were not the property of a licensed caste. Teachers, nurses, parents, community workers — if the dimensions could be rated, they could be taught. That democratic claim is why his work left counseling psychology for nursing, education, and, later, human-resource shops. It is also why a five-point sheet can turn into a workplace evaluation. The 1969 primer did not have to intend the corporate descendant for the descendant to arrive.

Garfield's 1978 citation studies and later "eminent psychologist" lists put Carkhuff's 1960s volumes among the most-cited clinical books of their moment. Those lists are bibliometric history. They are not a reason to turn a trainer into a monument. This page will not.

## Level 3 as a classroom proverb

Carkhuff's five-point revision of the empathy ladder, taught from the 1969 volumes and from *The Art of Helping* (HRD Press, 1971 in some records, 1972 in others; later editions through a ninth in 2009 per the author's site), made "level 3" a proverb: an interchangeable response, the floor of helpfulness, neither subtracting from what the person said nor adding much. Levels above were taught as additive, personalized, or action-oriented depending on the edition's language. This paragraph is as far as the description goes. The stems, the rating anchors, and the student workbook pages are not reproduced here. They are training objects. A reader who wants training wants a program, not a history page.

The accurate-empathy essay in this pack already told the rater's story. Carkhuff's figure essay is about what happens when a scale becomes a *pedagogy*. You can put fifty students in a room, play a tape, and demand a number. The number makes a supervisor feel honest. It can also teach a house dialect of reflection and call the dialect "human relations." Feminist and multicultural writers later pointed out that "accurate" often meant accurate to a particular feeling vocabulary. Disability communities pointed out that imagining "what it would be like" is still the helper's fantasy. Those critiques attach to the pedagogy as much as to Rogers. Carkhuff's later brand, from Amherst, Massachusetts companies under his name, moved into "human generativity" and information-age titles far from the 1967 Aldine list. The move is public. It is not this pack's job to review the later business books. The 1967 and 1969 objects are.

## Lay helpers, and the cost of a transferable scale

If helping dimensions are operational, they can be taught to people who will never sit for a counseling license. That was, in the 1960s, a radical administrative idea and a humane one. It also lowered the wall between a clinical hour and a training module. Ivey's microskills made a similar bet with videotape. Egan staged the hour. Hill researched it. Carkhuff graded it. The four names are this pack's teaching wave for a reason. They are how American helping became a course you could fail.

The cost is on the same page. A five-point sheet does not hear a parent of a newly diagnosed deaf child rearranging a life. It does not hear an SLP delivering a swallow result. Luterman's 1979 book would insist that some days the professional's job is not to offer a more additive response. The measurement tradition and the CSD counseling tradition grew up together and did not always share a hallway. Carkhuff's export of the scale into "all helping professions" made that hallway collision more frequent. Nurses scored. Teachers scored. The word *empathy* got a number. The number travelled farther than the 1957 "as if."
