**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1983 a paper appeared in *Behavioural Psychotherapy* with a title that did not yet sound like a brand: "Motivational Interviewing with Problem Drinkers." William R. Miller, then on the faculty at the University of New Mexico, argued that motivation was not a personality trait you had or lacked. It was an interpersonal process. The paper drew on experimental social psychology — attribution, cognitive dissonance, self-efficacy — and on empathic methods associated with Carl Rogers. It de-emphasized labeling. It was a research article, not a workshop flyer.

He is living at last verification (2026-09-14). His public curriculum vitae lists a 1947 birth year (Shamokin, Pennsylvania). This page uses public books, public papers, and that CV as a professional document. It does not discuss family, health, or private life. It does not teach OARS as homework. The OARS skill essay and the motivational-interviewing modality essay in this pack already carry those objects. This one is the psychologist who named the interview.

The 1983 paper's abstract is still the cleanest door. Motivation is conceptualized as interpersonal. Labeling is de-emphasized. Empathic process, social-psychological principles, and objective assessment feedback are named as the channels that might turn dissonance toward change rather than toward the "short circuits" of low self-esteem, low self-efficacy, and denial — Miller's words in that abstract, not a session script. A schematic diagram and a six-step sequence were *suggested* in the article. Later trainers lifted the sequence. This series will not. The historical point is that 1983 already looked like a method a journal would print, not like a charismatic school.

## Oregon, then Albuquerque

Miller took a B.A. at Lycoming College (1969), graduate study at Wisconsin, then an M.A. (1973) and Ph.D. in clinical psychology (1976) at the University of Oregon. He joined the University of New Mexico faculty in 1976 and stayed. Public later titles include distinguished professor of psychology and psychiatry, now emeritus. UNM is the institutional home of the 1983 paper and of a later coding and training culture that would travel far beyond Albuquerque.

Before the 1983 name, he had already published on problem drinking, including controlled-drinking and bibliotherapy studies that belong to the alcohol-treatment arguments of those years. Those arguments were fierce. This page will not restage them as a scoreboard. The 1983 contribution was a *process* claim: how a helper talks can raise or flatten the other person's own reasons for change. Confrontation-as-attack, in that paper's logic, was not toughness. It was a way to produce the very "denial" the field liked to diagnose.

## 1991, with Rollnick, and the editions that followed

Stephen Rollnick, working in the UK with people who had medical reasons to change and little interest in being lectured, became the co-author who turned a paper into a book. *Motivational Interviewing: Preparing People to Change Addictive Behavior* (Guilford, 1991) is the first edition. Later Guilford editions retitled as they went: *Preparing People for Change* (2nd, 2002); *Helping People Change* (3rd, 2013); a fourth edition in 2023, *Helping People Change and Grow*, is the object this bibliography names for the current desk. Translations of the first book began in the 1990s. A method that had started with drinkers was, by then, being asked to travel into health care, criminal justice, and any office where a professional wanted someone to do something the professional already believed was wise.

That travel is the historical problem. Miller and Rollnick kept publishing cautions about "MI lite" — the brand without the interpersonal claim. Theresa B. Moyers and colleagues built Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) coding manuals so that a tape could be rated. MITI is a historical coding object in this pack. It is not a homework sheet. Project MATCH, in the early 1990s, included Motivational Enhancement Therapy as a manualized cousin (Miller, Zweben, DiClemente, and Rychtarik, 1992, NIAAA monograph). MATCH is an alcohol-research event. It is not a proof that "MI will help your drinking." Efficacy sentences in this series stay dated and sourced. They never become a consumer promise.

*Motivational Interviewing in Health Care* (Rollnick, Miller, and Butler, 2008) made the medical office an explicit audience. Allied health, including speech-language pathology, met the method as a way to talk about adherence, homework, and the person who does not want the device you are holding. The meeting can be useful. It can also flatten MI into a trick for getting compliance. Miller's 1983 paper was about an interpersonal process, not a trick. The difference is the whole teaching problem.

## What he was not selling

He was not selling a personality. The 1983 argument ran the other way: stop treating motivation as a trait inside the drinker. He was not selling Rogers under a new brand, though empathic process is in the 1983 abstract as a named inheritance. He was not selling a self-help worksheet. Later popularizations that turn OARS into a fridge magnet have a history of their own. They are not this page's job to reprint.

UNM's Center on Alcoholism, Substance Abuse, and Addictions (CASAA) became the institutional shed for much of this work: trials, training tapes, visiting researchers. Miller's public CV lists films and tape series from the 1990s, including a six-tape motivational-interviewing series with Rollnick and Moyers (1998). Those tapes are teaching objects with a date. They are not embedded here.

He wrote, across decades, on addiction, spirituality as a research topic, and the training of helpers. The public CV is long. This series does not owe a complete bibliography. It owes a clean line from 1983 to 1991 to the later editions and to the coding culture, without gossip. Later editions made "ambivalence" a keyword the rest of health care borrowed. The keyword is a historical claim about talk. It is not a fridge magnet on this site.

## Why a speech clinic keeps the 1983 citation

WOW Therapies is a Southeast Arkansas speech-language practice. People arrive with reasons to change a swallow, a sound, a device, a school plan — and with reasons not to. The professional temptation is to push. Miller's published claim, in 1983, was that the push can manufacture the resistance it then complains about. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names counseling about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not name UNM. The inheritance is a warning about how talk works, not a license to "do MI" as if an SLP were an addiction counselor.

This URL will not diagnose anyone's drinking. It will not print a miracle question or an OARS drill. It will keep the paper, the 1991 Guilford book, the later editions, and the living author inside the bounds of public work. The modality essay in this pack can carry the package. The figure essay keeps the dated name on the 1983 contents page.
