**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1969 Holt, Rinehart and Winston issued Robert R. Carkhuff's *Helping and Human Relations* in two volumes. Beside a shortened five-point version of Truax's empathy ladder, the books added scales for a more active helper: concreteness, genuineness and self-disclosure, confrontation, immediacy. Counselor education, which had spent a decade teaching students to track feelings without adding much, suddenly had official permission to *push*. The push was still a number. A rater could, in principle, hear whether a confrontation was interchangeable with the client's material or an attack from another planet. This pack will not reprint those anchors as a worksheet, and it will not supply lines a reader could try on a relative. It will say how three dangerous family names — confrontation, immediacy, disclosure — became teachable objects, and who argued they were something else.

## After warmth, a second toolkit

Carkhuff had come through the Wisconsin weather with Truax. *Toward Effective Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1967) still centered the facilitative conditions. The 1969 volumes behave like a profession that feared its students had learned only to be kind. Kindness without concreteness, in that argument, left people in a warm fog. Confrontation, on Carkhuff's sheet, was not a street fight. It was a rated attempt to point at a discrepancy — between what a person said and did, between statements, between self-picture and performance — without dropping the facilitative floor. The pedagogy is mid-century American: if a human act can be botched, give it a level.

The word still carried the older American addiction-counseling meaning. To confront, in that guild, was often to break denial, sometimes loudly. Miller's 1983 drinkers paper, treated in this pack as a questioning and later as an OARS history, is in part a dated refusal of that loudness. So the 1970s–1980s student could meet "confrontation" on a Carkhuff exam as a high-level helping skill and meet it in an alcoholism lecture as a moral assault. The homonym is not a coincidence. Both uses claim that the helper sees something the speaker will not. They disagree about whether the seeing should arrive as an invitation or as a verdict.

Allen E. Ivey's microcounseling, from the 1968 APGA paper through *Microcounseling* (Charles C. Thomas, 1971) and then through later *Intentional Interviewing* editions with Mary Bradford Ivey, put "confrontation" on a hierarchy of influencing skills — often later called empathic confrontation — after the basic listening sequence. The placement is an argument in furniture. You do not get the sharp tool until you can attend. Beginners still reached for it first. Every skills teacher in the public literature says so. The hierarchy was built to slow that reach. Slowing is not the same as stopping. Ivey's later editions, written for a multicultural audience, had to warn that pointing at a discrepancy is a culturally loaded act. A discrepancy that looks like insight to a White intern can look like disrespect in another house. The warning is late relative to the 1971 book. It is still part of the skill's history.

## Immediacy: the hour talks about itself

Immediacy is a stranger word. In Carkhuff's cluster it names talk about the helping relationship as it is happening — the here-and-now of two people, not the there-and-then of a childhood. Donald J. Kiesler's interpersonal work gave that move a different theoretical engine. Kiesler's 1979 paper in *Psychiatry*, "An Interpersonal Communication Analysis of Relationship in Psychotherapy," and his 1988 book *Therapeutic Metacommunication: Therapist Impact Disclosure as Feedback in Psychotherapy*, treated the helper as someone who gets "hooked" into a client's usual interpersonal cycle and must then say something about the hook. The vocabulary is Sullivan, Leary, complementarity: control and affiliation, the pull to answer dominance with submission. Metacommunication, in Kiesler's public definition, is verbal feedback about the recurrent relationship issues occurring between the two people in the sessions.

That is not Carkhuff's five-point sheet. It is not Ivey's hierarchy. It is an interpersonal-theory claim that the relationship *is* the data, and that withholding the data is a way of repeating the client's ordinary fate. Psychoanalytic readers heard countertransference confession and either reached for a prohibition or, in later decades, for a more flexible use of the analyst's inner experience. Kiesler, writing from Virginia Commonwealth University and the interpersonal-circle research program (the 1982 *Psychological Review* paper on complementarity), was trying to make the confession empirical: impact messages, inventories, a circle. Living or dead, the public record is the books and the instruments, not a private life.

Clara E. Hill's *Helping Skills* (APA, 1999 and later editions) sorted the sharp tools again. Challenges belong to an insight stage: they point at contradictions, defenses, discrepancies. Immediacy is given its own chapter-logic as a way of processing the therapeutic relationship — inquiring about reactions to the work, drawing parallels, speaking about closeness or distance in the room. Hill's research program at the University of Maryland treated these as countable events with consequences that could be studied, not as mystic turning points. The taxonomy is another teaching machine. It is also a historical truce. Humanistic warmth, interpersonal hook, psychodynamic here-and-now, and skills-training all get a row in the table. The row is not a proof that they are the same act.

## Disclosure and the ethics of being known

Sidney Jourard's *The Transparent Self* (Van Nostrand, 1964) made self-disclosure a research topic and a humanistic virtue. Healthy people, in Jourard's public argument, let themselves be known. Helpers who remained opaque were not only Freudian; they were, on this view, modeling concealment. Carkhuff put self-disclosure on a scale next to genuineness, as if the amount and quality of the helper's revelations could be rated like empathy. The combination is unstable. Jourard's transparency is a moral anthropology. Carkhuff's disclosure is a training dimension. A student who read both could think that telling a client about a divorce was a Level 4.

Professional ethics codes had to do the duller work. The American Personnel and Guidance Association, later the American Counseling Association, wrote and rewrote codes (the ACA *Code of Ethics* of 2014 is a dated object among others) that treat counselor self-disclosure as something that must serve the client, not the counselor's loneliness, and that sit near rules about boundaries, dual relationships, and harm. This series will not reprint a decision tree. The historical point is that disclosure became an *ethical* object because the humanistic decades had made it a *skill* object. Once you can teach it, you can misuse it on purpose.

Hill's public research on helper self-disclosure — distinguished, in her textbooks, among disclosures of facts, feelings, insight, and strategies — tried to pull the act back into empirical humility. Some disclosures help. Some steal the hour. Immediate disclosures about the relationship overlap with Kiesler's impact talk. Historical disclosures ("I once…") overlap with Jourard and with every intern who wants to be liked. The profession never found a ratio. It found a literature of caution.

Feminist therapists had already named a related danger: the helper's confession can be a bid for care. The client becomes the audience. Multicultural writers named another: a White helper's disclosure of "not seeing color," or of a parallel pain that is not parallel, can be a second injury. None of those critiques is a ban on ever being known. They are a record of injuries that the 1969 scale could not hear.

## Addiction rooms, medical rooms, the wrong sharp tool

Confrontation's worst public history in the United States is not Carkhuff's classroom. It is the Synanon-descended, attack-therapy, and some chemical-dependency cultures in which breaking a person down was billed as honesty. Naming that harm is in bounds. Teaching the method is not. Motivational interviewing's later coding manuals (MITI) treated confrontational responses as things to count *down*, a historical reversal of the addiction field's older pride. The reversal is a dated event in a modality essay; it belongs here only as proof that "confrontation" is not one skill. It is a family of acts, some of which this series will not describe in usable detail.

Immediacy has a quieter failure mode. A helper who constantly narrates the relationship can make the client responsible for the helper's feelings. A remark about distance in the room may be data in Kiesler's sense. It may also be a demand. Carkhuff's levels were supposed to catch the demand as a low-level, self-serving move. Catching it requires a rater who is not impressed by psychological language. Many supervisors were impressed. The tape, again, is only as honest as the ear.

Allied-health rooms inherit a thinner version. An SLP who comments on a repeated halt before a difficult sound is doing a kind of immediacy about a motor task, not a Carkhuff confrontation about a personality. Collapsing those acts trains nobody well. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice allows counseling about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not license attack, and it does not ask clinicians to process transference. The useful historical warning is smaller. Once helping taught sharp tools as skills, every guild had to decide which sharpness belonged to its license. Speech clinics that borrowed "confrontation" from a counseling textbook without borrowing the listening floor were repeating the beginner error Ivey's hierarchy was built to prevent.

## What will not be printed

No stems. No "try saying this when you notice a discrepancy." No empty-chair instructions, no OARS drills, no disclosure scripts. The dated objects are enough: Carkhuff 1969 putting confrontation, immediacy, and disclosure on training scales; Ivey 1971 and after placing confronting after attending; Kiesler 1979 and 1988 making impact talk an interpersonal method; Hill 1999 sorting challenges and immediacy into a stage model; Jourard 1964 making transparency a virtue that ethics codes then had to bridle; Miller 1983 as a public turning away from pugilistic confrontation in drinking treatment.

The names remain in syllabi because a profession that can only soothe is as untrustworthy as a profession that can only attack. The history is the oscillation, not a recipe for the midpoint. Living authors on this list get the public books, not a story about their character. Character, ironically, is what genuineness scales and disclosure ethics were both, in different decades, trying to professionalize. They did not finish.
