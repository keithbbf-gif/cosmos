**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

On 2 June 1920 President Woodrow Wilson signed the Smith-Fess Act — also called the Civilian Vocational Rehabilitation Act, Public Law 236 — and a civilian counseling job was written into federal statute before most states had a counseling license. The program matched federal and state money, fifty-fifty. Congress put $750,000 on the first year. The eligible population, at that point, was people with physical disabilities. The services named in the early paper trail were vocational guidance, training, occupational adjustment, and placement. A profession later called rehabilitation counseling grew inside that list.

This essay is a skill-package history, not an inspiration story. It will not narrate anyone "overcoming" a body for the reader's comfort. It will follow a federal-state program, a credential, and a counseling claim: that vocational work and personal adjustment could be one job.

## Before civilians, soldiers and a board

The Smith-Hughes Act of 1917 had already created a Federal Board for Vocational Education and put matching money into vocational training. That is why, later, so many rehabilitation-counseling programs sat in colleges of education rather than in medical schools. The 1918 Soldier's Rehabilitation Act (Smith-Sears) then asked the same board to serve veterans disabled in the First World War. Smith-Fess, two years later, copied the veteran machinery for civilians. The copy was not permanent at first. Congress had to reauthorize. The Social Security Act of 1935 made the federal vocational-rehabilitation program permanent, which is a dry sentence with a large consequence: the job would outlast any one war.

Early VR was not "counseling" as a Rogers tape would later mean it. It was a case, a plan, a placement. The person in the file might be fitted for a prosthesis, sent to a trade, or told there was no suitable work. Occupational adjustment was a phrase that could mean a conversation or a refusal. Historians who tidy this into a humanistic origin story are reading the 1960s backward into 1920.

## Amendments that changed who counted

The Vocational Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1943 — the Barden-La Follette Act — added physical restoration to the federal goals. A program that had been mostly training and placement could now pay for certain medical services that made work conceivable. Later amendments widened the census again. The Rehabilitation Act of 1973 replaced the older vocational-rehabilitation statute, put new emphasis on people with the most severe disabilities, and wrote an individualized planning process into the grant machinery.

Section 504 of that 1973 Act is the sentence most civilians eventually heard, even if they never entered a VR office: no otherwise qualified person with a disability was to be excluded, solely by reason of disability, from a program receiving federal financial assistance. The implementing regulations were not a gift. Disability activists occupied federal buildings in 1977, most famously in San Francisco, to force the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare to issue them. Rehabilitation counseling did not invent that politics. It worked, afterward, in a country where 504 existed as law and as a memory of a sit-in.

The 1978 amendments funded a separate independent-living program. Independent living said that a life was not only a job. Vocational rehabilitation had been built to return people to employment. Independent living insisted on housing, attendants, and the right to refuse a professional's idea of a suitable plan. The two programs have shared buildings, budgets, and arguments ever since.

The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 — signed 26 July — took a 504-shaped nondiscrimination logic into private employment, public services, and public accommodations. It is context for this essay, not a counseling technique. A counselor who spent the 1980s filling out an Individualized Written Rehabilitation Program spent the 1990s in a labor market that had new legal language for the same staircase. Later Workforce Investment Act amendments (1998) and the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (2014) renamed and re-housed pieces of the system. The skill question survived the letterhead: what, exactly, was the counselor doing besides a file?

## Vocational counseling and adjustment counseling

Rehabilitation counseling's distinctive claim, in the textbooks, was a double task. Vocational counseling meant assessment, labor-market information, training, and placement — cousins of Parsons, Super, and the career essay in this pack. Adjustment counseling meant the rest of the life that a job does not automatically settle: a new impairment, a family rearranged, a grief that is not a "stage" on a poster, a discrimination that is not a feeling.

Stanford E. Rubin and Richard T. Roessler's textbooks became the desk objects for that claim. They are professional-history books, not worksheets. They taught generations of students that a rehabilitation counselor was not a stripped-down social worker and not a private therapist who happened to know the Dictionary of Occupational Titles. The job sat at a desk with a caseload, a state-federal manual, and a person whose goals might not match the agency's closure categories.

The skills this series has already named — listening, questions, silence, confrontation as a dated technical claim, cultural humility — all entered that desk. So did job-placement skills that clinical programs often treated as someone else's problem. A history that only keeps the "counseling" half and drops the vocational half has already chosen a side in an old fight. The CRC exam, built from role-and-function studies, kept both halves as a knowledge domain. That is a measurement history as much as a moral one.

## A credential with a date

The American Rehabilitation Counseling Association and the National Rehabilitation Counseling Association formed a joint committee on certification. In January 1974 that committee was chartered as a nonprofit and renamed the Commission on Rehabilitation Counselor Certification (CRCC). Field-review examinations ran in 1974–1975. CRCC later described the CRC as the first counseling certification of its kind in the United States; the boast is theirs, and it belongs in quotation marks as a professional claim. Thousands of counselors were certified in the early "grandpersoning" window. The CRC program later sought NCCA accreditation (CRCC dates that to 1980). The exam is rebuilt from periodic role-and-function studies. Those studies are historical objects: they say what the job *was* when the survey went out, not what a reader should do this afternoon.

Accreditation of graduate programs ran, for decades, through the Council on Rehabilitation Education (CORE), which had been writing empirical standards for this specialty before CACREP existed for the rest of counseling. On 1 July 2017 a merger put rehabilitation-counseling programs under CACREP. The merger is still argued in the *Journal of Applied Rehabilitation Counseling* and elsewhere: some programs chose a "clinical rehabilitation" track; some closed; exam scores and identity papers followed. A skills history does not need to referee that fight. It needs to say that the credential and the accreditor are part of how the skill package became inspectable — and that inspectability is not the same as a how-to.

## Against the poster

Disability communities have had to watch a certain kind of professional story for a century: the client who, with enough counselor grit, "overcomes" and gets a job, preferably one photogenic enough for an annual report. That story is a genre. It is not evidence. It flattens discrimination into attitude. It makes the counselor the hero of someone else's morning. Rehabilitation counseling's better paper trail is duller: statute, eligibility, a plan, a disagreement about closure, a 504 complaint, a labor market that may not want the person the plan describes.

Section 504 and the ADA are in this essay because they changed the *room*, not because they are counseling skills. A counselor who ignores that legal weather is doing vocational folklore. A counselor who treats the laws as a substitute for a conversation is doing paperwork folklore. The skill lineage lives in the gap, which is not a comfortable sentence and is the one worth keeping.

## What this is not, and where it sits beside speech

Rehabilitation counseling is a licensed or certified profession with its own exams and, in many states, its own license title. An SLP who uses counseling *skills* about communication is not a CRC. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice does not mint that credential. The two jobs do share systems: VR funding for devices and training, 504 plans in schools, the aftermath of a stroke or a progressive disease, the family that has already been told to be inspiring.

WOW Therapies is a Southeast Arkansas speech-language practice. Families here meet vocational rehabilitation when a device, a job, or a training program is on the table. They meet school 504 teams. They do not need this page to sell them a miracle of attitude. They need a history that admits the American rehabilitation counselor was legislated into existence in 1920, taught to do vocational *and* adjustment work, credentialed in 1974, and then asked to practice in a country that had 504 and, later, an ADA — without turning any of that into a poster.
