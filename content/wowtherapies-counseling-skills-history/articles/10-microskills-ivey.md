**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1967, at Colorado State University, Allen E. Ivey's group pointed a two-inch videotape camera at beginning counselors. The decks were heavy. The method was a dare: isolate a single interview behavior, show a model, let the trainee try, play the tape back, try again. Ivey later said the model was born that year. The public paper trail is 1968. In April, the American Personnel and Guidance Association meeting heard "Micro-Counseling and Attending Behavior: An Approach to Pre-Practicum Counselor Training," now ERIC ED021275. In September, the *Journal of Counseling Psychology* printed a monograph supplement (volume 15, number 5, part 2) by Ivey, Cheryl J. Normington, C. Dean Miller, Weston H. Morrill, and Richard F. Haase. *Microcounseling: Innovations in Interviewing Training* (Charles C. Thomas, Springfield, Illinois) bound the project as a 1971 book. History can live with 1967, 1968, and 1971 if it does not mash them into one mythic morning.

This essay is not a life of Ivey; that figure piece sits later in this pack. It is the history of a *teaching technology*: skills cut small enough to film, then accused of being so small they were no longer counseling. Ivey is a living writer at last verification (2026-09-14). The sources here are public papers, public textbooks, and public critiques.

## Why a morning's unit

Counselor education before the camera had a dignity problem and a competence problem. Students could talk about Rogers and still freeze, or fill, or advice-give, once a real person sat down. Truax and Carkhuff had already answered with ratings and with hours of tape to recognize warmth and empathy. Ivey's group answered with something closer to medical microteaching, which was in the air in the 1960s: a short cycle, a single skill, immediate playback. The first research focused on attending behavior, reflection of feeling, and summarization of feeling. Attending was the hub: look, face, follow the talk, sound as if you are with the speaker. The 1968 ERIC abstract is blunt. Attending and its related concepts may be described in behavioral terms meaningful to beginning counselors.

"Meaningful to beginning counselors" is the political center of the project. The units had to be sayable in a language that did not require a personal analysis or a year of theory. Eye contact, body orientation, verbal following: a supervisor could point at a minute of tape. The beginner could change one thing. Medical students, teachers, librarians, and agricultural extension agents appear in later Ivey advertising as audiences. The migration is part of the skill's history. It is also how a clinic word became a workshop industry. The camera did not choose the later room.

Norman Kagan's Interpersonal Process Recall, circulating from a 1963 paper with David Krathwohl and William Miller, used video for a different confession. Stop the tape, ask what was thought and felt. IPR taught inner weather. Microcounseling taught units. Histories that mention only Ivey miss a parallel born of the same machines. Both assumed a trainee could bear to watch. Not every trainee could. Refusing the playback is also a datum. It rarely made the methods chapters.

## The book, the hierarchy, the second author

The 1971 Thomas book made microcounseling portable without the Colorado lab. Later came *Microcounseling* with Jerry Authier (1978) and then the long textbook life of *Intentional Interviewing and Counseling*, the title that carried the hierarchy into the century of Cengage. Mary Bradford Ivey, a school counselor by training and Allen Ivey's collaborator in the public textbooks, is the second name a fair history has to keep. Carlos P. Zalaquett joined later editions. The subtitle *Facilitating Client Development in a Multicultural Society* is itself a dated revision. The first papers were less careful about culture. The later books put ethics, multicultural competence, and wellness at the base of a pyramid, then attending, then the basic listening sequence, then focusing and empathic confrontation, then influencing skills, then integration.

The pyramid is a curriculum, not a natural history of conversation. Students still meet it as if it were the order of the soul: first you attend, then you reflect, then you confront. Clients do not live the pyramid. The pedagogical claim was smaller and more defensible. Complexity should be reduced until a beginner can succeed at one act. Ivey and colleagues defined microskills, in later textbook language, as communication skill units of the interview that help the student interact more intentionally. Intention is the humanistic residue. Units are the behavioral residue. The hybrid is the product.

Translations followed the textbooks. Japanese, Spanish, Chinese, Korean, and other editions of the interviewing volumes circulated as counselor education globalized; exact first-year dates belong on the copyright page of the copy on the desk, not in a guessed sentence. ## Attending is not a universal face

Eye contact as "good attending" is a North American classroom rule. The 1968 studies treated looking as a positive, countable sign. Later Ivey editions, under multicultural pressure and in conversation with Derald Wing Sue's competencies work (1992 and the *Counseling the Culturally Diverse* textbooks), tried to write eye contact down as a *choice* — vary it, watch the other person's culture and comfort, do not make a stare into a virtue. The correction is real. It is also an admission that the first operationalization had smuggled a White, middle-class, professional face into the definition of listening.

Verbal following has its own parochialism. Some speakers tell a story in circles, with the point last. Some use a pause that is not a request for a prompt. Some families expect the professional to receive the eldest's speech without chasing every noun. A student trained to "track" like a camera may experience those rooms as resistance. The 1968 paper cannot be blamed for every later misuse. It can be blamed, gently, for making a local attending style look like an atom of help.

Disability and CSD rooms break other atoms. A child who looks away while assembling a motor plan is not failing to attach. A person with autism who gives little eye contact may be regulating, not fleeing. A speaker with aphasia may need a longer unfilled second than Ivey's anxious beginner was taught to allow. The microskills literature eventually grew footnotes. Speech-language pathology grew its own counseling texts — Luterman 1979, Holland and Nelson 2007 — that never agreed to treat attending as a stare. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names counseling about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not name the 1968 APGA paper. The inheritance is the temptation to grade a session as if it were a prepracticum tape.

## Skills as atoms: the 2011 quarrel

By 2011 *The Counseling Psychologist* could devote space to a reexamination. Charles R. Ridley, Shannon Kelly, and Debra Mollen's "Microskills Training: Evolution, Reexamination, and Call for Reform" (volume 39) is a dated public critique from inside counselor education, not from a rival guild. The charge, in outline, is that cutting counseling into teachable units produced competence at units and a weaker account of counseling as a judgment, a culture, and a relationship. Other papers in that issue offered alternative competence models. Invited reactions noted that microskills had been useful and well researched *and* that the critique had landed. This pack will not referee a 2011 special issue. It will say the atomization problem was visible from the start. Ivey's own later editions kept adding context — neuroscience chapters, trauma, telehealth, social justice — around the same hierarchy, which is one way a model admits that atoms are not the organism.

Gerard Egan's *The Skilled Helper* (Brooks/Cole, 1975, then a long Cengage life) and Clara Hill's *Helping Skills* (APA, 1999—) are neighboring teaching machines. Egan organized stages and skills into a problem-management model. Hill organized exploration, insight, and action. Both assumed, with Ivey, that a mouth can be taught in pieces. Both have been accused, with Ivey, of producing students who can name a skill and still not know what the hour is for. The accusation is the profession talking to its own success. Before 1968, the complaint was that students could not do anything. After 1968, the complaint was that they could only do small things. That reversal is a historical achievement even when the complaint is fair.

The microskills story is a case of a familiar tension in professional teaching. The 1968 paper subtracted mystery and added a count. Later textbooks added context back in as chapters, without subtracting the count. The hierarchy grew. Beginners still need a first successful tape. The ethical problem is claiming that the first successful tape is counseling.

## What the camera could not finish

Microskills are not all of counseling. Ivey's later editions said so with increasing emphasis, which is why the opposite slogan — that the units *are* the work — is historically false. Attending is not universal eye contact. Confrontation, in the later "empathic" dressing, still sits after listening in the furniture of the pyramid. Translations carried a Colorado idea into rooms that had never seen Fort Collins. Mary Bradford Ivey's name on the textbooks is a reminder that school counseling, not only university laboratories, fed the teaching model.

A rural speech clinic does not need a microskills drill on a public page. It may still be living with the 1968 inheritance every time a supervisor says "watch your attending" to a graduate student who is, in fact, watching a child search for a sound. The camera was a genuine answer to a genuine training failure. The failure it created — mistaking a unit for a practice, a face for a culture, a morning's skill for an hour's judgment — is the rest of the skill's life. The dates stay stubborn: 1967 in the lab, 1968 on the APGA and journal record, 1971 between Thomas's boards. Keep the three dates. They are how a technique remains a history instead of a brand.
