**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1920 John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner published "Conditioned emotional reactions" in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology*. The subject was an infant they called Albert B. A white rat. A steel bar struck behind the head. The paper reported that fear could be attached to a previously neutral animal, then spread to fur and a Santa Claus mask. The infant left the Johns Hopkins laboratory without a documented unconditioning. That is the first dated fact this essay will hold. It is an ethics history, not a method to copy.

This pack does not reprint Watson's life. It asks how a laboratory claim about *behavior* became a counseling skill package: something you could name, teach, measure on a ward, and later regret.

## A lab that should have stopped the profession

Albert could not consent. The wet-nurse arrangement, the graduate-student coauthor, the faculty power in the room — those are not colorful details. They are the conditions of the experiment. Later historians have argued over the child's later identity. One trail follows Douglas Merritte, who died in 1925. Another follows William Albert Barger. The disagreement is part of the record. A closed identification is not required to say the obvious: an infant was used to demonstrate a fear, and the paper still sits in introductory textbooks as if the demonstration were a gift.

Introductory textbooks have a habit. They show the stills. This series will not. The 1920 paper is a document. It is not a picture of a child to recycle as a header.

American Psychological Association ethics codes of later decades made a replication of Albert's hour unthinkable as a method. That is the only useful inheritance of the study for a counseling-skills history: a dated reminder that a countable response can be produced by a procedure no contemporary clinic would defend. Behavior as a skill package begins, honestly, with that refusal.

## Jones, Peter, and the other 1924

Mary Cover Jones published "A Laboratory Study of Fear: The Case of Peter" in *Pedagogical Seminary* 31 (1924): 308–315. Peter was two years and ten months old. He feared a white rat; the fear ran to a rabbit, a fur coat, a feather, cotton wool. Jones chose the rabbit for "unconditioning." Other children who were not afraid of the animal were present in a play period. Food Peter liked was later paired with the rabbit in its cage. Jones wrote that the work continued where Watson had left off.

Later trainers called Peter the first behavior-therapy case. The compliment is a century's marketing. Jones was running a laboratory study of fear removal in a research nursery, not a counseling hour. She listed progressive "degrees of toleration" as a tolerance series — a historical object, not a worksheet. This series will not reprint the series as a how-to. It will say that 1924 already contained the two moves later schools would quarrel over: a graded approach to the feared thing, and the presence of other people who were not afraid. Modeling and pairing. Both would return under other names.

Jones was a woman in a laboratory culture that remembered Watson's name more readily than hers. That, too, is skill history. A method that claims to be about observable acts still chooses which observers get the textbook chapter.

## Johannesburg, cats, and a 1958 book

Joseph Wolpe's *Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition* (Stanford University Press, 1958) is the book American counselors later meant when they said "systematic desensitization." Wolpe, a South African psychiatrist, had worked with experimentally induced fear in cats and then with anxious humans. Reciprocal inhibition was the claim: a response incompatible with anxiety — often relaxation — could occupy the same moment as a feared cue and weaken the fear. Graded lists of situations appeared in the book as clinic documents.

This essay will not print an exposure hierarchy. It will not tell a reader how to build one, order one, or sit with one. The 1958 lists are dated objects. They belong to a psychiatrist's mid-century argument with psychoanalysis, which Wolpe thought had spent too many hours on origins and too few on the present cue. The fight was public. So was the later fight among behavior therapists over whether gradual pairing was kinder, faster, or neither, compared with more intensive exposure methods that other laboratories named in the 1960s and 1970s. Those later names are neighboring history. They are not a protocol on a speech-clinic website.

Arnold Lazarus, who had been with Wolpe in South Africa, claimed in a 1958 *South African Medical Journal* paper to have used the phrase "behavior therapy." Hans Eysenck in London used the same family of words and, in 1952, had published "The effects of psychotherapy: an evaluation" in the *Journal of Consulting Psychology*, arguing that the talking cures of the day had not beaten spontaneous remission. Eysenck's 1952 paper is not a final verdict. It is the quarrel that gave behavior therapy a public excuse to arrive as a rival craft. The naming fight — whose "behavior therapy," whose journal, whose cats — is the origin story counselors actually inherited. *Behaviour Research and Therapy* began in 1963 with Eysenck as editor. American counseling programs later put Wolpe on a week of the syllabus and left Johannesburg off the map.

## Skinner's 1953 vocabulary, borrowed

B. F. Skinner's *Science and Human Behavior* (Macmillan, 1953) is not a counseling manual. It is a behaviorist's account of how operant language — reinforcement, extinction, shaping, stimulus control — could describe human action without inner ghosts. Counseling borrowed the vocabulary anyway. "Homework" as a scheduled practice. "Reinforcement" as a word for what a parent does after a sound. "Extinction" as a word some classrooms used too casually for a child's bid for attention.

The inheritance is mixed. An SLP room already had practice schedules before it read Skinner. The 1953 book made the schedule sound like a science. That is a historical event in the mouth of the helper, not a proof that the helper had become an experimental analyst of behavior. Counselor-education programs in the 1960s and 1970s sometimes taught "behavioral counseling" as a unit: specify the target, count it, arrange consequences. John Krumboltz and others wrote that language into guidance. The unit could be humane. It could also shrink a life to a chart.

*Verbal Behavior* (1957) sits next door in the SLPWOW pack's scientific weather. This essay will not annex it. It will say only that counseling's behavioral skill package and speech science's learning theories grew up in overlapping decades and did not always admit the overlap.

## Tokens on a ward: Anna, Illinois

Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan H. Azrin, working at Anna State Hospital in southern Illinois, reported in the *Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior* (1965) that tokens — conditioned reinforcers — could be exchanged later for events patients already preferred, and that ward behaviors defined as adaptive rose and fell with the procedure. *The Token Economy: A Motivational System for Therapy and Rehabilitation* (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1968) made the hospital program a book. Attendants could run it if the criteria were objective enough.

That last clause is the institutional claim and the institutional danger. Who chose the "adaptive" behavior? Who held the tokens? What counted as a reinforcer when the alternative was a bare ward? Disability-rights and institutional-reform arguments of the 1970s — including the long legal fight over conditions in American public hospitals — did not need a behaviorist's permission to say that a system which uses food, cigarettes, or a walk outdoors as wages is a system of control. Token economies migrated into classrooms, group homes, and some prisons. Classroom versions are not the same ethical object as a locked ward. Historians who flatten them into one "behavioral skill" are doing the flattening.

This series describes token economies as institutional history. It will not tell a teacher or a parent how to run one. A speech clinic in Southeast Arkansas that uses stickers after a session is not Anna State Hospital. It is also not innocent of the history. Once a profession learns to pay for a response, it has to say who is paying, with what, and whether the person being paid can leave.

## What counseling kept, and what it dropped

By the late 1960s a master's student could be taught to write a target in observable verbs, to take a baseline, and to call the result counseling. The Rogerian reel of this pack's first essay was a rival teaching machine: count the helper's empathy. The behavioral sheet counted the client's acts. Two measurement cultures, same decade, two answers to "what would we have to see to say this is happening?"

Albert Bandura's modeling work — the 1961 Bobo-doll experiments are laboratory history, not a counseling hour — added a third answer: people change by watching. *Principles of Behavior Modification* (1969) carried modeling into a clinical vocabulary. Counselors who later said they used "behavioral skills" often meant a stew: Wolpe's graded lists, Skinner's consequences, Bandura's models, and a chart from a course titled Behavior Modification. The stew is the modality. Precision was the advertisement. The advertisement was not always the hour.

Feminist and multicultural critics later asked whose behavior was being modified toward what norm. A child's "on-task" sitting can be a classroom peace and a cultural demand. A woman's "assertiveness training" in the 1970s could be a feminist tool and a demand that she perform a certain middle-class speech. The behavioral package did not escape politics by counting. Counting is a politics of what is allowed to count.

Cognitive therapy, the next essay, would insist that the missing object was a thought. Behavior therapists had already been arguing among themselves about whether covert events were fair game. The "cognitive revolution" in experimental psychology and the cognitive turn in the clinic are not the same revolution, though textbooks rhyme them. Wolpe remained suspicious of inner commentary as the engine. Beck and Ellis made it the engine. The quarrel is the through-line of the next file. It does not erase 1920, 1924, 1953, 1958, or 1965.

## A speech clinic and a borrowed mouth

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. It does not run a token-economy ward and it does not teach exposure. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names counseling as a service-delivery domain about communication, feeding, and swallowing. Practice schedules, models, and "try that sound again" are older than Wolpe in the speech room. They are not, on that account, free of the behavioral century.

Families still hear "behavior plan" in schools and clinics as if the phrase were neutral. The phrase has a Johns Hopkins scar, a 1924 nursery, a 1958 book, and a 1965 Illinois ward inside it. Heritage education on a speech site can say that without becoming a behavior-therapy shop. The live license is not counseling. The rooms still require a person to decide whether a countable act is the thing that matters, or only the thing that was easy to see.

The therapy-history pack can keep the founder biographies. This pack keeps the skill claim: that a helper could change a life by arranging cues and consequences. The claim produced techniques, textbooks, wards, and refusals. The refusals belong in the same paragraph as the techniques. A modality that cannot say no to its own 1920 paper is not yet a craft.
