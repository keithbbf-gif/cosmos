**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1964 Grove Press published Eric Berne's *Games People Play: The Psychology of Human Relationships*. Airport racks took it. Dinner tables learned to diagnose a "game." Parent, Adult, and Child — three circles, stacked or overlapping — left the Tuesday-night seminar and entered the American living room. The 1961 clinical book, *Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy* (Grove), had already named the method for professionals. 1964 is the date the language escaped.

This essay is about TA as a *skill language*: a vocabulary a helper and a client could share without a year of analysis. It is not a license to scrape a workshop headshot. Many photographs of Berne still sit in copyright. Until a public-domain or Creative Commons file is confirmed, this page uses type or a title page, not a guessed face.

## San Francisco seminars, then a bestseller

Berne (born Eric Bernstein, 1910, Montreal) ran the San Francisco Social Psychiatry Seminars, a regular room in which papers were argued and ego-state diagrams were drawn. The *Transactional Analysis Bulletin* carried the in-house science. The International Transactional Analysis Association organized in 1964, the year of the popular book. Berne died on 15 July 1970. The dates matter because TA's public life is longer than its founder's, and the language outlived the seminar.

*Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy* (1961) offered clinicians a structural account: ego states labeled Parent, Adult, and Child; transactions as the unit of social action; scripts as longer life stories; games as predictable sequences with a payoff. The 1961 book is the clinical object. *Games People Play* (1964) is the translation into named sequences a general reader could gossip about. Chapter titles — among them "Why Don't You — Yes But" and other labels Berne printed as names of games — became party tricks. This series will not reprint the games as a diagnostic checklist a reader could run on a spouse. Naming a 1964 chapter is history. Scoring your household is a protocol we will not print.

The skill claim was radical in a mid-century way. You did not need to wait for the transference to ripen across years. You could, in Berne's account, hear which ego state had just spoken, and which had answered. Crossed transactions explained the sudden fight. Complementary transactions explained the smooth ones. A counselor who learned the ear had a map. Maps are teachable. They are also easy to over-apply.

## Parent-Adult-Child as popular language

The three-circle diagram is TA's public face. It is why this essay treats TA as a *skill language* rather than only as a school. Other modalities in this wave have institutes, manuals, and trials. TA had those too — the ITAA, later training standards, later journals — and it had something the others envied: a cartoon a personnel office could mimeograph.

"Parent" in the diagram is not your mother. It is a set of borrowed attitudes and voices. "Child" is not childishness as an insult. It is a set of archaic feelings and adaptations. "Adult" is the ego state Berne associated with present-tense data. Folk use collapsed the three into inner children and inner critics. Folk use is part of the history. It is also how a clinical distinction dies into a greeting card.

Thomas A. Harris's *I'm OK — You're OK* (Harper & Row, 1967) sold even more widely than Berne's 1964 book and taught a four-cell life-position grid that later TA trainers both used and tried to distinguish from Berne. The popularization quarrel is the fight this essay will keep. Some ITAA people heard Harris as a door. Some heard a dilution. Counselor-education surveys often assigned Harris because undergraduates would finish him. They did not always assign the 1961 Grove text. Language that travels light travels without its footnotes.

Workshop culture did the rest. Weekend certificates. Chalk-circle demonstrations. "What ego state are you in?" as a cocktail question. The 1970s were kind to any method that could be drawn in ten minutes. TA was kind to the 1970s. Corporate training borrowed the circles for "communication skills" that had little to do with Berne's psychiatric seminar. Leakage, again. The leakage is why a speech-site history must say: this is a counseling language that became a general American dialect, not a proof that every use of "inner child" is TA.

## Scripts, strokes, and the things the poster dropped

Script theory — the idea that a person lives a story decided early, with a payoff — is closer to narrative's later re-authoring (essay 27) than the party-game version of TA admits. Claude Steiner and others developed script analysis in Berne's circle and after. *Scripts People Live* (1974) is a later object. This file will not teach script analysis. It will say that TA, at its clinical register, was already a story method with a structural cartoon on top.

"Strokes" as units of recognition are another TA word that escaped. The claim that people need recognition, even negative recognition, and will play games to get it, is a 1960s social-psychiatric sentence. It is not a permission slip for a reader to reorganize a marriage. Feminist readers later asked whose scripts were being called games, and whether a woman's adaptation to a violent house was being cute-named. That question does not require a composite case. It is a reading of a slang that made suffering sound clever.

Berne's own tone in *Games People Play* is dry, sometimes comic, sometimes cold. The coldness is in the record. A language that makes people into players can help a clinician hear a sequence. It can also help a clinician refuse pity. Whether that is a virtue depends on the hour. History can name the tone without handing out a tone to copy.

## Copyright, headshots, and the living apparatus

ITAA and related bodies still train. Living teachers are not public property. No health speculation. No scraped conference badges as portraits. The Berne estate and Grove/Penguin reprints still sit in ordinary copyright. Title pages are safer images than faces. A napkin diagram of three circles, drawn for this series as an original, would be a period object if it is clearly a teaching diagram and not a worksheet to complete. Even then, a caption should not ask "which one are you?" That caption is a quiz. This series does not print quizzes.

The portrait field in this file is `confirm` because a later graphics pass might find a clean Commons image. Until then: type. Honesty about the face is part of the same ethic as honesty about the language. TA taught America to label people quickly. A history page that labels Berne with an unlicensed photograph would be a joke the 1964 book could have named.

## What counseling programs kept

By the 1980s many master's theories courses gave TA a week: draw the circles, define a game, mention scripts, move on to Gestalt. The week is how a skill language becomes a fossil. Clinicians who stayed with the ITAA kept a more technical ear — transactional options, game formulas, group contracts. The fossil and the technical ear are both historical. An integrative counselor (essay 35) might still say "that sounded like a Parent" without paying ITAA dues. The sentence is a loanword. Loanwords do not make the speaker a TA therapist.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. It does not offer transactional analysis. Families already have a folk language of "games" and "that's your inner child talking." Heritage education can say the folk language has a 1961–1964 binding and a 1967 popularizer. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice does not include ego-state diagrams. An SLP who hears a parent and a child talking past each other is not required to draw three circles. They are required, some days, to notice that two people are in different conversations. Berne named that noticing in a particular slang. Other essays in this pack named it as reflection, as circular questions, as alliance. The slang is the modality's contribution. The rest is the field.

Do not come away with a diagram to try on relatives. Come away with a date: 1964, Grove Press, a clinical vocabulary that escaped its seminar, and a portrait we will not fake.
