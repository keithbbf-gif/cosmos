**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In September 1944 the *American Journal of Psychiatry* published Erich Lindemann's "Symptomatology and Management of Acute Grief." The paper still sits on crisis-history syllabi because part of its clinical material came from bereaved people connected to the Cocoanut Grove nightclub fire in Boston on 28 November 1942. Lindemann described acute grief as a syndrome with psychological and bodily features, capable of appearing at once, of being delayed, or of arriving in distorted form. He wrote as a psychiatrist at Massachusetts General Hospital, in wartime, with a medical audience. This essay will not restage the fire. It will not use photographs of the dead. Lindemann is here as a *pointer*: American clinical language about grief has a 1944 document. Communication clinics borrowed that language, often badly, for losses that were not a nightclub and not always a death.

If you are in danger, call local emergency services.

That is the only safety sentence this piece will carry. No protocol follows it.

## Acute grief is the wrong template, and clinics used it anyway

Lindemann's grief was acute, relational, and, in his argument, work that could be done: a "grief work" toward resolution. Later crisis psychiatry — Gerald Caplan's *Principles of Preventive Psychiatry* (1964) is the usual next book — built a time-limited idea of crisis as a period with a beginning. A stroke on a Tuesday is a beginning. The swallow that does not return on the timeline a family was promised is not a crisis in Caplan's sense. It is a chronic rearrangement. Aphasia that is still aphasia at six months is not a delayed Cocoanut Grove. Voice that never sounds like the voice on the wedding tape is not a syndrome to be "completed."

CSD clinicians talked as if it were, for a long time, because the culture handed them stages. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's *On Death and Dying* (1969) — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance — was a hospice observation that leaked into every American helping profession. Speech-language pathology textbooks and in-service days borrowed the stages to describe "what parents go through" or "what the patient is feeling." The borrow was a historical event. It was also a category error. Kübler-Ross was writing about dying people in a hospital. Luterman's parents of a newly identified deaf child, and Holland's adults with aphasia, were often not dying. They were living in a language that had changed.

The professional temptation is to grade the family on the stages. A father who is "still angry" at month four is then behind. A woman who jokes in an aphasia group is then "in denial." History of this skill includes the harm of that grading. Luterman's line of CSD counseling kept insisting that affect was not a deviation from the home program. Holland's wellness line, after 2007, kept insisting that a life with aphasia still had contents other than loss. Both were corrections. Neither was a script for a family member reading this page.

## How the professions talked about swallow, speech, and voice after a neurological event

A hospital speech-language pathologist, mid- to late twentieth century, often entered a room as the person who would say whether the person could eat. That sentence is medical and existential at once. The history of "counseling" in those rooms is, first, the history of how results were delivered: standing, sitting, with family present or not, in English or not, with or without an interpreter. Informational counseling, in the later ASHA Practice Portal sense, is the name for the explanation. Personal-adjustment counseling is the name for the feeling that arrives when the explanation is understood. The professions did not always have those names. They had the rooms.

Rehabilitation literature talked about "acceptance of disability" in a vocabulary borrowed from mid-century rehabilitation counseling — Beatrice Wright's work on attitude toward disability is a related object in that neighboring field, not a CSD protocol. "Acceptance" can be a humane hope. It can also be a demand that the patient stop being inconvenient. ASHA's 2016 Scope named "acceptance, adaptation, and decision making" as the topics of SLP counseling about communication, feeding, and swallowing. The nouns are institutional. They do not tell you whether the clinician in 1975, 1995, or 2015 treated adaptation as a stage to be reached or as a long ordinary labor.

Voice after a neurological event, or after laryngeal surgery, produced another dialect of adjustment talk: the person who "mourns the old voice." Some of that talk was accurate about attachment to a sound. Some of it was the clinician narrating a feeling the speaker had not offered. Rogers's 1957 condition — the client must *perceive* the empathy — is the caution. A mourning speech delivered at the patient is not personal-adjustment counseling. It is a eulogy to the wrong person.

Aphasia groups, from the mid-century through Audrey Holland's later writing, became a place where adjustment was social rather than a private stage. Humor in those groups embarrassed visitors who had come to watch grief. Holland's wellness textbooks (2007, 2014, later third edition) treated that embarrassment as the visitor's problem. The historical claim is not that groups "cure" grief. It is that CSD had a practice — sitting with other people whose sentences had also broken — that did not match Lindemann's acute syndrome and did not need Kübler-Ross to begin.

## Chronic change, ambiguous loss, and the borrowed library

Counselors outside CSD later used Pauline Boss's "ambiguous loss" for disappearances without death: a person present and not present. Families after severe brain injury met that structure without needing the term. CSD borrowed it when it was useful and ignored it when a chart required a simpler "adjusted / not adjusted." The word *adjustment* in this essay's title is therefore already a professional artifact. It sounds like a thermostat. Lives are not thermostats.

Luterman's later editions kept existential notes: freedom, responsibility, isolation, meaning — a temperature some CSD students found too philosophical for a swallow clinic. The portal's later list of "particularly applicable" theories includes existential among others. That is a syllabus fact. It is not an instruction to conduct existential therapy. The SLP who recognizes that a feeding tube has rearranged a family's meals is doing the 2016 domain. The SLP who treats a major depressive episode, a suicidal plan, or a trauma syndrome as if it were "adjustment to dysphagia" has left the domain. Referral is the historical and ethical next act. This essay will not describe how to assess those states. It will say the border exists.

Grief in the identified patient and grief in the spouse are not the same hour. CSD family work, as the previous essay argued, had to learn that without becoming marriage therapy. A spouse who has become a full-time interpreter for an aphasic partner is living a job change. Calling that job "caregiving" is already a frame. History can name the frame. It cannot issue a schedule for rest. No caregiver instructions belong here.

## What Lindemann cannot do for a speech clinic

Lindemann remains worth reading as a 1944 argument that grief has a bodily picture and that medicine was tempted to ignore it. He cannot tell an SLP how to talk after a modified barium swallow. He cannot tell a family how to feel about a voice. He cannot underwrite a staged model of "getting over" aphasia. Caplan cannot either. The crisis-counseling essay later in this pack will stay with that lineage as a short-form history. This piece's job is narrower: to separate *acute grief as a dated psychiatric object* from *adjustment-talk in CSD* as a decades-long professional habit, sometimes humane, sometimes coercive, always adjacent to a license the SLP does not hold.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. Families here meet communication and swallowing change in the same rooms where mileage, hospital discharge, and a local SLP's hour coincide. These pages do not claim the clinic runs grief groups or practices a psychiatric method. They claim only that the words "grief" and "adjustment" arrived in such rooms with a paper trail — 1944, 1964, 1969, Luterman's CSD books, Holland's wellness books, ASHA 2016 — and that the trail is easier to honor if no one pretends a nightclub fire and a chronic aphasia are the same event.

The useful caution is almost administrative. When a speech clinic uses "grief" it should know whether it means Lindemann's syndrome, a family's ordinary sorrow, a stage model it cannot defend, or a need to refer. The words are not interchangeable. The 1944 paper is a pointer, not a map of the rest of a life.
