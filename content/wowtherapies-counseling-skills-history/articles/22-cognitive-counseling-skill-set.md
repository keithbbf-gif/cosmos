**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1961 Aaron T. Beck, C. H. Ward, M. Mendelson, J. Mock, and J. Erbaugh published "An Inventory for Measuring Depression" in the *Archives of General Psychiatry*. Twenty-one item groups. A score. A paper instrument that a later profession would treat as furniture. The inventory is the first dated object in this essay because cognitive counseling as a *skill set* arrived as paperwork before it arrived as a brand. You could hand someone a list of sentences about sadness, guilt, appetite, and suicide, and you could add. The adding was the claim: inner weather had become countable without a Freudian year.

This is not another life of Beck. That life sits in the therapy-history pack. Ellis's life sits there too. Linehan's dialectical year is a third neighboring biography. This file is the skill claim those lives packaged: that a helper could work on thoughts as thoughts, on a form, in a shorter hour than analysis had budgeted.

## A 1961 list, a 1967 book, a 1976 name, a 1979 manual

Beck's *Depression: Clinical, Experimental, and Theoretical Aspects* (Harper & Row, 1967) argued from clinic and from the inventory that depressed people showed a patterned view of self, world, and future. Later classrooms called it the cognitive triad. The 1967 book is a research-and-clinic document, later reprinted as *Depression: Causes and Treatment*. It is not a worksheet.

*Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders* (International Universities Press, 1976) named the therapy in a title a bookstore could carry. Automatic thoughts, assumptions, and the idea that a mood might follow a reading of an event — those are the 1976 skill objects. They are descriptions of a method's furniture. They are not instructions for a reader to run on a Sunday afternoon.

*Cognitive Therapy of Depression* (Guilford, 1979), with A. John Rush, Brian F. Shaw, and Gary Emery, is the manual that counselor-education programs actually stole from. Session structure. Activity scheduling as a dated clinic object. The Daily Record of Dysfunctional Thoughts: a columnar sheet that asked, in the 1979 printing, for a situation, an emotion, an automatic thought, a more considered response, and an outcome rating. That record is a historical object. This series will not print a blank one. A blank record is a protocol. A described record is a fact about 1979.

The inventory, the 1967 argument, the 1976 name, and the 1979 columns are four different claims. Collapsing them into "Beck invented CBT" is the therapy-history pack's temptation. This pack's job is meaner: to say that a skill set is a set of *forms and questions* that could be taught to people who would never read the 1967 footnotes.

## Ellis, 1955 in the office, 1956 at APA, 1962 in hard covers

Albert Ellis dated his clinical turn toward "rational" work to 1955. The first public paper, "Rational Psychotherapy," was delivered at the American Psychological Association meeting in Chicago on 31 August 1956. "Rational psychotherapy and individual psychology" appeared in the *Journal of Individual Psychology* in 1957. "Rational psychotherapy" in the *Journal of General Psychology* in 1958. *Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy* (Lyle Stuart, 1962) is the book this bibliography keeps. The method's later names — rational-emotive therapy, then rational emotive behavior therapy after Ellis's 1993 rename — are a naming history, not a second biography.

Ellis's skill claim differed from Beck's even when undergraduates mixed the two into one "cognitive" week. Ellis organized the hour around beliefs he called irrational, often in an ABC pattern (activating event, belief, consequence) that trainers later drew on whiteboards. He argued in public. The Institute in New York ran Friday-night workshops that were, among other things, a theater of disputation: a volunteer, a microphone, a famous man interrupting a sentence. That room is part of the skill's history. It is also why some later clinicians heard "cognitive" as an attack. Beck's Penn style was quieter on the surface and more self-consciously empirical. Ellis's style was faster and more willing to be rude in the service of a belief. Two houses. One textbook chapter. The chapter is the problem.

This essay will not print Ellis's lists of "musts" as a self-help inventory. It will not print Beck's columns as a form to complete. Both objects exist. Completing them is a treatment act. Reading that they existed is history.

## What the skill set was for, in the argument of its decade

Cognitive work presented itself as a reply to two predecessors at once. To psychoanalysis it said: you do not need a childhood reconstructed at fifty-minute length to change a present sentence. To behavior therapy, the previous essay's package, it said: the missing variable is the interpretation of the cue, not only the cue. Wolpe's 1958 book had treated inner commentary as a poor engine. Beck and Ellis made it the engine. The fight was not always polite. Behavior therapists accused the cognitive camp of smuggling ghosts. Cognitive therapists accused the behavioral camp of counting the wrong thing. By the 1980s many training programs had simply hyphenated the quarrel into "CBT" and moved on. The hyphen is a historical truce, not a proof that 1958 and 1976 were the same craft.

Measurement culture followed the inventory. Later revisions of the BDI, the flood of other paper-and-pencil scales, and the demand that a graduate student produce a pre-post number — those are how a 1961 research tool became a clinic's weather. Critics said the weather favored people who could read English forms quickly and who shared the inventory's idea of a symptom. That critique belongs with the multicultural-competencies essay later in this wave. It does not wait for 1992 to be true. A 1961 list already chose a dialect of distress.

## Socratic questions, records, and the restatement problem

This pack already has an essay on questions (Socratic, circular, miracle). Cognitive counseling borrowed a "Socratic" adjective for a style of inquiry that asked the speaker to test a thought. Trainers loved the adjective because it made a clinic sound like a seminar. Historians of philosophy are allowed to wince. The counseling object is narrower: a helper who does not immediately supply a better thought, but who asks for evidence, alternatives, or a percentage of belief. Clara Hill's later taxonomies would sort such questions among other helping skills. Ivey's microskills had already made "open question" a beginner unit in 1971. Cognitive therapy did not invent the question. It specialized the question toward a thought-as-hypothesis.

The thought record then did what reflection had done in the first essays of this pack: it made an inner event filmable as handwriting. A supervisor could look at a sheet and say whether the automatic thought had been caught. The sheet could also become the hour. Clients who wrote to please the columns learned a genre. That is the restatement problem in paper form. Parroting had been a mouth. The record could parrot a thought back to itself until the week looked like a form.

Third-wave and contextual therapies of the 1990s–2000s — including Linehan's dialectical behavior therapy, whose life is next door — later argued that changing the relationship to a thought might matter as much as editing its content. That argument is a later chapter. It is not a reason to skip 1961–1979. The skill set those years named is still what most public "CBT" posters mean, even when the posters have never seen the 1979 table of contents.

## Efficacy sentences, kept historical

Beck's group and later trialists published outcome studies that a certain kind of health system learned to cite. This essay will not say that cognitive therapy is the treatment of choice for a diagnosis. It will say that a 1979 manual and a 1961 inventory together made cognitive work *auditable* in a way that some rivals were not. Auditability is a historical advantage. It is also a historical distortion. What is easy to score is not identical with what happens between two people.

Ellis's outcome claims were noisier and less beloved of later grant cultures. The Friday-night room was not a randomized trial. Both facts can sit in one paragraph. A profession that kept Beck's forms and dropped Ellis's microphone still kept Ellis's idea that a belief could be argued with. The argument, not the trademark, is the skill.

## A speech clinic that already meets a thought

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. Its license is not an LPC wall. Families in the waiting room still arrive with sentences about a child's "attitude," a partner's "motivation," a grandparent's "giving up" after a swallow result. Those sentences are not DSM objects on this site. They are the folk cousins of what 1967 called a view of self, world, and future.

ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice locates counseling in communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not license an SLP to run cognitive therapy of depression. Luterman's 1979 parent-counseling lineage, already named in this pack, warned against turning the hour into advice. Cognitive counseling's skill set is a historical warning of a different kind: that a helper who goes after thoughts can forget the body that is trying to speak, chew, or stay in the room. Audrey Holland's later wellness-framed CSD texts (2007 and after) kept a humanistic inheritance and did not ask the person with aphasia to fill a 1979 record. Different rooms. Same century of paperwork.

The sister pack may keep Beck and Ellis as founders. This pack keeps the inventory, the columns, the 1962 book, and the quarrel with behavior. A curious reader can know that "cognitive skills" were named in particular years. A curious reader cannot, from this page, be handed a blank record. The blank is the treatment. The date is the history.
