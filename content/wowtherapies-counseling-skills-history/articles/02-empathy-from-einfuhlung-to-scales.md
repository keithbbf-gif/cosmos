**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1909 Edward B. Titchener, lecturing at Cornell on the thought-processes, needed an English word for a German aesthetic claim. He chose *empathy*. The German had been *Einfühlung* — a "feeling-into" that Theodor Lipps, around 1903–1906, had used for the way a viewer enters a form. The word did not begin in a clinic. It began in a quarrel about art and the self.

A century later a graduate student can still be told that empathy is "walking in someone's shoes," as if the shoes were the method. This essay is the opposite of that poster. It follows the word into psychology, into Rogers's 1957 conditions, into Truax's 1961 rating sheet, and into the later split between a measurable counselor behavior and a psychoanalytic *immersion*. It does not teach you how to be empathic. It tells you when people thought they could tell.

## Aesthetics, not kindness

Lipps was not writing a helping manual. *Einfühlung* was a theory of how inner movement gets attributed to an object. The danger of the later clinic is that it kept the warmth and lost the strangeness. Titchener's 1909 lectures imported the term into an English experimental vocabulary that was already arguing about introspection. The American century then did what it often does: it made the import useful.

Social psychologists later measured "empathic concern" as if it were a trait you could have more of. C. Daniel Batson's empathy-altruism work (1980s–1990s) belongs to that laboratory, not to a fifty-minute hour. A history of *counseling* empathy has to keep the lab and the hour from stealing each other's clothes.

## Rogers makes it a condition

The 1957 paper did not say "be kind." It said empathic understanding — sensing the client's inner world as if it were your own, without losing the "as if" — was one of the necessary and sufficient conditions of therapeutic personality change, *if* the client perceived it. The "as if" is the whole technical claim. Lose it and you have identification. Keep it and you have a discipline.

*On Becoming a Person* (1961) popularized the tone. Supervisors popularized the failure mode: the counselor who narrates the client's feeling one beat late, or one adjective off, and calls the miss warmth. Rogers taped the misses. That is why the skill has a history and not only a reputation.

G. T. Barrett-Lennard's 1962 monograph built a Relationship Inventory so that the *client's* view of empathy, regard, and congruence could be scored. The inventory is a reminder that Rogers's last condition was never only the counselor's self-report. A later training culture sometimes graded the helper and forgot to ask the person in the other chair.

## The scale, the slice, the fight

Charles Truax's Accurate Empathy Scale (1961) tried to make a rater, hearing a short tape segment, place the two-person event on a ladder. Stage language — low awareness of feeling at one end, fine-grained tracking at the other — made empathy look like a craft exam. *Toward Effective Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1967) argued that the exam predicted change. Replication arguments followed. So did the charge that raters were scoring a house dialect of reflection, not a human universal.

Robert Carkhuff's 1969 five-point revision (*Helping and Human Relations*) shortened the ladder for training. Level 3 as "interchangeable" response became a classroom proverb. Level 3 is a historical object. It is not a homework assignment in this series. The proverb outlived the grant.

Heinz Kohut's *The Analysis of the Self* (1971) used empathy as the method of data-gathering in a psychoanalytic frame: prolonged empathic immersion. That is a different claim from Truax's slice. Kohut was not offering a five-point sheet. Counselors who later said "we are all empathic now" were mixing a humanistic condition, a rating technology, and a self-psychological method. The mix is the modern word. The mix is also why the word feels spongy.

## What empathy was accused of

Feminist and multicultural writers in the 1970s–1990s pointed out that "accurate" empathy often meant accurate to a white, middle-class feeling vocabulary. A woman naming anger as anger could be scored as well tracked. A woman naming anger as tiredness — because tiredness was safer — could be scored as poorly differentiated. The miss was not always the counselor's ear. Sometimes it was the scale's idea of a feeling.

Disability communities said a related thing. Empathy that imagines "what it would be like if I could not speak" is still the speaker's fantasy. Luterman's later CSD work, and Holland's wellness frame, kept insisting that the professional's job was not to occupy the other life. Occupation is not the 1957 "as if." It is the collapse of the "as if."

## The Wisconsin weather

The Wisconsin project is usually told as Rogers's late institutional disappointment. For empathy's history it is something sharper: a place where a university condition met a hospital census. Truax's scales were built in that weather. If empathy "worked" on a tape from a motivated student, the project asked whether it still worked when the other person did not share the vocabulary of "feelings." Some of the later skepticism about the scales is really skepticism about that translation. A rater in a grant office and a patient on a ward did not owe each other a dialect.

Gendlin's experiencing work, already named in the listening essay, belongs here as a rival measurement. The Experiencing Scale tried to hear whether the *speaker* was in contact with a felt sense. Truax tried to hear whether the *helper* was tracking. Two instruments, one decade, two answers to "what would we have to hear to say this is happening?" Counselor education mostly kept Truax's answer because it graded the student. That is an administrative history as much as a scientific one.

## A small-city clinic and a large word

Kohut's heirs and Rogers's heirs spent the 1980s talking past each other with the same English noun. One meant a method of gathering psychoanalytic data over years. The other meant a condition that might be present in a single recorded hour. A textbook that used both footnotes without saying so produced a generation of students who thought they had learned one skill.

On a WOW Therapies page this history has a local use. Families in Southeast Arkansas will hear "we are an empathetic office" on a hundred websites. The word, in that sentence, means manners. The 1957 sentence meant a testable interpersonal event. The 1961 scale meant a rater and a reel. The 1971 Kohut sentence meant a long analytic stance. If a clinic uses the word, it should know which century it is borrowing.

The therapy-history pack can keep Rogers the man. This pack keeps the translation problem: German aesthetics to Cornell English to Chicago conditions to Wisconsin numbers to every later syllabus that wanted a soft word for a hard skill.
