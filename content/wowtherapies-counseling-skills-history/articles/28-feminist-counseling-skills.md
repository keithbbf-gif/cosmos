**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1972 Doubleday published Phyllis Chesler's *Women and Madness*. The book did not arrive as a treatment manual. It arrived as an accusation with footnotes: that American psychiatry and psychology had used madness as a name for women's noncompliance, misery, and confinement, and that the hospital, the marriage, and the diagnostic sentence could be the same politics in different buildings. This essay follows how a political literature and a living-room practice called consciousness-raising became, by the 1970s–1990s, a set of counseling *skills* with institutes and a code. It will not invent a case. Composite patients do not belong on this page. The books are enough.

## Consciousness-raising was not a clinic

Second-wave women's groups in the late 1960s — New York Radical Women and related circles — treated the small group as a political form. "Consciousness-raising" meant speaking personal experience in a circle in order to hear a pattern that private suffering had hidden. Kathie Sarachild and others named the practice in movement documents. It was not Rogerian therapy. It was not a leader's technique. The leaderless (or leader-suspicious) circle is the room this essay will keep beside Chesler's book, because feminist counseling had to decide whether it was that circle with a license, or a profession that had read the circle and then billed.

The Association for Women in Psychology organized in 1969. APA's Division 35 (later the Society for the Psychology of Women) dates to 1973. Those are professional-history objects. They are how a movement grew a committee. Skills are slower than committees. A counselor who had sat in a CR group and then took a job in a community clinic had to decide what to *do* with the mouth: still refuse expertise, or become a feminist expert. That decision is the modality's inner fight. It never fully ended.

## Broverman 1970, a study the decade needed

Inge K. Broverman, Donald M. Broverman, Frank E. Clarkson, Paul S. Rosenkrantz, and Susan R. Vogel published "Sex-role stereotypes and clinical judgments of mental health" in the *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 34 (1970): 1–7. Clinicians described a healthy adult, a healthy man, and a healthy woman. The paper argued that the healthy adult matched the healthy man more than the healthy woman — that to be a healthy woman, on those ratings, was to be less like a healthy adult. Feminist counseling cited the study for decades as evidence that "neutrality" was already sexed.

Later methodologists, including Widiger and Settle in 1987, argued that the finding was partly an artifact of the instrument and the design. That critique is also the record. A skills history can say both: 1970 became a movement fact, and the movement fact was then argued as science. Feminist therapy did not wait for the last methodological word. It treated the clinic's double standard as something a helper could *hear* in adjectives — "dependent," "hysterical," "not feminine enough" — and refuse.

Chesler's 1972 chapters on hospitals, on lesbians, on the double bind of femininity as both requirement and diagnosis, gave the Broverman problem a narrative temperature. *Women and Madness* is not a skills textbook. It is why a later skills textbook had a problem to be skilled *about*.

## What got named as feminist counseling skills

By the late 1970s–1980s, feminist therapists were listing practices that this pack would recognize as skill claims: making power in the room visible; refusing to treat a woman's anger as a symptom to extinguish; locating distress in conditions (pay, violence, law) as well as in interior sentences; self-disclosure used as a political and relational act rather than as a slip; a suspicion of "adjustment" as the goal. None of those sentences is a session script. They are the opposite of the behavioral target that said "reduce complaints." They are also in tension with Glasser's 1965 responsibility talk (essay 23) when responsibility is heard as a private duty that leaves the street unnamed.

The Feminist Therapy Institute, organized in the early 1980s, issued a feminist code of ethics in 1987 (later revised). The code is a professional-history object: overlapping relationships, accountability, diversity, social change as ethical matter. It is not a quiz for the reader. This pack does not print ethics codes as self-score sheets.

Jean Baker Miller's *Toward a New Psychology of Women* (1976) and the later Stone Center / relational-cultural work are a neighboring New England lineage: growth in connection, not as a separate brand this essay will annex. They show that "feminist psychology" was never one institute. Skills scattered. The scattering is the history.

## Brown 1994: theory as the skill

Laura S. Brown's *Subversive Dialogues: Theory in Feminist Therapy* (Basic Books, 1994) is the book this bibliography names for the 1990s. Brown argued that feminist therapy is not a kind mood in any modality. It is a theory of power that must rewrite technique. "Subversive" in the title is a claim about the counseling hour: that a helper who does not ask how gender, sexuality, race, and class wrote the presenting story will reproduce the story. Living author. Public book. No gossip.

The 1994 text is also a fight with the idea of feminist therapy as a white, middle-class, heterosexual office. Brown wrote inside a profession that had already been told, by women of color, that "woman" was not a simple category. *This Bridge Called My Back* (1981), the Combahee River Collective Statement (1977), and a long Black feminist and Chicana literature are not counseling manuals. They are the political documents that made a 1972 book incomplete as a last word. A feminist-skills history that ends with Chesler and a CR group has stopped in a room that was already being criticized.

Multicultural counseling competencies (next essay) are a sibling professional object. They are not a substitute for feminist therapy, and feminist therapy is not a substitute for them. A 1992 competencies paper and a 1994 theory book can sit on one shelf and still argue.

## No invented cases, and the other refusals

This series will not offer "a woman I saw who…" stories. It will not offer a composite "Maria" to make a point. Chesler used published cases, interviews, and historical confinement as evidence. Brown used theory and the profession's own documents. A heritage page that invents a client to prove sexism would be doing a different, worse thing than the Broverman study.

Conversion practices aimed at lesbian and bisexual women are part of the harm record. Bayer's 1981 *Homosexuality and American Psychiatry* sits in this pack's bibliography as backdrop. Name the harm. Do not teach the method. Feminist counseling's skill claim included a refusal to treat desire as a symptom to be reversed. That refusal is historical. It is not a worksheet.

Efficacy sentences stay bounded. There is no honest way, on this URL, to say "feminist therapy works better than X." There is an honest way to say that a profession's adjectives were shown, in 1970, to be already gendered, and that a 1972 book and a 1994 book made that hearing a craft.

## A speech clinic, a gendered waiting room

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. It is not a feminist-therapy institute. Mothers still carry more of the clinic bag. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice locates counseling in communication, feeding, and swallowing. Families of children with communication difference, and women after a voice or swallow change, sit in a history this file has named without becoming a gender clinic.

Luterman's parent work often met mothers first. That is a demographic fact of many CSD rooms, not a theory. Heritage education can say that feminist counseling skills taught the wider helping fields to hear expertise, unpaid labor, and the medicalization of unhappiness as questions about the hour. An SLP who "just explains the report" still chooses a listener. Chesler and Brown are why that choice has a literature.

Do not take a CR agenda from this page. Take the dates: Broverman 1970, Chesler 1972, AWP 1969, Division 35 in 1973, FTI ethics 1987, Brown 1994. And the standing warning: a skill that cannot say which women it was written for has already failed the 1981 bookshelf.
