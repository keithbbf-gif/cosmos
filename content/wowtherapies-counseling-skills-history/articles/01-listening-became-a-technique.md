**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1940 Carl Rogers, then at Ohio State, began recording interviews on a machine that made the hour heavier than a conscience. Wire and disc were clumsy. They were also a method. A student could count pronouns. A supervisor could stop the reel and ask what the counselor had just done. Listening, which pastoral manuals had treated as a virtue, acquired a timestamp.

This essay is not another life of Rogers. That life sits in the therapy-history pack. This one is about the moment a moral habit became a *technique* — something you could name, fail, teach, and later export into speech-language pathology classrooms that had never heard of the Wisconsin schizophrenia project.

## Before the reel

Nineteenth-century moral treatment already required a kind of attention. Samuel Tuke's York Retreat (the 1813 *Description*) asked keepers to speak as if the patient were a guest. That is not counseling. It is an institutional etiquette with a theology. Freud's "evenly hovering attention" is closer to a skill claim, but it was taught as an analytic attitude, not as a countable behavior. You did not get a score of 3.0 for hovering.

American guidance after Frank Parsons (Bureau 1908; book 1909) told the counselor to know the person and know the work. The knowing was often a file. School "listening" in the 1920s and 1930s meant a conference period and a card. The card survived. The silence in the room was not yet a research object.

## Chicago, Wisconsin, and the scandal of playback

*Client-Centered Therapy* (1951) made the recordings public argument. Rogers was not the first to tape. He was the first whose movement treated the tape as the thing itself. The 1957 paper in the *Journal of Consulting Psychology* listed conditions. Empathic understanding was one. Congruence was one. Unconditional positive regard was one. The client's *perception* that these were happening was the last gate. A later generation would forget the last gate and keep the first three as a poster.

At Wisconsin in the late 1950s and early 1960s the project tried to take the conditions into a hospital population that did not share the university clinic's manners. Charles Truax and Robert Carkhuff came out of that weather with scales. The Accurate Empathy Scale is dated to 1961 in Truax's own grant notes; Unconditional Positive Regard and Genuineness scales follow in 1962. *Toward Effective Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1967) bound the claim: you could rate a slice of tape, and the rating would talk about outcome. Critics later said the slices were too short and the raters too trained in the house style. The critics did not put the reel back in the box.

## Attending as a behavior you could film

Allen Ivey's group at Colorado State used videotape when two-inch decks were still a dare. The April 1968 APGA paper — "Micro-Counseling and Attending Behavior," ERIC ED021275 — treated eye contact, body orientation, and verbal following as units a beginner could practice in a morning. *Microcounseling* (Charles C. Thomas, 1971) made the units a book. Ivey later said the model was born in 1967. The paper is 1968. The book is 1971. History can live with all three dates if it does not collapse them.

"Attending" was a word that let listening leave the Rogerian church without leaving the room. You could teach it to a medical student, a librarian, an agricultural extension agent. Ivey's later editions advertised those migrations. They are part of the skill's history. They are also how a clinic word became a corporate workshop. The tape did not choose its later audience.

## What the count could not hear

A scale hears what it was built to hear. Truax's stages describe a counselor who tracks "acknowledged feelings" with rising accuracy. They do not describe a counselor who sits still while a parent of a newly diagnosed deaf child rearranges a life. They do not describe an SLP who has just delivered a swallow result. Luterman's 1979 *Counseling Parents of Hearing-Impaired Children* would insist that the professional's job, some days, is not to reflect faster. The measurement tradition and the CSD counseling tradition grew up in the same decades and did not always share a hallway.

Cross-cultural critiques arrived on the skill itself. Eye contact as "good attending" is a North American classroom rule. Ivey's later work with Mary Bradford Ivey and others tried to write that rule down as a *choice*, not a law. The first papers were less careful. A history that loves the 1968 film should also say what the film assumed about faces.

## Kagan's other machine

Norman Kagan's Interpersonal Process Recall, circulating from a 1963 paper with Krathwohl and Miller and then through 1970s manuals, used video for a different confession. The tape was stopped. The counselor was asked what had been thought and felt in the moment just shown. IPR did not hand the beginner a list of attending behaviors. It handed a playback and a question about inner weather. Historians who treat Ivey as the only video story miss a parallel: one machine taught units, the other taught recall. Both assumed that a person could bear to watch themselves. Not every trainee could. The refusal to watch is also a datum, though it rarely made the methods chapters.

Eugene Gendlin, who had been with Rogers in Chicago and on the Wisconsin project, later published *Focusing* (1978) and kept the Experiencing Scale as a way to talk about the *client's* inner process rather than the counselor's mouth. Listening, in that lineage, is not only attending behavior. It is a bet that the speaker has a bodily next step that words have not caught. CSD clinics meet a version of that bet when a gesture outruns a sentence. They did not need Gendlin's name to meet it. They needed not to reduce the hour to eye contact.

## Why a speech clinic still inherits the reel

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. Its live license is not counseling. Its rooms still require a person to hear another person after bad news, after a stalled sound, after a family says the child is "just shy." ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice named counseling as a service-delivery domain about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It did not name Truax. The inheritance is older than the scope PDF: once listening was taught as a technique, every allied-health syllabus had to decide whether to teach it, ignore it, or pretend it was "just being nice."

Rural American clinics inherited the quarrel without inheriting the grant. A speech office in Southeast Arkansas does not have a two-inch deck. It has a conversation after a result. The 1968 paper still sits in that conversation because it made listening *assignable*. Once a skill is assignable, a later scope document can name it. Once a scope document names it, a later deposition can ask whether it was done. The reel's last gift was legal as much as clinical.

Being nice is not a timestamp. The reel was. The quarrel that followed — whether you can atomize listening without killing it — is the rest of this series.
