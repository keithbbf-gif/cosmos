**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1985 W. W. Norton published Steve de Shazer's *Keys to Solution in Brief Therapy*. Milwaukee, not Vienna, not Palo Alto, though both ghosts are in the room. Behind a one-way mirror at the Brief Family Therapy Center, a team had been watching interviews and arguing about what, if anything, had moved. *Clues: Investigating Solutions in Brief Therapy* followed in 1988, the book in which the overnight-miracle hypothetical became a named, portable object. This essay treats those questions as dated objects. It will not print the miracle question as a script a reader could ask in a kitchen.

Insoo Kim Berg, de Shazer's partner and co-founder, belongs in the therapy-history pack as a biography. Here she is the clinician whose work shaped how the Milwaukee questions landed in rooms with children, with protective services, with substance. The modality on this page is the questioning package. The life is next door.

## A storefront, a mirror, 1978

BFTC's own later website said the Center was incorporated in 1978 as a not-for-profit training and research institution that also ran a Wisconsin-certified outpatient clinic. De Shazer (born 25 June 1940, Milwaukee) and Berg are the names the world kept. Teams behind the mirror rotated. Four therapy suites, one-way screens, intercoms, videotape in some rooms: that is the physical plant described in early BFTC papers. The room is the method. Solution-focused questioning is a *watched* hour. An observer who phones in a suggestion is part of the skill's history, even when later brief workshops dropped the team and kept the questions.

The Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto had opened a Brief Therapy Center in 1968. Weakland, Fisch, Watzlawick, and colleagues studied how problems were maintained in interaction and how a short intervention might interrupt the maintaining sequence. De Shazer knew that literature. Milwaukee's later claim, as BFTC told it, was that in 1982 they named "Solution-Focused Brief Therapy" after noticing *exceptions* — times the complaint was not happening — as more useful than a map of the problem's machinery. The naming year is theirs. The 1985 and 1988 Norton books are the objects a historian can put on the table without joining the institute.

Palo Alto asked, in caricature, how the problem worked. Milwaukee asked what was already working. The caricature is too clean. Both shops used the mirror. Both were brief as a boast. Both irritated long-term therapists. The fight that matters for a skills pack is the object of the question: sequence that maintains a problem, or exception that might be built on. Essay 04 in this pack already placed the miracle question among Socratic and circular cousins. This file is the modality that made such questions a whole house.

## Miracle, scaling, exceptions — as objects, not homework

The miracle question, as printed in *Clues* (1988), is a long hypothetical: a night, a miracle that solves the problem, a next day in which the person is asked to notice small signs. Trainers later shortened it until it fit a slide. The printed 1988 version is a historical document. Reproducing the prompt here would be a script. This series does not print runnable prompts. Description without the text a reader could copy is the line the page will hold.

Scaling questions — often a 0-to-10 rating of how close life already is to the preferred picture — are the other portable object. They look like the 1961 Beck inventory's love of numbers. They are not the same instrument. The BDI claimed to measure a syndrome. The Milwaukee scale asked a person to locate themselves relative to a future they had named. A number in 1988 was a conversational move. A number in 1961 was a research score. Confusing the two is how "outcome" talk flattened every scale into one culture of audit.

Exception questions asked when the problem was absent or less. Coping questions asked how a person had gotten through a bad stretch. These are named moves in the SFBT training literature. This series will not list them as a session agenda. It will say that a 1980s Milwaukee team believed the helper's job was to listen for competence and for a future that had already, in pieces, arrived. Humanistic critics heard that as cheerleading. Psychodynamic critics heard it as a flight from pain. Milwaukee heard the critics as people still in love with problems.

Berg's clinical applications — including work that later books and videos placed in child-protection and substance-use settings — showed the questions traveling into systems that had not asked for a family-therapy boutique. The biography of that travel is the sister pack's. The skill fact is that the questions were never only for a middle-class couple behind a Milwaukee mirror.

## Brief as a boast, and the fights it picked

"Brief" is a marketing word and a moral claim. It says: do not keep people in treatment as a professional habit. It also says, sometimes: do not stay with a grief that has no miracle. Luterman's 1979 CSD counseling, in this pack, is a useful dissent without being an enemy. A parent of a newly diagnosed deaf child may not need a miracle hypothetical in the first conversation. They may need the unfilled second. Solution-focused trainers sometimes answered that the preferred future could include grief. The answer is more sophisticated than the slide. Histories should keep both the slide and the sophistication.

Feminist and cultural critics asked whose solutions counted. A miracle that restores a household's old order may be a miracle for one member. Scaling a 7 may be loyalty, fear, or a language difference. BFTC worked with a wide census, on its own account. The later international workshop circuit did not always carry Milwaukee's census. A modality that travels as three questions will be asked those questions about itself.

De Shazer died on 11 September 2005 in Vienna. Berg died in 2007. BFTC's operations wound down with them; training rights later sat with the Solution-Focused Brief Therapy Association and related bodies. Living trainers remain. Public books and public lectures only. No gossip.

## What leaked into every other school

By the 1990s a school counselor, a social worker, and a substance-use counselor might all "do the miracle question" on a Thursday without having read *Keys*. Ivey's later editions and Egan's later editions could absorb a future-oriented question as a stage. Motivational interviewing, two files forward, also lives in ambivalence and in the client's own reasons. The family resemblance is real. The trademarks are different. MI's 1983 paper is a drinkers' document from New Mexico and a Norwegian sabbatical. SFBT's 1985 book is a Milwaukee mirror. Historians who treat all "brief, hopeful questions" as one skill have joined the leak.

Circular questions from the Milan team (*Family Process*, English door 1980) are another cousin. Selvini Palazzoli, Boscolo, Cecchin, and Prata asked about relationships in a pattern that made the system visible. Milwaukee asked about exceptions. Both used the team's ear behind the glass. A master's program that taught them in different weeks produced students who thought they were different centuries. They were different bets about what a question is *for*.

## A speech clinic, no miracle script

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. It does not run a one-way-mirror team. Families still arrive with a preferred future: a child who talks, a meal that is safe, a voice that lasts a teaching day. Hope is not a Milwaukee invention. The 1985–1988 books named a *way of asking* about hope that American helping professions then photocopied.

ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice locates counseling in communication, feeding, and swallowing. An SLP who asks what is already going a little better is not thereby an SFBT practitioner. The question has a history. So does the risk: that a communication impairment will be scaled as a motivation problem. Berg and de Shazer wrote for presenting complaints that were often interactional. A swallow result is not a game. Heritage education can keep the distinction without insulting the questions' genuine hospitality to competence.

Do not take a miracle prompt from this page. There isn't one. Take the dates: 1978 incorporation, 1982 naming as BFTC told it, 1985 *Keys*, 1988 *Clues*, a mirror in Milwaukee, and a partner whose biography is filed next door.
