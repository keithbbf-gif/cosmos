**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1992 the *Journal of Counseling & Development* printed "Multicultural Counseling Competencies and Standards: A Call to the Profession," by Derald Wing Sue, Patricia Arredondo, and Roderick J. McDavis. Thirty-one competencies. Three dimensions that a generation of students would memorize as awareness, knowledge, and skills. The Association for Multicultural Counseling and Development had approved a rationale document in April 1991. The 1992 paper asked the American Association for Counseling and Development — soon to be the American Counseling Association — to treat those competencies as more than a special-interest memo. Curriculum reform, the authors said, should follow.

Sue is living at last verification (2026-09-14). Catalogs and name authorities give 1942 as a birth year (8 August in some public listings). This page uses public books, public papers, and public academic posts. No private life. No health speculation. Microaggressions will appear as a research and professional-history object, including later published disagreement. This is not a social-media round. Portrait: none.

## A textbook that changed its adjective

Wiley issued *Counseling the Culturally Different: Theory and Practice* in 1981, Derald Wing Sue with David Sue, in the Wiley series in counseling and human development. Later editions took the title *Counseling the Culturally Diverse*. The adjective change is itself a small history of the field's English. "Different" from whose center? "Diverse" as a census? The book, through many Wiley editions — Teachers College's faculty page notes a ninth in 2022 with additional co-authors — became the object most counseling students were assigned when a program decided it needed a multicultural text. Frequency of assignment is not a sacrament. It is a fact about syllabi.

Before the book there was a 1982 position paper in *The Counseling Psychologist*: "Cross-Cultural Counseling Competencies," Sue with Joseph E. Bernier, Anna Durran, Lawrence B. Feinberg, Paul Pedersen, Elsie J. Smith, and others. The 1992 AMCD paper did not come from nowhere. C. Gilbert Wrenn's 1962 "culturally encapsulated counselor" had already named the problem in the *Harvard Educational Review*. Sue's contribution was to turn the problem into *standards* a profession could adopt or refuse. The multicultural-competencies essay in this pack is the place for the document as a skill package. This page is the psychologist who kept writing the document's descendants.

He is professor of psychology and education at Teachers College, Columbia University, and was founding president of the Asian American Psychological Association. Those are public posts. Portland, Oregon, appears as a birthplace in some biographical listings; Oregon State and the University of Oregon as degrees. The academic line is counseling psychology, Asian American psychology, racism as a research topic, and, later, racial dialogues as a research topic. Keep it on that line.

## Awareness, knowledge, skills — as a 1992 architecture

The 1992 competencies were organized around the counselor's own worldview and biases, the worldviews of clients, and culturally responsive intervention — the triad that textbooks shortened to awareness, knowledge, and skills. The shortening is how a thirty-one-item professional-standards document becomes a slide. The slide can be taught as a self-congratulation. The 1992 paper was a *call*, which means it assumed the profession had not already arrived. Tervalon and Murray-García's 1998 "cultural humility" paper, treated in this pack as a skill essay, later argued that competence-as-arrival was the wrong metaphor. Sue's competencies and the humility literature are neighbors. They are not the same claim. A history that collapses them into "be culturally sensitive" has already lost the paper trail.

Arredondo and McDavis are co-authors, not assistants. McDavis's University of Arkansas address on the 1992 byline is a reminder that this was not only a coastal conversation. The document asked accreditation to change. CACREP's later standards did change, in waves. Psychiatry's DSM culture, as this pack's guardrails note, sometimes ignored the 1992 paper as "counseling" rather than "real" clinical science. That snobbery is part of the history. So is the counseling profession's own delay.

## Microaggressions, as a published argument

In 2007 Sue and colleagues published "Racial Microaggressions in Everyday Life: Implications for Clinical Practice" in *American Psychologist*. The term had an older psychiatric and later legal-academic life; the 2007 paper is the object that put it on many clinicians' desks. Later books and trainings followed. So did published critique: questions about definition, measurement, replication, and the risk that a clinical idea would become a workplace reporting system without the paper's clinical cautions. Lilienfeld's 2017 critique in *Perspectives on Psychological Science* is one dated example of the scholarly disagreement. Other papers answered him. This series will not officiate a winner. It will not re-stage campus or Twitter fights. It will say that a 2007 APA journal article became a professional-history event, that the event included dissent in other journals, and that neither the article nor the dissent is a protocol for a reader to run on a neighbor.

The clinical implication Sue's 2007 co-authors were after was not a gotcha. It was that brief, often ambiguous slights can shape a helping hour, and that a helper who only tracks "feelings" in a Truax sense may miss the political weather in the room. You can accept that as a research program, dispute the measurement, or both. What this page will not do is treat the argument as a personality contest with Sue as mascot or villain. Living scholars are not public property. The paper is.

## What this page will not do

It will not print a cultural-competence self-test. It will not offer a list of microaggressions as a checklist to deploy. It will not speculate about Sue's family or health. ## A Southeast Arkansas clinic and a 1992 contents page

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. The people in its rooms are not a diversity poster. They are families with particular churches, languages, school systems, and histories with medicine. An SLP who uses counseling skills about communication is not, by ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice, a multicultural counseling specialist in Sue's accreditation sense. The inheritance is still real. Once "the culturally encapsulated counselor" was a 1962 insult, and once 1992 made competencies a standards document, every later allied-health syllabus had to decide whether "rapport" was enough. It was not. The 1981 Wiley book and the 1992 paper are two of the reasons the decision could be named.
