**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In the March–April 1992 issue of the *Journal of Counseling & Development* (volume 70, pages 477–486), Derald Wing Sue, Patricia Arredondo, and Roderick J. McDavis published "Multicultural Counseling Competencies and Standards: A Call to the Profession." Thirty-one competencies. A three-by-three matrix: the counselor's awareness of own assumptions, values, and biases; understanding of the client's worldview; culturally appropriate intervention strategies — each crossed with beliefs and attitudes, knowledge, and skills. The Association for Multicultural Counseling and Development had approved a rationale document in April 1991. The 1992 paper is the object American counselor education could not, after that spring, honestly pretend not to have seen.

This essay treats the competencies as a professional-history document. It will not print them as a self-score quiz. A quiz is a protocol. A dated call to a profession is history.

Living authors. Sue, Arredondo, and colleagues who remain in public work are not public property. Public books, public lectures, public interviews. No health speculation. No scraped headshots. Type, until a licensed portrait exists.

## Wrenn 1962: the counselor in a capsule

C. Gilbert Wrenn published "The Culturally Encapsulated Counselor" in the *Harvard Educational Review* 32 (1962): 444–449. The encapsulated counselor, in Wrenn's argument, is sealed in a white, middle-class, North American taken-for-granted, and then calls the seal "technique." 1962 is early. It is before the 1970s ethnic-counseling literature piled up. It is a guidance-and-personnel voice, not yet the AMCD. This pack keeps it because the 1992 paper did not invent the complaint. It standardized a complaint that Wrenn had already, from inside the profession, named as a defect of the helper rather than as a defect of the client.

The 1960s–1980s produced a shelf: cross-cultural counseling, minority counseling, "culturally different" as a title phrase. Derald Wing Sue's *Counseling the Culturally Different: Theory and Practice* (Wiley, 1981), with chapter contributions in that first edition, is the textbook object that later editions retitled *Counseling the Culturally Diverse*. The title change is a historical event in the mouth of the field: "different" from whom? "Diverse" as a later, broader claim. Cite the edition on the desk, as the bibliography says. This file cites the 1981 first title and the later Wiley name as a living series, not as a shopping list of years.

Paul Pedersen and others argued, in the 1990s, that multiculturalism was a "fourth force" in counseling, after psychodynamic, behavioral, and humanistic. Force-talk is a slogan. It is useful as a slogan of the decade that wanted the 1992 paper to feel like a paradigm. Historians can report the slogan without kneeling to it. Psychodynamic, behavioral, and humanistic work had never been culture-free. They had been culture-blind and had called the blindness science.

## AMCD, AACD, and a standards fight

AMCD — earlier the Association for Non-White Concerns in Personnel and Guidance (1972), renamed in 1985 — is the division that commissioned the competencies work. The 1992 paper asked the American Association for Counseling and Development (AACD, later the American Counseling Association) to take the competencies into accreditation and curriculum. That is a standards fight, not a workshop mood. CACREP (founded 1981) and later ACA ethics documents are the machinery. This essay will not audit an accreditation file. It will say that multicultural counseling, as a modality-like package in this wave, is unusual: it is less a school with a founder-saint than a *profession's attempt to name what every school had omitted*.

McDavis, on the 1992 byline, was then at the University of Arkansas, Fayetteville. The Arkansas fact is checkable and, on a Southeast Arkansas speech site, is not a branding stunt. It is a reminder that the competencies paper was not only a coastal document. Sue's later public career, textbooks, and lectures on racism and microaggressions are neighboring literature. Essay 43 is the figure file. This file keeps the 1992 matrix.

A second printing of the same call appeared in the *Journal of Multicultural Counseling and Development* 20 (1992). Two journals, one spring, a profession being asked to copy itself.

## What the matrix packaged as skills

Beliefs/attitudes, knowledge, and skills — the three columns — are how a 1992 paper translated a moral demand into something a syllabus could grade. "Awareness of own assumptions" is a cousin of cultural humility (essay 16; Tervalon and Murray-García 1998 in medicine) and not the same object. The 1998 paper, in the *Journal of Health Care for the Poor and Underserved*, argued that *competence* as a finished mastery was the wrong metaphor, and that humility as a lifelong stance was better. Counseling borrowed the humility word. Essay 16 is the skill. This essay is the 1992 competencies as a historical *competency* document — the thing humility later criticized.

The fight is real. A matrix can become a checklist a student ticks. A humility stance can become a mood that refuses to learn any particular community's history. 1992 and 1998 need each other as arguments. They do not merge into a poster.

"Culturally appropriate intervention strategies," the third row of the 1992 call, is the dangerous cell. It can mean: stop exporting a white Rogerian hour as universal. It can also be misread as a recipe book ("for group X, do Y"). The paper is a call for standards, not a cookbook. This series will not extract a cookbook. A cookbook would be a protocol dressed as respect.

Feminist counseling (previous essay) asked a related question about gender as structure. Multicultural competencies asked it about race, ethnicity, and, in later expansions, a longer list of identities. The 2015 Multicultural and Social Justice Counseling Competencies (Ratts, Singh, Nassar-McMillan, Butler, and McCullough), adopted in the ACA/AMCD later wave, is a subsequent object. It is not a reason to skip 1992. It is proof that a standards document gets rewritten when the profession admits the first matrix was not the last word.

## What the competencies were accused of

Critics from a "color-blind" stance said the 1992 paper was politics, not science. The accusation is itself a historical object: it treats the encapsulated counselor of 1962 as the default scientist. Other critics, from inside multicultural work, said the competencies still centered the counselor as the one who *acquires* competence about others, a museum model of culture. Still others said "multicultural" in U.S. counseling meant race in a particular American key and did not automatically travel.

Conversion-practice harm, named in this pack's bibliography via Bayer 1981, is in-bounds as harm history. A competencies document that cannot say no to a practice that treats a person's sexuality or gender as a defect has failed its own third row. Name the harm. Do not teach the method.

Ivey's later work with Mary Bradford Ivey and Carlos Zalaquett tried to write culture into microskills as *choice* rather than as a North American attending law. The first 1968 attending paper was less careful. The competencies paper is, among other things, a later profession answering that film.

## A speech clinic, a 2016 scope, no quiz

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. It does not administer the 1992 competencies as a staff test. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names counseling about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not reprint Sue, Arredondo, and McDavis. The inheritance still arrives: whose "good attending" is the SLP using; whose parent is being heard as "noncompliant"; whose dialect of distress matches the intake form.

Essay 16 (cultural humility as a skill) and essay 14 (families around communication difference) are this pack's CSD-facing companions. This file is the counseling profession's 1992 standards event. Heritage education can say that a speech clinic in the Delta sits in a country that produced Wrenn, a 1981 textbook, and a 1992 call, without claiming that the clinic "does multicultural counseling" as a brand. Superlatives about the practice are forbidden here anyway.

Do not score yourself on a 3×3. Take the dates: Wrenn 1962, Sue 1981, AMCD's 1991 rationale, JCD 1992, Tervalon and Murray-García 1998, later MSJCC 2015. A profession that writes competencies is a profession that had to admit a skill was missing. The document is the admission. The quiz is someone else's product.
