**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In May 1998 the *Journal of Health Care for the Poor and Underserved* printed Melanie Tervalon and Jann Murray-García's "Cultural Humility versus Cultural Competence: A Critical Distinction in Defining Physician Training Outcomes in Multicultural Education." It was eight pages, Johns Hopkins University Press, aimed at people who train doctors. Counseling programs photocopied it as if it had been written for them. In a way the theft was fair. The paper refused to let "competence" mean a finite body of knowledge a trainee could master and then stop. Humility, the authors proposed, was a better training *outcome*: a lifelong habit of self-evaluation and self-critique, a continuing effort to redress power imbalances in the patient-physician relationship, and partnerships with communities that were not paternalistic. Those three clauses are the paper's own architecture. They are not a checklist for a reader to run at home.

This essay is the history of humility as a *named skill-claim* in helping work. It is not Derald Wing Sue's biography — that figure essay comes later — and it is not the full modality history of multicultural counseling competencies, which has its own number in this pack. It is the quarrel between two English nouns, competence and humility, and the older 1962 warning that counselors live inside cocoons.

## Wrenn, 1962: the cocoon as a professional device

C. Gilbert Wrenn's "The Culturally Encapsulated Counselor" appeared in the *Harvard Educational Review* in 1962. Wrenn was a guidance figure, writing when APGA (born 1952) still had "personnel and guidance" in its bones. His warning was that counselors protect themselves from the disturbance of change by wrapping themselves in a pretended reality built from the past and the known. The encapsulation was not only ignorance of "other cultures" as a census category. It was a time-capsule: techniques and values suited to a world that had already moved.

That is a skill claim in negative. The counselor's failure is not missing a fact about a holiday. It is the refusal to let the present revise the method. Wrenn's essay is short, dated, and still assignable because the cocoon is an occupational hazard of any profession that trains people in a three-year window and then lets them practice for forty. Encapsulation is not cured by a module.

When later writers say Wrenn "anticipated" multicultural counseling, they are doing ancestral work. He did not write the 1992 competencies. He named a way of not seeing. The 1992 document would try to name the seeing.

## Sue, Arredondo, McDavis, 1992: competence as a standard

In March–April 1992 the *Journal of Counseling & Development* published Derald Wing Sue, Patricia Arredondo, and Roderick J. McDavis, "Multicultural Counseling Competencies and Standards: A Call to the Profession." The Association for Multicultural Counseling and Development had approved a rationale in April 1991. The Professional Standards committee went further: thirty-one competencies, pressed on AACD (soon to be ACA) as material for accreditation and curriculum. The paper is a political document as much as a scientific one. It said the profession's center had been white, and that "competence" without a multicultural account was a local dialect pretending to be a language.

The 1992 grid — beliefs and attitudes, knowledge, skills, crossed with counselor awareness of own assumptions, understanding of the client's worldview, and developing appropriate interventions — became the teaching machine. Later editions of Sue and Sue's *Counseling the Culturally Diverse* carried the argument into textbooks. ACA ethical revisions took in multicultural language. Counselor education could, after 1992, fail a student on a competency that had not existed as a numbered list in 1962.

The list's power is also its danger. A competency statement can be studied for a quiz. Tervalon and Murray-García's 1998 objection to "competence" in medical education was aimed at exactly that: detached mastery of a theoretically finite body of knowledge. Physicians who had attended a lecture on "the Hispanic patient" could believe they were done. Counselors who had memorized thirty-one items could believe the same. The 1992 authors were not asking for a quiz. They were asking for a standard that would change who got to be called competent. History has to hold both: the list as a necessary institutional weapon, and the list as a thing that can be gamed.

Roderick J. McDavis, it is worth saying for a Southeast Arkansas reader, had been at the University of Arkansas at Fayetteville. The 1992 paper is not a coastal object that arrived in the Delta by mail. One of its authors had already been in-state. That is geography, not a claim that Arkansas owned the movement.

## 1998: humility as a different kind of outcome

Tervalon and Murray-García were writing about house staff and medical students, about power in the exam room, and about partnerships with defined populations. Cultural humility, in their usage, is not shyness. It is a refusal to treat the Other as a chapter. Self-critique is the engine. Power is named. Community partnership is not a service-learning decoration.

Counseling's adoption of the paper produced a slogan fight that still bores and still matters. Some programs kept "multicultural competence" as the brand and added "humility" as a virtue word on the same slide. Some treated humility as a replacement that made the 1992 list look arrogant. Some, more carefully, said competence without humility becomes a trophy, and humility without knowledge becomes a mood. This series will not issue a winner. The historical fact is that 1962, 1992, and 1998 are three different objects: a warning about encapsulation, a competency standard, and a medical-education outcome that rejected finite mastery.

What humility-as-skill meant in supervision was awkward to measure. Truax could rate a tape for accurate empathy. MITI could count reflections. Humility resists the slice. A trainee can perform ignorance as a pose. A trainee can perform knowledge as a shield. Supervisors who tried to grade "humility" sometimes graded manners. Tervalon and Murray-García had asked for a lifelong commitment, which is a terrible rubric for a semester. The mismatch is the point. A skill that is a life habit will always sit badly in a course that must issue a grade.

This pack will not print a humility inventory, a self-audit of privilege, or a list of self-quiz items. Those documents exist in abundance. They are the DIY this series refuses. The 1998 paper is the dated alternative to that DIY: an argument about training *outcomes* in an institution, not a consumer exercise.

## CSD, dialect, and the borrowed nouns

Speech-language pathology met the same nouns with a delay and a twist. "Cultural competence" entered ASHA documents and continuing education as a professional-practice expectation. "Cultural humility" followed, often as a slide that cited 1998 without the medical-education context. CSD's own fights — dialect as difference rather than disorder, interpreters in assessment, which language a family is asked to abandon — are the SLPWOW pack's institutional story. This lane's claim is smaller. When an SLP "counsels" a family about a communication difference, the 1962 cocoon, the 1992 list, and the 1998 humility paper are all in the room if the clinician has been to a workshop. They are also in the room if the clinician has not, as unnamed habits.

A competency approach that treats "the Arkansas client" or "the Black family" as a type repeats the finite-knowledge error Tervalon and Murray-García disliked. A humility approach that treats every family as so unique that history, racism, and language policy vanish is a mood. CSD assessment already knows that tests can be racially discriminatory in administration; Part C regulations say so in their own register. Counseling skills that ignore that fact are Wrenn's cocoon with a kind face.

Eye contact as "good attending," already criticized in the listening and microskills essays, is the classroom version of encapsulation. Ivey's later editions tried to write attending as a choice. The first films were less careful. Humility, as a 1998 outcome, would ask who built the film. It would not ask the family to sit for a demonstration.

## Living authors, local practice, no trophy

Sue, Arredondo, Tervalon, and Murray-García were alive at last verification (2026-09-14). Public books and public papers only. No gossip. No health claims. Wrenn died in 2001; past tense.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. These pages do not claim the clinic holds a multicultural-competency credential, teaches humility workshops, or practices a counseling specialty. They claim that the words on a well-meaning website — "we respect all cultures" — have a paper trail, and that the trail is more interesting than the slogan. 1962: a cocoon. 1992: a numbered standard that tried to change a profession's center of gravity. 1998: a short medical paper that told trainers to stop treating culture as a chapter you finish. A small-city speech clinic that serves families across race, language, and county lines inherits the quarrel every time a form asks for "preferred language" and a clinician decides how much of the hour to spend on the form.

The sister essay on multicultural counseling competencies will stay with the 1992 grid as a modality package. This one stays with humility as a skill-claim that was invented to keep competence from becoming a trophy. A trophy is a finite object. A speech clinic's actual hours are not.
