**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In March 1980, *Family Process* opened its nineteenth volume with a paper whose title was three nouns joined by dashes: "Hypothesizing — Circularity — Neutrality: Three Guidelines for the Conductor of the Session." The authors were Mara Selvini Palazzoli, Luigi Boscolo, Gianfranco Cecchin, and Giuliana Prata, writing from Milan. English-language family therapists had already met the team's *Paradox and Counterparadox* (1978). The 1980 essay was something colder and more operational. It treated the interview itself as a research instrument. A question was no longer a polite way to fill an hour. It was a move that produced "meaningful information," the phrase the abstract used, without which the conductor could not choose a next act.

This essay is not a manual of those moves. It will not print prompts a reader could run as a session. It is a history of how counseling, family therapy, brief therapy, and later cognitive work *named* the question — Socratic, circular, miracle, open, closed, scaling — and then fought over whether the name was a method or a costume.

## The Athenian loan

Socrates did not invent the intake form. He is a character in Plato's dialogues, a civic irritant who refutes, leads, and sometimes humiliates. Twentieth-century clinicians borrowed the adjective anyway. By the time cognitive therapy had classrooms, "Socratic questioning" was a unit on a syllabus: a way to sound Greek while steering a belief. The branding did work that the dialogues never asked for. It made interrogation feel like philosophy.

Christine A. Padesky's keynote in London on 24 September 1993 — "Socratic Questioning: Changing Minds or Guiding Discovery?" — is a dated protest against that costume. Delivered at a European behavioural and cognitive congress, it argued that many cognitive therapists had been using the Athenian name to mean persuasion: get the other person to concede. Padesky wanted guided discovery instead, a sequence in which questions, listening, and summaries moved from what a person already knew toward a reevaluation they could own. The quarrel is historical. It is also a warning about labels. Once a clinic calls a habit Socratic, the habit inherits a prestige it has not earned and a method it may not be using.

Clara E. Hill's *Helping Skills* (American Psychological Association, first edition 1999; later editions 2004, 2009, 2014, 2020) did a different, plainer job. It sorted questions by form — open, closed — and by the stage of a helping model she called exploration, insight, and action. Open questions, in that taxonomy, invite a narrative; closed questions pin a fact. The table is a teaching machine, kin to Ivey's lists and Carkhuff's levels. It does not prove that a conversation has three acts. It proves that American counselor education kept needing a table of contents for the interrogative mood. Hill is a living writer; this pack uses the public book, not a private life.

William R. Miller's 1983 paper in *Behavioural Psychotherapy*, "Motivational Interviewing with Problem Drinkers," belongs on the same shelf for a different reason. American addiction counseling of that decade still carried a confrontational inheritance: the helper's job was to break denial. Miller treated open questions, among other moves, as an alternative to that pugilism. The later OARS grammar — open questions as the first letter — has its own essay in this pack. The 1983 claim is older and narrower. A question can be a refusal to prosecute.

None of these Anglo-American namings is the Milan paper. They share a century with it. They do not share a theory of the family.

## Four names, a one-way mirror, a split

The Milan team worked behind glass. Hypotheses were formed before the session, revised in the room, and argued in the corridor. Circularity, in the 1980 definition, was not a metaphor for "everything is connected." It was a way of interviewing so that each question was informed by the last answer and aimed at difference, relationship, and sequence rather than at a linear cause. Neutrality was the conductor's refusal to take a permanent side. American master's students later met a reduced version: ask one family member to comment on the relationship between two others. The reduction traveled because it was teachable. The 1980 paper was doing something more expensive. It was trying to keep the therapist from becoming an extra relative.

The team did not stay one team. After 1980 Selvini Palazzoli and Prata leaned into research on invariant prescriptions; Boscolo and Cecchin became the traveling teachers of the interview. Gianfranco Cecchin's 1987 *Family Process* essay, "Hypothesizing, Circularity, and Neutrality Revisited: An Invitation to Curiosity," is a dated revision from the training side. Neutrality, he argued, had been heard as ice. Curiosity was a warmer word for the same discipline: do not marry your hypothesis. The institutional fight is easy to flatten into personalities. It was also a fight about what a question is *for*. Research questions hunt confirmation. Clinical questions, in the Boscolo–Cecchin reconstruction, try to keep more than one description alive in the room.

English uptake had its own authors. Peggy Penn's "Circular Questioning" (*Family Process*, 1982) and Karl Tomm's later "Interventive Interviewing" papers (1987–1988, also *Family Process*) translated the Milan interview into North American supervision language. Tomm's distinction — some questions gather information, some intervene by the asking — made the ethics of curiosity visible. If a question changes the system that answers it, then "just asking" is already an act. Counselor-education programs that taught "open questions" in week three and "family systems" in another semester often missed that the same mouth was doing both jobs.

## Milwaukee, keys, clues, a word that stuck

Steve de Shazer's *Keys to Solution in Brief Therapy* (W. W. Norton, 1985) is the English door to a Milwaukee turn. The Brief Family Therapy Center had been watching families through a one-way mirror, as MRI in Palo Alto had, and as Milan had. *Keys* argued that the lock was the complaint and that a therapist might try skeleton keys — interventions that did not require a full map of the problem's origin. John Weakland, in a jacket comment that is public and checkable, linked the book to both Erickson and to Selvini Palazzoli's recent work. The kinship is the history. Milwaukee was not Athens. It was a Midwestern room with glass, a team, and a bet that solutions had a different grammar from problems.

*Clues: Investigating Solutions in Brief Therapy* (Norton, 1988) is where general readers of this series should date the miracle question as a printed object. Later teaching lore, including De Jong and Berg's textbooks, traces the prompt to a 1984 session in which Insoo Kim Berg took up a client's own word *miracle* and asked what would be different if the overnight change had already happened. This pack will not reprint the prompt. The historical claim is smaller and safer. A future-oriented hypothetical, tried in Milwaukee in the mid-1980s and bound in *Clues*, asked for the first *visible signs* of a change imagined as already complete. Those signs were then treated as present-tense information: exceptions, next steps, a description of a Tuesday that did not yet exist. The device is famous because it is easy to parody. The parody is part of the record. So is the team's insistence that they were investigating solutions rather than excavating causes.

Scaling questions, later a signature of solution-focused workshops, belong to the same Milwaukee grammar. A number was invited into the conversation as a conversational object, not as a psychometric score. *Words Were Originally Magic* (1994) would make the practice even more portable. The earlier books already assumed that a person could locate a complaint, a hope, or a difference along a line and then talk about movement. This series will not print a scale for a reader to fill. The dated fact is that a clinic in Milwaukee taught numbers as questions.

## What the names hide

A closed question in Hill's taxonomy and a circular question in Milan are not cousins except in punctuation. One seeks a fact the helper has already imagined. The other tries to make a relationship speak. An "open question" in motivational interviewing is a refusal of the courtroom. A "Socratic" question in a clumsy cognitive hour can be a courtroom with better lighting. The miracle hypothetical is not open in Hill's sense; it is tightly designed. Trainers who lined these objects up as a buffet of "questioning skills" produced a generation that could name four brands and still not know which century they were in.

There is an older American anxiety under the brands. Counselor education after Parsons (Bureau 1908; *Choosing a Vocation*, 1909) already used questions as a file: work history, school record, a card. The guidance interview was an inventory. Rogers's recordings, treated in the listening and reflection essays of this pack, made the helper's *statements* the thing to count, and left questions slightly suspect — as if asking were a failure of empathy. Ivey's microcounseling, from the 1968 APGA paper through *Microcounseling* (Charles C. Thomas, 1971), put questions back on the skill list as units a beginner could practice. Hill put them in a stage model. The Milan paper and the Milwaukee books never agreed to that atomization. For them a question was a move in a system, or a key in a lock, not a mouth behavior on a checklist.

Cross-cultural critics, including writers in the multicultural-competencies lineage that this pack takes up later, pointed out that the American open question can feel like surveillance. A family that answers a stranger's curiosity with delay is not necessarily "resistant." A question that asks one sibling to rank another, a classic circular device in supervision tapes, can be an insult in a household that does not discuss hierarchy with outsiders. The 1980 paper's "neutrality" does not survive that critique unchanged. Curiosity has a politics. So does the miracle hypothetical, which assumes that a person is free to imagine an overnight change without naming the landlord, the border, or the diagnosis that will still be there in the morning. Solution-focused trainers later wrote about constraints. The first English books were quicker to celebrate the skeleton key.

## Speech rooms, and what this series refuses

A speech-language pathologist asks questions all day: about a swallow, a sound, a school day, a family's theory of why a child is "just shy." Those questions are clinical data collection under a license that ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names as including counseling *about communication, feeding, and swallowing*. They are not Milan circularity. They are not a miracle prompt. Collapsing them into "counseling questions" would mint a second license the scope document does not grant. The useful inheritance is more modest. Once the helping professions treated questions as named skills, every allied-health syllabus had to decide whether its questions were an inventory, a conversation, or a disguised lecture.

This series will not teach you which. It will say: Socrates is later branding; the 1980 *Family Process* paper is a checkable object; *Keys* (1985) and *Clues* (1988) are the Milwaukee dates for a solution-focused questioning culture, including the miracle hypothetical as a printed device and scaling as a numbered conversation. Miller 1983 and Hill 1999 are American taxonomies with different enemies — confrontation in addiction work, chaos in a skills classroom. The names traveled because they could be workshopped. The fights traveled because a question is never only a question. It is a theory of what the next sentence is allowed to be.
