**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1936 Saul Rosenzweig published "Some Implicit Common Factors in Diverse Methods of Psychotherapy" in the *American Journal of Orthopsychiatry* — four pages that borrowed Lewis Carroll's Dodo from *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* (1865) and left a joke later meta-analysts had to take seriously. After a caucus-race that nobody had measured, Carroll's Dodo declared that everybody had won and all must have prizes. Rosenzweig used the bird to name a suspicion: if unlike methods help, perhaps they share unnamed ingredients. He did not run a trial. He did not prove a law. He wrote a short paper in a year when American psychotherapy was already a quarrel of schools. The bird became a verdict only when later people needed a cartoon for a statistical argument.

This essay is that argument's history, not a shopping guide to "what really works." The Dodo bird verdict did not prove all therapies equal. Rosenzweig was a joke with a thesis. Later meta-analyses are an argument. They are not a settled statute. A speech clinic that hears "it's all the relationship" and throws away technique has misunderstood the bird as badly as a clinic that hears "technique is everything" and throws away the hour.

John Tenniel's 1865 illustrations of the Dodo are in the public domain. A later plate may use one. It would still be Carroll, not clinical evidence.

## Four pages and a borrowed bird

Rosenzweig listed implicit common factors as a way to think across methods that advertised themselves as unique. The 1936 paper is brief enough to assign in a sitting and still be misquoted by dinner. What it did not do is compare outcome numbers. American orthopsychiatry in the 1930s was a meeting ground of psychiatry, psychology, and social work. Schools were loud. A four-page suggestion that the loudness might be about branding was easy to file as a witticism. It waited.

The waiting is part of the history. A field that is busy founding institutes does not want a bird that says the institutes are running the same race. Behavior therapy, client-centered work, and the remaining analytic empires of the postwar decades each had reasons to insist on specific ingredients. The Dodo returned when someone counted.

## Frank, 1961: healing as a social form

Jerome D. Frank's *Persuasion and Healing* (Johns Hopkins Press, 1961; later editions with Julia Frank) treated psychotherapy as one of a family of healing practices. Frank described features that recur: a socially sanctioned healer, a distressed person, a place set apart, a rationale or myth that makes the trouble intelligible, and a ritual that requires the person's participation. Hope — expectancy — was not a contaminant. It was part of the form.

Frank is easy to vulgarize into "it's all placebo." He was more interesting than that. A ritual without a rationale is a gesture. A rationale no one believes is a lecture. The 1961 book is a historical object for common-factors talk because it gave the "nonspecific" a structure. Counselors who later said "relationship, expectancy, ritual" were often speaking Frank without naming him. The book is not a protocol for a speech-language pathologist. It is a 1961 argument that healing talk has a sociology.

Frank does not cancel specific techniques. A swallow study is not a myth. A reflection of feeling is not a nightclub séance. The useful pressure of the book, for this pack, is on people who sell a skill as if the rest of the hour were empty. OARS, microskills, accurate empathy scales: all of them can become the "ritual" Frank named. They can also be, on their better days, ways of not abandoning the other person. Common-factors history is the record of that double use.

## Luborsky, 1975: a box score with a Carroll title

Lester Luborsky, Barton Singer, and Lise Luborsky's "Comparative Studies of Psychotherapies: Is It True That 'Everyone Has Won and All Must Have Prizes'?" appeared in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 1975. The title put Carroll in the medical literature. The method was a review of comparative studies that met some standard of control, scored for quality, and then boxed: which treatment won, which lost, which tied. Group versus individual, time-limited versus unlimited, client-centered versus other traditional therapies, behavior therapy versus psychotherapy: the pairs of the era. The impression the paper left, fairly or not, was of many ties.

1975 is the year the Dodo stopped being only a joke. Critics said the box score mixed unlike patients, unlike doses, unlike measures. Supporters said the burden had shifted: if your school claims unique potency, show it against a bona fide other therapy, not against a waiting list. Both sentences are still in circulation. The Luborsky paper is the dated event that made them circulate in psychiatry's own journal.

Smith and Glass's 1977 meta-analysis in *American Psychologist*, and Smith, Glass, and Miller's 1980 *The Benefits of Psychotherapy*, belong on the same shelf as Rosenzweig, Frank, Luborsky, Wampold, and Lambert. The 1970s were when outcome research acquired a statistical machine. The bird sat on the machine.

## Lambert's pie and the poster problem

Michael J. Lambert, in a 1992 chapter and in later popularizations (including Asay and Lambert 1999 and pieces that became workshop posters), grouped outcome variance into four crude shares: extratherapeutic change around forty percent, common factors around thirty, expectancy around fifteen, techniques around fifteen. The numbers are estimates from a reading of a literature, not a single meta-analytic law. Lambert himself has been clearer about that than some of the posters.

The pie became a training-culture object. Students learned that "the relationship" was the large slice they could influence, after the even larger slice they could not (the client's life). Technique shrank to a sliver. Schools that had sold unique techniques heard an insult. Schools that had sold "just being with" heard a vindication. Both hearings overfit a heuristic. A pie chart is a teaching cartoon. It is not a reason to stop learning how to reflect, or how to refrain from advice, or how to know when an SLP should refer.

Hubble, Duncan, and Miller's *The Heart and Soul of Change* (1999) carried Lambert's grouping into a common-factors movement that sometimes talked as if specific treatments were a managed-care distraction. Meanwhile APA Division 12's mid-1990s lists of empirically supported treatments talked as if specific treatments were the only honest talk. Wampold's books are, among other things, a long argument with that list-making.

## Wampold, 2001 and 2015: a debate with a title

Bruce E. Wampold's *The Great Psychotherapy Debate* (Erlbaum, 2001; second edition 2015 with Zac E. Imel) set a medical model of psychotherapy (specific ingredients for specific disorders) against a contextual model (the common factors of a healing practice, including alliance, expectancy, and a coherent method the therapist believes). Wampold's meta-analytic claim, in the form students remember, is that differences among bona fide therapies are small relative to the difference between therapy and no therapy, and that therapist effects can outweigh treatment labels. The book is an argument. Opponents — Chambless among the EST defenders — answered with their own reviews. Specific treatments for some anxiety presentations, to take a fight Wampold does not simply erase, remain a live research quarrel. This series will not declare a winner in a sentence that a clinic could paste onto a service page.

What Wampold did for *skill* history is to make the hour's shared features look like the thing worth training, while still requiring that the therapist have a coherent method. A method can be a common factor. That sentence surprises people who think "common factors" means "no method." Bordin's 1979 working alliance — goals, tasks, bond — is one way the method gets into the relationship. This pack already gave Bordin a number. The Dodo essay has to mention him because alliance research is how the bird got a questionnaire (Horvath and Greenberg's Working Alliance Inventory, 1989; later meta-analyses by Flückiger and colleagues as historical objects).

## Why a speech clinic should not mishear the bird

WOW Therapies does not, on the strength of a Dodo cartoon, practice "all therapies" or none. It is a speech-language practice. Common-factors talk enters a CSD room as a temptation and as a caution. The temptation: because relationship matters in psychotherapy outcome literatures, an SLP might think niceness is the treatment. The caution: because relationship matters, an SLP who only dumps information (Luterman's old enemy) may fail even at the informational job. Neither sentence makes ASHA's 2016 domain into an LPC.

The bird's honest use, in this lane, is modest. When counseling skills were atomized — attending, reflection, OARS, scales — someone had to ask whether the atoms were the help or whether the help was a smaller set of human events that many atoms try to name. Rosenzweig asked in 1936. Frank gave the events a sociology in 1961. Luborsky counted in 1975. Lambert drew a pie that escaped the paper. Wampold wrote a debate that still has two sides. A reader who leaves this page saying "all counseling is equal, so never mind the rest of the series" has joined the cartoon. A reader who leaves saying "only my school's technique counts" has joined the other cartoon. The series will keep naming skills and packages. The bird is the argument that those names might be, in part, dialects of a few older forms: a relationship, a reason to hope, a ritual, a life that was already changing before anyone billed an hour.

That last slice — the client's life, Lambert's largest number — is the one a small-city speech clinic already knew without a pie. A child's language, a stroke, a family's miles to Little Rock: extratherapeutic is a cold word for those. History can still use it, as long as it does not pretend the number was a law.
