**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1912 Sigmund Freud published "Recommendations to Physicians Practising Psycho-Analysis," a technical paper that told the doctor to be opaque, "like a mirror," showing the patient nothing but what was shown to him. The sentence is in the Standard Edition. It is not a poem about quiet. It is a rule about influence. Three years later, in "Observations on Transference-Love" (1915), he stated the treatment must be carried out in abstinence: the physician was not to supply the gratification the transference demanded. Later teachers folded mirror, abstinence, and the blank screen into one atmosphere. Silence became the weather of that atmosphere. Freud had not written a stopwatch manual. He had written a prohibition. The unfilled second entered the profession as a side effect of a moral-technical rule, then acquired theories of its own.

This essay will not teach a reader how to "use silence." It will not time a pause for a living room. It is a history of how an absence of speech became a *skill claim* — named, praised, feared, misread across cultures, and, in communication-disorders rooms, frequently mistaken for a psychological event when it was a motor one.

## Abstinence is not a mute button

Early Freud talked. The *Studies on Hysteria* (1895) still show a physician who questions, presses, and hypothesizes. The later technical papers try to subtract that pressure. Evenly hovering attention, the 1912 recommendations, the 1915 abstinence rule: a cluster of instructions whose shared enemy is the doctor's helpfulness. Helpfulness, in that frame, contaminates the field. The patient must be left with longing that can be analyzed rather than soothed away.

Silence, in the first generations of analytic literature, was therefore rarely celebrated as a gift to the speaker. It was more often read as a symptom. Sándor Ferenczi wrote in 1916 about the silent patient as a problem for the fundamental rule, which demanded speech. Later papers treated the mute stretch as resistance, aggression, or a transferential test: who will break first? Edward Glover and others argued, across decades, about whether the analyst's own quiet was technique or counter-resistance. The quarrel is the history. A profession that had advertised itself as a talking cure had to explain the minutes when nobody talked.

Theodore Reik's *Listening with the Third Ear* (1948) belongs here as a popular English bridge. Reik made the analyst's waiting sound like an art of noticing. American readers who never entered an institute still inherited the glamour of that wait: the wise person who does not rush. Glamour is not a method. It is how a technical abstinence leaked into ordinary advice columns.

## The reel hears a different quiet

Carl Rogers, at Ohio State in 1940 and then in the Chicago recordings that *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951) made into argument, put a machine in the room. The machine did not care about abstinence. It cared about what could be counted. On those tapes the helper's speech is often small. The silences are not the mirror's refusal. They are what happens when the counselor has decided not to steer. A later rater, working with Truax's scales in the 1960s, might hear the same unfilled second as evidence that the helper was tracking — or as dead air that failed to communicate the 1957 conditions.

The two silences share a clock and not a theory. Analytic quiet withholds in order to let transference bloom. Rogerian quiet (if one may use the adjective without reprinting the life, which sits in the therapy-history pack) tries not to add a second self to the speaker's sentence. Supervisors who told students to "just wait" without saying *which* wait they meant produced a hybrid that satisfied neither church. The student sat there, virtuous and lost. The tape recorded the virtue. It could not record the theory.

Allen Ivey's group, filming at Colorado State in 1967 and publishing through the April 1968 APGA paper and *Microcounseling* (Charles C. Thomas, 1971), named a related object: pacing. Attending behavior included vocal tempo and the matching of a speaker's speed, as well as the more famous eye contact and body orientation. Pacing, in that usage, is not the later commercial "pacing and leading" of workshop NLP. It is a training word for the helper's clock. A beginner who fires questions into every gap has a pacing problem that Ivey's units could point at. The pointing is pedagogical. It is not a law of human time.

Researchers later tried to measure pause length as if the second itself were an intervention. Some found that counselors who allowed longer gaps heard more. Some found that clients experienced long gaps as abandonment. The findings never settled, because the unfilled second is not one thing. It is a hole whose meaning is supplied by the relationship, the culture, the diagnosis on the chart, and whether the person in the other chair can physically produce the next sound.

## Quiet is not a universal dialect

North American counselor education often treated a short pause as "comfortable silence" and a long one as something to interpret. The comfort was local. Japanese clinical and anthropological writing on *ma* — an interval that can be a fullness rather than an emptiness — did not travel into most master's courses as theory. It arrived, if at all, as a multicultural footnote: "some clients may not maintain eye contact; some cultures use silence differently." The footnote was better than nothing. It was still a tourist's caption.

Derald Wing Sue's public textbooks, *Counseling the Culturally Diverse* in its later Wiley editions, and the 1992 competencies paper with Patricia Arredondo and Roderick J. McDavis, insisted that communication style is not a personality defect. A pause that a White midwestern intern reads as shutdown may be respect, disagreement, or a wait for the eldest person in the room to speak. Finnish and other Northern European ordinary conversation, often described in intercultural research as tolerant of longer gaps, makes the American intern's anxiety look like the variable. Indigenous writers, including work in the lineage of Eduardo Duran's historical-trauma arguments that later Ivey editions cited, have described Western urgency as the intrusion. This pack will not flatten those arguments into a poster that says "Group X likes silence." The historical claim is that the skill literature was late to notice it had been timing a White, urban, English-speaking second.

Class and gender sit in the same interval. A woman who has been punished for taking up time may fill every gap because emptiness has been unsafe. A man trained to hold the floor may experience another person's pause as incompetence. Pastoral counseling, which older American guidance borrowed from without always naming, had its own silences: prayer, waiting with the bereaved, the hospital chair. Those silences were not "microskills." They were liturgies. When counselor education secularized the wait, it kept the posture and lost the theology, then wondered why the posture felt empty.

## When the second is a body, not a resistance

Communication-disorders rooms break the psychoanalytic and Rogerian readings at once. A person who stutters may be in a block. A person with aphasia may be in a word search. A child with apraxia of speech may be assembling a motor plan that does not arrive on the intern's preferred beat. The unfilled second is then labor. Interpreting it as withholding is a category error with a long professional history. Speech correction, as the SLPWOW pack tells it, spent decades teaching clinicians to fill that second with cues, models, and "help." Counseling skills, imported later, sometimes taught them to leave it alone. Both instructions can be wrong for the same child on the same afternoon, which is why this series refuses a how-to.

David Luterman's *Counseling Parents of Hearing-Impaired Children* (Little, Brown, 1979) is a dated insistence that the professional's job, some days, is to sit with a family's rearrangement of a life. The sitting includes quiet. It is not analytic abstinence. The parent is not a transference engine. The quiet is closer to not interrupting a grief with a pamphlet. Audrey L. Holland, who died in 2023, and Ryan L. Nelson's *Counseling in Communication Disorders: A Wellness Perspective* (Plural, 2007; later editions 2014 and a third that should be verified on upload) kept a humanistic inheritance while changing the assumption about who could produce tidy talk on demand. Holland's aphasia work had already shown that a pause might be the language system working. A rater trained on Truax slices of fluent undergraduates was not built for that room.

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. Its license is not counseling. Its hours still contain unfilled seconds after a swallow result, after a stalled sound, after a family says the school called again. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names counseling as a service-delivery domain about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not name Freud's mirror and does not name Ivey's pacing. The inheritance arrives anyway, as a temptation to read every gap as psychology. In a CSD clinic the first historical question is not "what does this silence mean?" It is "is this person trying to talk?"

## Pacing as a professional clock, not a trick

Once attending was filmable, tempo became a correction. Supervisors told students they were "too fast," which usually meant they were frightened. Medical interviewing, later, developed its own literature on interruption: physicians who cut off a patient's opening statement in seconds. Those studies belong to a different profession's shame. They share a clock with Ivey's beginners. The unfilled second, in that literature, is simply the time the speaker would have used if the professional had not been so efficient.

Efficiency is the American tell. Guidance after Parsons filled cards. Managed care later filled units. A pause that cannot be billed looks like waste. Analytic institutes could afford waste because the method required it; or they told themselves they could. Community clinics, school counseling offices, and rural speech rooms could not. The skill literature of the 1960s–1970s tried to split the difference: teach a countable wait that still looked productive on a tape. That is a historical compromise, not a discovery about the soul.

Cross-professional borrowing made the compromise messier. Nursing textbooks taught "therapeutic silence." Chaplaincy taught presence. Motivational interviewing later coded "silence" only indirectly, as the space in which change talk might appear if the helper did not rush to fill it with advice. Each guild timed a different virtue. A general reader who meets all of them on the internet meets a mush: silence heals, silence is violence, silence is respect, silence is resistance. The mush is what happens when a dated technical object loses its date.

## What the history will not do

It will not tell you to count to ten. It will not tell you that waiting is always kinder than speaking. Analytic abstinence was a theory of drive and transference, written in 1912 and 1915 for physicians who were being warned against helpfulness. Rogers's tapes made a different quiet audible, a quiet that was a refusal to add content. Ivey's 1968–1971 training culture made tempo a beginner skill. Cross-cultural and CSD arguments then showed that the second on the clock is not a universal psychological organ. Sometimes it is a motor struggle. Sometimes it is a language of respect. Sometimes it is a frightened intern performing a method they could not name.

The unfilled second is real. The meaning is not portable. A speech clinic in a small city inherits the second every afternoon. It does not inherit a right to interpret it. That is the whole technical lesson, and it is not a technique.
