**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Audrey L. Holland is easy to lose in a card catalog. John L. Holland wrote *Making Vocational Choices* (first edition 1973) and the RIASEC codes that still haunt career corners. Audrey L. Holland wrote tests and books for people living with aphasia. They do not share a life, a campus, or a counseling claim. This series will not mix them again.

She died in Tucson, Arizona, in August 2023. The University of Arizona's Department of Speech, Language, and Hearing Sciences published a memorial. A public memorial listing gives 4 August 2023 and a birth date of 21 May 1933. The series index still carries 1927–2023. Treat 1933 as the working public date and mark the conflict [VERIFY] on upload week. Write her in the past tense. Do not scrape a university headshot for a featured image; there is no clean Commons portrait in this pack.

## Pittsburgh, Emerson, Arizona

She earned her undergraduate, master's, and Ph.D. degrees at the University of Pittsburgh, the campus later memorials called her alma mater without embarrassment. She taught at Emerson College in Boston, then returned to Pittsburgh: Department of Speech, then appointments that reached the Faculty of Arts and Sciences and the Departments of Psychiatry and Otolaryngology in the School of Medicine. In 1991 she joined the University of Arizona in Tucson as professor and department head in Speech, Language, and Hearing Sciences. She retired as Regents' Professor. Arizona's faculty listing associates her Tucson years with 1991–2002 as a core appointment; she remained a presence in the field after the administrative titles ended. A 2009 Boston University lecture already called her professor emerita.

She held the CCC-SLP and board certification from the Academy of Neurologic Communication Disorders and Sciences. Public honors include the Honors of the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (1990), ANCDS's Clinical Achievement Award, and a Professional Achievement Award from the Council of Graduate Programs in Communication Sciences and Disorders. She served on the Advisory Council of the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders and on a Department of Veterans Affairs advisory committee. Those are institutional facts. They locate a researcher who did not leave the clinic.

## Functional communication as a test, 1980

*CADL: Communicative Abilities in Daily Living* (University Park Press, 1980) tried to measure how an adult with aphasia got along in ordinary encounters, using residual skill rather than a clinic's favorite confrontation-naming list. Later revisions followed. The test is a speech-science object as much as a counseling one. It belongs in this figure essay because it shows what Holland thought *counted*. Getting along in a pharmacy line is a life. A score that cannot see that life is a partial instrument.

Her publication list — memorials say on the order of 145 to 200 articles, chapters, books, and tests, the counts vary by who is counting — stayed close to language disorders of children and adults, with adult aphasia as the center of gravity. She kept seeing people in clinic while she wrote. That is a professional fact repeated in the Arizona notice, not a saint's life.

## A counseling book with a wellness subtitle

With Ryan L. Nelson she wrote *Counseling in Communication Disorders: A Wellness Perspective* (Plural). First edition 2007. Second edition 2014. Third edition 2018 in library records (ISBN 978-1-63550-045-5; some catalogs show a 2020 printing). [VERIFY the third-edition year against the title page on upload.] The Library of Congress catalog record for the first edition is dated 2007. The subtitle is the argument. Counseling, in this book, was not only the management of a deficit. It was a wellness frame, later in open conversation with positive psychology, coaching language, and the question of how a person with chronic aphasia gets on with a life.

Her 2007 paper "Counseling/coaching in chronic aphasia: getting on with life" (*Topics in Language Disorders*) put coaching in the title on purpose. After she retired she trained as a life coach, including work associated with Martin E. P. Seligman's authentic-happiness coaching — a public biographical note on Arizona's faculty page, not a protocol. The sequence matters for historians: a senior aphasiologist, trained in a medical-model century, spent late career arguing that quality of life, self-determination, and ordinary pleasure were not extras around "real" language treatment. National Aphasia Association memorial language remembered her asking why the field was not taking care of the whole person when everyone else was still counting the medical model as the whole job. That is a memorial's paraphrase. It is not a treatment instruction.

Yoga, meditation, music, and art appear in some memorials as things she was willing to name when they were still easy to mock. Name them as a historical willingness. Do not print a home program. This series does not teach aphasia treatment, counseling steps, or coaching drills.

## What she was not

She was not a licensed professional counselor by virtue of a CSD counseling textbook. She was not John L. Holland. She was not a founder in the therapy-history pack's sense. She was a speech-language pathologist who made counseling in communication disorders a book with a point of view, and who treated functional communication as something you could try to measure without pretending a test was a life.

She mentored a long list of doctoral students; a 2024 *Seminars in Speech and Language* appreciation counted more than thirty. Mentorship is part of how a skill lineage moves. It is not a reason to invent classroom scenes.

## Past tense, and a clinic that still reads her

WOW Therapies is a Southeast Arkansas speech-language practice. Stroke, progressive disease, and the family that has to live with a language that will not come back are not abstractions here. Holland's counseling book is one of the objects a CSD student can be handed beside Luterman. They do not say the same thing. Luterman's through-line is affect and grief against the information-dump, born in a family nursery for hearing difference. Holland's through-line is wellness and getting-on-with-life in chronic aphasia, from a researcher who had already tried to score daily communication. A clinic can inherit both without collapsing them.

ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice named counseling as a domain about communication, feeding, and swallowing. Holland's 2007–2018 titles are one of the ways that domain acquired a wellness vocabulary. The vocabulary can go soft if a later reader hears only "positive." Her paper trail is tougher: a test of daily living, a career in aphasiology, a late book that refused to treat counseling as the polite ten minutes after the real work.

She died in 2023. The books remain dated objects. This pack will not use a generated face, a workshop photograph of uncertain license, or a caption that implies she is still lecturing. Type is enough.
