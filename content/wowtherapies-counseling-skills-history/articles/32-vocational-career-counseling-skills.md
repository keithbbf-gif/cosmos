**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In January 1908 the Vocation Bureau opened as a department of Boston's Civic Service House, a North End settlement house Pauline Agassiz Shaw had founded in 1901 for immigrant and working-poor education. Frank Parsons, engineer, lawyer, reformer, and director of the Breadwinners' Institute there, became the Bureau's director. He died in Boston on 26 September 1908, before a planned October course that would have trained vocational counselors. *Choosing a Vocation* (Houghton Mifflin, 1909) was published posthumously. Ralph Albertson's 1 May 1909 introductory note says the manuscript was practically ready when Parsons died. Meyer Bloomfield, who had headed Civic Service House, later directed the Bureau. The book is the checkable object. The man did not live to see the binding.

Later textbooks treated Parsons as if vocational guidance had a single origin point and a single name attached to it. Settlement houses, teachers, and labor reformers were already in the work. Parsons wrote a method down and died on the way to a training class. That is enough.

Early-1900s Boston reform portraits often still sit in copyright. Until a clean public-domain file is confirmed, this page uses type or the 1909 title page, not a guessed face.

## Three parts, a file, a city

Parsons's formula, as the 1909 book organizes it, is a three-part skill claim: a clear understanding of the person (aptitudes, interests, limitations); a knowledge of the requirements and conditions of work; and "true reasoning" on the relations between the two. The Bureau gathered facts. It interviewed. It filed. Part III of the book includes cases that had already appeared in newspapers. Those cases are 1908–1909 public documents. This series will not turn them into composite clients, and it will not reprint them as a matching exercise for a reader.

The room is a settlement house, not a psychoanalytic couch and not a 1965 school meeting. Vocational guidance as a counseling skill package begins as Progressive Era urban reform: immigration, factories, the fear that a young person would "drop into" a job. Civic Service House is the geography. The North End is not a metaphor. It is a neighborhood.

The National Vocational Guidance Association organized in 1913. It would, much later, become the National Career Development Association (1985 rename). The rename is the last move this essay will reach. First the tests, the wars, and two theories that rewrote Parsons without erasing him.

## Tests, wars, and the matching machine

World War I army testing, the growth of psychometrics, and then the GI Bill's flood of students made "know the person" into a score. E. G. Williamson's trait-and-factor work at Minnesota is the mid-century university version of Parsons's match: measure, inform, decide. School guidance in the 1920s–1950s often meant a card, a conference period, and a track. Essay 01 already said the card survived. This file says the card had a 1909 grandfather.

Matching can be a kindness. It can also be a sorting of class, race, and gender into the jobs a counselor's tables already believed in. *Choosing a Vocation* included remarks on work for women that are of their year. A 2026 history does not need a composite "girl in the Bureau" to say that a matching skill inherits the labor market's inequalities. It only needs the 1909 table of contents and the later civil-rights critique of guidance as tracking.

## Super 1953: development instead of a snapshot

Donald E. Super published "A Theory of Vocational Development" in *American Psychologist* 8 (1953): 185–190. The skill claim shifted. A vocation was not only a match at a moment. It was a developmental process across a life, with stages, with a self-concept that sought implementation in work, with "career maturity" as a later named variable. *The Psychology of Careers* (1957) and later life-span, life-space statements expanded the 1953 paper. This file keeps 1953 as the dated door.

Super did not throw Parsons out. He historicized him. A person at 16 and the same person at 40 are not the same file. School counselors who had been trained to place a graduate now had a theory that said placement was an episode. The episode still mattered. The life around it had become the object.

Women's career development, interrupted work, and the unpaid labor the 1953 paper's generation often treated as background became later Super-school problems. Feminist counseling (essay 28) and the competencies paper (essay 29) are how those problems re-entered counselor education as more than a footnote to Super. Keep the 1953 paper anyway. It is the moment vocational psychology said *time*.

## John L. Holland, 1973, and the other Holland

John L. Holland published "A Theory of Vocational Choice" in the *Journal of Counseling Psychology* in 1959. Types were already in motion. The hexagon and the RIASEC labels — Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional — are the 1973 book's public machine: *Making Vocational Choices: A Theory of Careers* (Prentice-Hall, 1973). Congruence between person and environment, consistency, differentiation: the 1973 vocabulary. The Self-Directed Search and related inventories carried the hexagon into high-school libraries and college career centers.

John L. Holland is not Audrey L. Holland. Audrey L. Holland (1927–2023) is the speech-language pathologist whose counseling-in-CSD texts sit in this pack's figure essay 37 and in the SLPWOW weather. The surname is a collision this series will not allow a search engine to win. RIASEC is a vocational-personality typology. Aphasia counseling is a different room. If a reader came here from a speech-clinic page, that is the sentence to keep.

Later Holland editions (1985 and after) added vocational identity and adjusted the weight of constructs. This file does not need every revision. 1959 is the journal door. 1973 is the book the INDEX named. The hexagon is a historical object. It is not a personality you must become. This series will not print a self-score RIASEC as a quiz.

## From vocational guidance to career counseling

The language shift of the 1970s–1980s — "career" over "vocation," "career counseling" over "vocational guidance" — is more than fashion. It marks Super's developmental claim winning the letterhead, while Holland's typology won the bulletin board. NCDA's 1985 name is the institutional stamp. School counseling's later National Model documents (essay 33's weather) absorbed career as one domain among others. Rehabilitation counseling (essay 34) absorbed work as a disability-rights and statute history (Smith-Fess 1920 and after). This file is the skill line from 1908 matching through 1953 time through 1973 type.

Critics of Holland asked whether six types could hold a life, and whether congruence talk blessed the labor market as a natural habitat. Critics of matching asked who wrote the occupational information. Both criticisms are part of the skill's history. A hexagon that cannot say "the job may be the problem" has become a poster.

## A speech clinic, "what can he do," no RIASEC homework

WOW Therapies is a speech-language practice in Southeast Arkansas. It does not run a Vocation Bureau. Families still ask, after a communication or swallow change, what work and school are still possible. That question is older than ASHA. It is Parsons's question in a clinical accent. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice locates counseling in communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not mint a career counselor. An SLP who talks with a teacher about classroom access is not running the Self-Directed Search. Rehabilitation and school colleagues may. Essay 33 and 34 are those lineages.

Heritage education can say that the helping professions learned to treat work as a counseling object in a 1908 settlement house, a 1909 book the author did not live to see, a 1953 developmental paper, and a 1973 typology whose author's name must not be confused with this pack's SLP figure. The portrait, if any, waits on a license. The 1909 title page does not.

Do not choose a career from this page. Take the dates: Bureau 1908, death 26 September 1908, book 1909, NVGA 1913, Super 1953, Holland 1959 and 1973, NCDA 1985. And the correction: John L., not Audrey.
