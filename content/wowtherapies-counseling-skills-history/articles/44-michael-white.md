**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Michael Kingsley White was born in Adelaide, South Australia, on 29 December 1948. He died in San Diego on 4 April 2008, age 59, of a heart attack while traveling to teach. The *New York Times* printed an obituary. The Dulwich Centre, the Adelaide counseling service he had co-founded, confirmed the date. Between those two dates an Australian social worker and a New Zealand colleague taught the English-speaking helping trades that a problem could be spoken as a story with a history, not only as a symptom with a code.

This is a figure essay. The modality essay in this pack — narrative re-authoring — already named the package. White's page is the person, the address, and the dates. No workshop headshot. No generated face. Type is enough.

## A social worker, then a centre

He worked briefly as a probation and welfare officer, then took an undergraduate social work degree from the institution now called the University of South Australia (1979 in public obituaries). He worked as a psychiatric social worker at Adelaide Children's Hospital. In 1983 he and Cheryl White established the Dulwich Centre in Adelaide as a base for family therapy and community work. Cheryl White also established Dulwich Centre Publications, which remains the paper trail: journals, conferences, a literature that did not have to wait for New York.

David Epston, in New Zealand, was the colleague. The two developed, through the 1980s, the practices later collected as narrative therapy. *Literate Means to Therapeutic Ends* (Dulwich Centre Publications, 1989) is the local object. W. W. Norton's *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* (1990) is the object most North American students own. The Norton book is not a sudden invention. It is an Adelaide–Auckland literature meeting a New York list. Historians who start in 1990 without 1989 have already misplaced the mailbox.

Foucault appears in that literature as a named influence on how power and "the problem" get spoken. Derrida appears in some later commentaries. This page will not lecture French philosophy. It will say that an Australian family-therapy culture read those writers as tools for questioning professional monopoly over a person's story. You can dispute the reading. You cannot, if you are writing this history, pretend the 1990 book was only a nice metaphor about storytelling.

## Externalizing, unique outcomes, a literate practice

The 1990 book and the Dulwich papers described practices with names: externalizing conversations, unique outcomes, re-authoring, letter-writing, definitional ceremony. Those names are historical objects. They are not reprinted here as scripts. A reader who wants the method wants a trained practitioner and the books, not a webpage that compresses a 1990 argument — the problem spoken as something other than the person — into tote-bag English. White's later *Maps of Narrative Practice* (Norton, 2007) tried to draw the practices as maps a student could study. Maps are still not a treatment plan on this URL.

*Experience, Contradiction, Narrative and Imagination* (Dulwich, with Epston; selected papers spanning the late 1980s and early 1990s) is the local collected object for people who want the papers before Norton. Community work associated with Dulwich — including published collaborations with Aboriginal Australian communities — is part of the same mailbox. Name it as published community practice. Do not invent a project, a quotation, or a photograph.

The Australia–New Zealand lineage matters because so much of this pack is a North Atlantic story: Chicago, Wisconsin, Colorado, Albuquerque, Maryland, Boston. White's address was Adelaide. Epston's was New Zealand. The helping hour they described had to work in rooms that were not Harvard clinics and not billed as fifty-minute American psychotherapy. Community work, witnessing, and the written word (letters, documents) were not extras. They were the method's claim against an oral, expert-centered hour.

White kept teaching internationally until the 2008 trip. San Diego was a workshop city, which is why this pack's portrait rule is strict: a traveling teacher's headshot from an unpublished workshop is the image most likely to be scraped and the least likely to be cleanly licensed. Dulwich's own archive page exists for people who want the centre's photographs under the centre's terms. This folder uses none of them.

After 2008 Dulwich remained. An anthology of previously unpublished papers, *Narrative Practice: Continuing the Conversations*, appeared from Norton in 2011. David Denborough, Cheryl White, and others kept the centre's teaching and publishing. That is institutional aftermath, not a séance. This pack's job is the 1948–2008 life and the 1989–2007 books.

## What he was not

He was not an American counseling-psychology textbook author. He was not Ivey, not Egan, not a microskills man. Students who meet "narrative" only as a chapter after solution-focused questions have already lost the Southern Hemisphere address and the 1980s family-therapy context. He was not a founder in the therapy-history pack's European sense. He was a social worker who, with Epston, made a literate practice travel.

Obituaries mentioned childhood trauma as a population he worked with. This page will not invent cases, will not print a trauma protocol, and will not use a dead man's clients as color. The *Times* and *Guardian* obituaries are the public record of the death. Cause and place are in those notices. They are enough.

## A speech clinic, a story, and a refusal to annex

WOW Therapies is a Southeast Arkansas speech-language practice. People in its rooms already live inside stories: the child who is "just shy," the grandfather who is "not himself" after a stroke, the family that has been told to be inspiring. Narrative therapy, as White and Epston published it, is a specific family-therapy tradition. It is not an SLP's license to "re-author" a swallow result. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names counseling about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not name Dulwich. The useful inheritance is smaller and harder: a professional can ask whose story of the problem is in charge. The dangerous inheritance is a clinic that borrows "narrative" as décor.

This series will not teach externalizing questions. It will not offer a unique-outcomes worksheet. White died on 4 April 2008. The books remain. The portrait in this folder is none. If a later graphics pass wants an image, it can set type, a 1990 title page if a library scan is honestly licensed, or a map of Australia and New Zealand. It cannot lift a workshop photograph of uncertain copyright and call it heritage.

The therapy-history pack can keep the European founders. This pack keeps an Adelaide centre, a 1989 Dulwich book, a 1990 Norton book, a 2007 book of maps, and a life that ended, in public, on a teaching trip. Complementary, not a reprint of the re-authoring essay. The dates are the discipline.
