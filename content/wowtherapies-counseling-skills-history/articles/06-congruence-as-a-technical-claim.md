**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1962 Charles B. Truax, working with support from NIMH Grant M3496 and Vocational Rehabilitation Administration Grant RD906, circulated a Genuineness Scale — also called a Therapist Self-Congruence Scale — so that raters could place a slice of tape on a ladder. The scale is dated in Truax's own later grant notes and in the chapter that would be bound as *Toward Effective Counseling and Psychotherapy* (Aldine, 1967). Accurate Empathy had come a year earlier, in 1961. Unconditional positive regard, renamed in the same shop as nonpossessive warmth, shared the 1962 vintage. Genuineness was the awkward sibling. Empathy at least looked like a craft. Regard at least looked like an ethic. Genuineness looked like a personality, and personalities are not supposed to be homework.

This essay is about that awkwardness as a *technical* problem. Carl Rogers's 1957 paper in the *Journal of Consulting Psychology* had made the therapist's congruence one of six conditions, not a lifestyle. Later training cultures turned it into "be authentic," which is a poster, and then into a score, which is a bureaucratic object. Living people who still teach helping skills deserve care here: no gossip, no health stories, only the public books and the public fight over whether you can train a person not to be fake.

## What the 1957 sentence actually required

"The Necessary and Sufficient Conditions of Therapeutic Personality Change" listed six conditions that had to exist and continue over a period of time. Two persons in psychological contact. A client in incongruence, vulnerable or anxious. A therapist who is congruent or integrated *in the relationship*. Unconditional positive regard. Empathic understanding, communicated. And the client's perception, to a minimal degree, that the understanding and the regard had arrived. Posters later kept three therapist qualities and lost the contact, the client's incongruence, and the perception gate. Congruence suffered a second amputation. It was taken out of the relationship and turned into a character trait: a congruent person, the sort who owns a cabin and tells the truth at dinner.

Rogers's technical claim was narrower. The therapist's experience and awareness in *this* hour were not at war. The helper was not, in the relationship, a divided advertisement. *On Becoming a Person* (1961) popularized the tone. The 1957 paper had already insisted that congruence was a matter of degree, like the other conditions, and that research operations would have to define it. That invitation is how Truax got a ladder. It is also how a philosophical word became a rating.

G. T. Barrett-Lennard's 1962 monograph in *Psychological Monographs* (Whole No. 562) built a Relationship Inventory so that the *client* could score empathic understanding, level of regard, unconditionality, and congruence. The inventory is a reminder that genuineness, if it is the 1957 condition, is not only what a rater hears. It is what the other chair receives. A later master's course that graded the student's "authenticity" from behind glass, without asking the speaker, had already changed the object.

## Wisconsin weather, a renamed virtue

Truax and Robert R. Carkhuff came out of the Wisconsin project — Mendota, a hospital census, a university condition under strain — with scales that they said were "closely tied to Rogers' statements" and then, in the same breath, retitled. The 1967 book is frank about the retitling. Empathy became *accurate* empathy, with a nod to moment-to-moment diagnostic accuracy that psychoanalytic readers would recognize. Unconditional positive regard became *nonpossessive warmth*, because the authors judged that unconditionality did not greatly contribute to outcome. Congruence became *genuineness*, because what seemed related to improvement was not a match between "organismic self" and self-concept but "the absence of defensiveness or phoniness."

That last clause is the whole technical fight. Rogers's word still carried a theory of the self: integration, awareness, a person not split. Truax's word is a negative: not phony. You can train a negative, or you can think you can. You tell a student to drop the professional mask. The student drops a mask and puts on a warmer mask. The rater, trained in the house style, scores the second mask as genuine. Critics later said this was the method scoring itself. They were not only being cruel. They were describing a loop that any observable "authenticity" risks.

Carkhuff's *Helping and Human Relations* (Holt, Rinehart and Winston, 1969, two volumes) shortened several ladders to five points for training and added scales that made the helper more active: concreteness, self-disclosure, confrontation, immediacy. Genuineness, on a five-point sheet, became a classroom object a beginner could miss in the morning and pass in the afternoon. Carkhuff is a living writer. The public claim of those volumes is that facilitative conditions can be taught at scale. The public risk, named by opponents for decades, is that a taught genuineness is a contradiction in terms.

## Authenticity versus technique

Existential therapists — the American readers of Buber, the supervisors who liked Rollo May — already had a quarrel with technique. Martin Buber's *I and Thou* (1923; English 1937) was not a counseling manual, but it was quoted as if it were. An I–Thou encounter, they said, cannot be an item on Ivey's hierarchy. Congruence-as-skill looked, from that chair, like a category error: you cannot schedule presence. Humanistic psychologists who had fought behaviorism on exactly this ground then watched their own conditions get operationalized by Truax and Carkhuff, who had been in Rogers's weather. The betrayal narrative is too neat. Rogers had asked for research operations in 1957. He got them. The surprise is only for people who wanted the conditions to remain untestable and therefore unfalsifiable.

The other side of the fight is administrative. Counselor education needed something to put on a midterm. "Is this student real?" is not a midterm question. "Does this two-minute slice score at level 3?" is. Once genuineness was a slice, it could be a curriculum. Once it was a curriculum, it could be a workshop for librarians, medical students, and, later, customer-service trainers who wanted "authentic engagement." The tape did not choose that audience. The five-point sheet traveled because it was light.

Sidney Jourard's *The Transparent Self* (1964; revised later) belongs to the same decade as a rival account. Jourard treated self-disclosure as a human obligation and a research topic, not as a Carkhuff level. Transparency, for him, was health. Counselors who read Jourard and Carkhuff in the same semester could think they had learned one skill. They had learned a moral essay and a rating technology. The later ethics of disclosure — taken up in this pack's confrontation essay — had to separate "being known" from "performing knownness." Congruence is the name of that separation when it works. When it fails, the helper's inner weather is dumped into the client's hour and called honesty.

## What phoniness was accused of being

Feminist and multicultural writers in the 1970s–1990s pointed out that "phony" is a culturally loaded insult. A woman who keeps a professional formality may be surviving a room, not lying. A Black counselor who does not match a White client's idea of warmth may be scored as less genuine by raters who have never left a university clinic. A first-generation student who code-switches is not a divided self in Rogers's sense; they are competent. The Genuineness Scale was not written to hear that competence. It was written to hear defensiveness and phoniness as Truax's raters understood those words in the early 1960s.

Disability and CSD rooms raise a quieter problem. An SLP delivering a swallow result cannot be "congruent" in the 1957 sense by sharing every flicker of dread. The professional role is already a frame. Luterman's later insistence that the clinician is a person in the room, not a pamphlet, is a humanistic inheritance. It is not permission to make the family's hour about the clinician's feelings. Congruence as a technical claim includes a boundary: the integration is *in the relationship*, in the service of the other person's contact with their own experience, not a license to confess.

The Wisconsin project, again, is the stress test. A university helper's congruence with a motivated student is one event. A helper's congruence on a ward, with a person who does not share the vocabulary of "feelings," is another. If the condition only appears when both people already speak the dialect, then it is not a sufficient condition. It is a coincidence of manners. Some of the later skepticism about the scales is skepticism about that coincidence.

## Living people, dead posters

Robert Carkhuff, Allen Ivey, Clara Hill, and other trainers who were alive at last verification (2026-09-14) still have their names on books that teach genuineness, authenticity, or "being oneself" as a professional behavior. This pack will not narrate their health, their families, or unsourced private conflict. The public problem is enough. Every later edition has had to decide whether congruence is a condition you offer, a trait you have, or a skill you demonstrate on video. Ivey's later *Intentional Interviewing* texts, with Mary Bradford Ivey and Carlos P. Zalaquett, put authenticity near the ethical and multicultural base of a skills hierarchy. That placement is a historical compromise: keep the humanistic word, put it under ethics, then teach attending anyway. Hill's *Helping Skills* treats helper self-awareness as a chapter, not as a score. Different teaching machines, same 1957 ghost.

The popular poster — "just be yourself" — is the condition's most successful false friend. The 1957 paper did not invite the therapist to be a personality. It invited a disciplined lack of split in a specific relationship, plus the other five conditions, plus time. Truax's 1962 scale invited a rater to hear the absence of phoniness in a short sample. Carkhuff's 1969 revision invited a school to graduate people who could produce that sample. Each step is intelligible. Each step changes the object. By the time a website promises an "authentic therapist," the technical claim has been laundered into marketing. Marketing is not a scale. It is not 1957. It is not even phoniness in Truax's sense. It is a different industry using a leftover word.

## Why the claim still has a date

Congruence became a skill because American helping wanted its moral weather to be countable. It remains a problem because counting weather is a strange occupation. A speech-language practice does not need a genuineness protocol on a public education page. It needs readers to know that when a clinician sounds like a person rather than a script, a mid-century research program is in the room — including the program's retitling of Rogers's word, including the charge that a trained authenticity is a performance, including Barrett-Lennard's reminder to ask the other chair.

The therapy-history pack can keep Rogers the man. This pack keeps the technical residue: a 1957 condition, a 1962 ladder, a 1967 renaming, a 1969 classroom, and a long argument about whether the ladder was measuring a person or a style. Style can be taught. A person, the existential critics said, cannot. The profession taught it anyway, then spent sixty years explaining the grade.
