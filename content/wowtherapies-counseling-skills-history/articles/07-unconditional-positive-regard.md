**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1954 Stanley Standal, writing a doctoral dissertation at the University of Chicago, gave Carl Rogers's circle a phrase they would not put down: unconditional positive regard. Three years later Rogers's paper in the *Journal of Consulting Psychology* made the phrase a condition of therapeutic personality change. The 1957 text is explicit about the borrowing. It is also explicit about what the phrase was supposed to rule out: a selective evaluating attitude, an "I like you only if you are thus and so." Dewey's word *prizing* is in the same paragraph. The condition was not a smile. It was a claim about the helper's inner weather toward a person as a separate person, with permission to have their own experience — including the ugly parts of that experience.

This essay is the history of that claim as a *skill and measurement problem*. It is not a biography of Rogers; that life sits in the therapy-history pack. It is not a lesson in how to accept people. It is a record of a 1962 rating scale, a rename to "nonpossessive warmth," a split in Barrett-Lennard's inventory, and a line of critics who said the whole thing was impossible, ideological, or a way of refusing to judge harm. "Be nice" is the false friend. The 1957 sentence will not carry that weight.

## A condition, not a temperament

The 1957 paper listed six conditions, of which unconditional positive regard was the fourth. The therapist experiences it for the client. The sixth condition required that the client perceive, at least minimally, the regard and the empathic understanding. Training posters that said "UPR, empathy, congruence" as a trinity had already dropped the perception gate. They had also dropped the first two conditions: contact, and a client in incongruence. UPR without contact is a mood. UPR without the other person's perception is a private virtue. Rogers had made it an interpersonal event.

He also made it a matter of degree. The paper says so, and then points toward research operations — including Q-sorts of the relationship — as a way to stop the phrase from meaning whatever a disciple wished. That is how a theological-sounding unconditional became an empirical object. It is also how it became vulnerable. If regard can be partial, "unconditional" is a direction, not a switch. Critics who heard a switch were reading the adjective harder than the paper's own hedging. Defenders who heard a switch were often selling a brand.

The 1957 paragraph is careful about possession. Caring that mainly satisfies the therapist's needs is out. Caring that treats the client as a separate person is in. Acceptance of "negative" and "bad" feelings is to be as real as acceptance of mature ones. The scare quotes are in the paper. They are doing work. They say that the helper's moral sorting of feelings is the problem under study. They do not say that violence is a feeling to be prized as a public act. Later critics would insist the paper had smuggled that confusion in. The paper is more interior than that. It is about the client's *expression* in an hour, not about a civic theory of punishment. Whether that interior focus is a moral achievement or a moral evasion is the fight that never ended.

## The 1962 ladder and the warmth rename

Charles Truax's Unconditional Positive Regard Scale is dated to 1962 in the same grant history that dates Accurate Empathy to 1961 and Genuineness to 1962 (NIMH M3496; VRA RD906). Raters heard short tape segments. The dimension, as later printed in Truax and Carkhuff's 1967 Aldine book, ran from a high end — the patient's experience taken as part of that person, without conditions — toward conditional or possessive warmth, approval withdrawn when the patient was disapproved. This pack will not reprint the scale's stage language as a worksheet. The historical fact is that regard became a ladder.

Then the shop changed the sign on the ladder. *Toward Effective Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1967) states that the evidence had suggested unconditionality of positive regard did not greatly contribute to outcome, and that what seemed related was the communication of a "nonpossessive" warmth, with a nod to Franz Alexander (1948). The identifying label moved. Students who learned "UPR" in a theory course and "warmth" on a rating sheet were not always told they were looking at a revision. Revisions are how research talks to itself. They are also how a sacred phrase gets quietly demoted.

Robert Carkhuff's 1969 five-point versions, in *Helping and Human Relations*, folded respect and warmth into the facilitative cluster a beginner could miss and then hit. Level language became proverb. The proverb outlived the grant. Counselor education now had a way to fail a student for being "conditional" without having to argue philosophy. The student heard: be warmer. The scale, even after the rename, was trying to hear something more specific — whether caring was being used as a leash.

## Barrett-Lennard splits the atom

G. T. Barrett-Lennard's 1962 *Psychological Monographs* paper, "Dimensions of Therapist Response as Causal Factors in Therapeutic Change," refused to treat UPR as a single variable. The Relationship Inventory asked the client (and, in other forms, the therapist) about *level of regard* and *unconditionality* as separate dimensions, alongside empathic understanding and congruence. You could, on that instrument, score as warmly positive and still highly conditional: I like you a great deal when you are the person I prefer. You could, in principle, score as less gushingly positive and still relatively unconditional. The split is the most technically interesting thing that happened to the phrase in its first decade.

It is also the split that training culture underused. Warmth is easy to demonstrate on a tape. Unconditionality is a pattern over time. A two-minute slice can hear a kind voice. It cannot hear whether the kindness will survive the hour in which the client is ugly, boring, or politically intolerable to the helper. Barrett-Lennard's instrument, by asking the person in the other chair about a relationship, at least gestured at duration. Observer ratings of slices gestured at reliability. The two technologies did not measure the same object, though both were filed under Rogers.

Later person-centered writers — Lietaer, for example, in essays of the 1980s — kept trying to say that UPR is a process in a relationship, not an on/off saintliness. The name works against them. "Unconditional" sounds like a vow. Vows attract both devotees and prosecutors.

## Impossible, ideological, or a refusal to name harm

Jeffrey Masson's *Against Therapy* (1988; later editions) is a blunt public attack on the helping professions' moral theater. Unconditional acceptance, in that prosecution, is a lie that protects therapists from politics. Other critics, cited in person-centered textbooks as a cluster rather than a single paper — Masson, Purton's discussions of self and behavior, Seager's 2003 line that UPR is impossible in any human relationship — argued that you cannot prize a person while refusing to condone what the person does, because the self is not detachable from the doing. Violence, in that argument, is not a "negative feeling" to be received. It is an act. A therapy that will not say so is an ideology of niceness wearing a 1957 citation.

Person-centered replies, equally public, said the critics had flattened a degree-word into a civic program. UPR was never a claim that the therapist has no morals outside the hour, or that the law should not act. It was a claim about the hour's inner hospitality toward the client's *experience*. Rogers's own later political writing — the encounter-culture years, the panels, the books after Chicago and Wisconsin — complicated the defense, because he did sometimes talk as if a way of relating could scale to groups and nations. This pack will not settle the political argument. It will say that the skill literature and the ideological literature used the same three words for different jobs.

Feminist therapists in the 1970s–1990s had a sharper, closer objection. Unconditional regard offered by a male professional to a woman who had been trained to earn love by disappearing could recreate the disappearance and call it acceptance. Phyllis Chesler's *Women and Madness* (1972) is not a UPR monograph, but it is a dated document of distrust toward psychiatry's and psychology's "help." Laura S. Brown's later *Subversive Dialogues* (1994) belongs to a feminist-therapy skill history this pack takes up elsewhere. The UPR-specific point is small and hard: regard that does not see gender, race, and power is not unconditional. It is uninformed. Multicultural writers in the Sue lineage said the parallel thing about "generic" warmth: warmth toward a White feeling vocabulary, withdrawn — or never extended — when the client's anger was politically inconvenient.

None of that is a proof that the 1957 condition was a trick. It is a proof that "unconditional" is an expensive word, and that American training often paid for it with smiles.

## Not niceness, and not a speech-clinic sticker

Niceness is an etiquette. It has a Southern form and a clinic form. It can coexist with extremely conditional inner weather: I will be sweet if you are compliant. UPR, in the 1957 sense, is almost the opposite claim. The test is the hour in which the other person is not easy to prize. Truax's ladder, even under the warmth rename, was built to hear that test in miniature. Barrett-Lennard's unconditionality scale was built to hear it as a pattern. Neither instrument is a moral victory. Both are evidence that the mid-century project knew niceness was not the variable.

A speech-language pathologist who is kind to a tired parent is doing something decent and ordinary. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names counseling about communication, feeding, and swallowing; it does not name Standal's dissertation. Importing UPR as a brand onto a small-city practice would be a category mistake and a marketing temptation this pack refuses. The useful residue is diagnostic in the historical sense: when a professional's warmth is a leash — praise for the fluent child, chill for the ugly diagnosis, charm for the paying adult — you are looking at the thing the 1962 scale was trying, clumsily, to catch. Calling the leash "high standards" does not make it unconditioned.

## What remains dated

Standal 1954 named it. Rogers 1957 made it a condition and tied it to the client's perception. Truax 1962 put it on a ladder; Truax and Carkhuff 1967 half-abandoned unconditionality for nonpossessive warmth. Barrett-Lennard 1962 split level from unconditionality and asked the other chair. Critics from Masson to later person-centered dissenters called it impossible or ideological. Feminist and multicultural writers said the unconditional often had a social type. The posters said be nice.

The posters won the internet. The technical literature won a few rating manuals and a permanent argument. This series keeps the argument, dated, and leaves the smile where it belongs — in manners, which are not a sufficient condition of anyone's personality change, and were never what Standal's phrase was for.
