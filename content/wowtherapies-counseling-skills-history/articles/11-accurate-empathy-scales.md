**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1970 *Psychological Bulletin* printed a short paper by Jack M. Chinsky and Julian Rappaport, "Brief Critique of the Meaning and Reliability of 'Accurate Empathy' Ratings." The Accurate Empathy Scale had been developed by Charles B. Truax in 1961, circulated with NIMH and Vocational Rehabilitation Administration support, and bound, with its sibling scales, in Truax and Robert R. Carkhuff's *Toward Effective Counseling and Psychotherapy* (Aldine, 1967). For a decade the ladder had let a rater hear a two-to-five-minute slice of tape and place a helper on a nine-point climb. Chinsky and Rappaport said the climb was a confusion of a construct: too few therapists and raters in the original work, a suspicion that vocal quality or some other house trait was being scored, reliability claims that did not mean what readers hoped. Truax's rejoinder, "The Meaning and Reliability of Accurate Empathy Ratings," appeared in the same journal in 1972. The fight is the history this essay keeps. The empathy essay in this pack followed a word from Lipps to Titchener to Rogers. This one follows a *sheet of paper*.

This series will not reprint scale items, stage anchors, or anything a classroom could use as a worksheet. It will date the instruments, name the institutional weather that grew them, and say what the replication arguments were actually about.

## Three ladders, two years, one shop

Truax's own notes, later printed with the scales, date Accurate Empathy to 1961 and Unconditional Positive Regard and Genuineness (or Self-Congruence) to 1962. The grants are named: NIMH M3496, VRA RD906. The acknowledgments are a Wisconsin roster — Rogers, Gendlin, van der Veen, colleagues and student raters. The scales were to be applied by trained independent judges to samples of therapy interaction, often randomly extracted. The scoring unit was short on purpose. Reliability loves a short unit. Meaning, the critics would say, does not.

The 1967 Aldine book is the bound claim. Therapist-offered conditions, rated from tape, relate to patient change. Tables of reliability coefficients fill the training chapter. Group and individual studies from the mid-1960s — Truax and Wargo, Truax, Carkhuff and Kodman, the Johns Hopkins collaborations with Jerome Frank's shop — are recruited as a pile. The pile convinced a generation of counselor educators that they could stop arguing metaphysics and start grading slices. It did not convince everyone who read the *Bulletin*.

Labels had already shifted. Truax and Carkhuff wrote that they had begun "closely tied to Rogers' statements" from the 1957 conditions paper and then moved. Empathy became *accurate* empathy, said to contain an element of moment-to-moment diagnostic accuracy that psychoanalytic ears would recognize. Unconditional positive regard became *nonpossessive warmth*, because unconditionality, they judged, did not greatly contribute to outcome. Congruence became *genuineness*, the absence of defensiveness or phoniness rather than a full theory of the self. The retitling is not a scandal. It is how operationalists talk. It is also how a 1957 philosophy became a 1967 technology with different nouns.

## The other chair: Barrett-Lennard 1962

G. T. Barrett-Lennard's "Dimensions of Therapist Response as Causal Factors in Therapeutic Change" (*Psychological Monographs*, 1962, 76, no. 43, Whole No. 562) is the other instrument of the same moment, and it is not a Truax ladder. The Relationship Inventory asked the client to score the relationship on empathic understanding, level of regard, unconditionality of regard, and congruence (an early form also toyed with "willingness to be known"). Barrett-Lennard had been in the Chicago orbit. He split UPR into two variables because he did not think it was unitary. He asked the person who was *there*, not a judge who arrived later with headphones.

The two technologies are still filed, in lazy histories, as "the empathy scales." They disagree about the witness. Observer ratings can be reliable across judges and still measure a dialect those judges were trained to love. Client inventories can measure a relationship and still be confounded by liking, gratitude, or fear of the helper. Later reviews — Gurman in the 1970s, then person-centered measurement essays such as Freire and Grafanaki's 2010 survey — treated the BLRI and the Truax scales as a pair that dominated research on the conditions. Dominance is not convergence. A field can run two instruments for forty years without deciding which object is the 1957 condition.

Rogers had required that the client perceive the empathy and the regard. Barrett-Lennard's inventory at least honors that sixth condition as a measurement choice. Truax's slices honor a different 1957 invitation: define the conditions as research operations that observers can apply. Both are legitimate children. They are not twins.

## Five points, more verbs: Carkhuff 1969

Robert R. Carkhuff's *Helping and Human Relations* (Holt, Rinehart and Winston, 1969, two volumes) truncated Accurate Empathy into a five-point Empathic Understanding in Interpersonal Process Scale. Later commentators called it a truncation aimed at reliability and at reducing the ambiguity of nine steps. Correlations between the nine-point and five-point versions were reported as high in subsequent comparison studies; Engram and Vandergoot (1978) is one such public comparison. High correlation does not mean the classroom object was the same philosophical object. Nine points invite fine-grained tracking. Five points invite a proverb. Level 3 as an "interchangeable" response became the proverb. Students aimed at 3 and called it a personality.

Carkhuff also added scales that made the helper more active — concreteness, confrontation, immediacy, self-disclosure — which this pack's confrontation essay treats as a second toolkit. For empathy's measurement history the addition matters because it changed what a "high" helper was. Truax's original Accurate Empathy still sounded like tracking. Carkhuff's cluster sounded like a full professional repertoire. Training programs that adopted 1969 as a bible were no longer only testing whether a student could hear. They were testing whether a student could *do*, in a house style, on a five-point sheet.

Carkhuff is a living writer. The public claim of the volumes is scale and teachability. The public risk is the house style: raters scoring students who sound like the raters.

## What the 1970–1973 papers were attacking

Chinsky and Rappaport (1970) went at meaning and reliability together. If a scale's reliability depends on a small guild of raters trained in one center, the number is a local dialect. If ratings follow a therapist's voice quality across patients, the scale may be hearing timbre. Their 1972 follow-up in *Psychological Bulletin*, "Accurate Empathy: Confusion of a Construct," kept the prosecution open. Truax's 1972 rejoinder defended the reliability math and the training of judges. Readers without a taste for psychometrics still inherited a smell: perhaps the famous ladder was a way of grading membership in a school.

Larry E. Beutler's 1973 note in the *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, "Some Sources of Variance in 'Accurate Empathy' Ratings," added a different doubt. Empathy, on his estimates, might not be a stable therapist trait from segment to segment and patient to patient. It might be dyadic — a relationship variable. If so, grading the student as if they carried a portable 5.0 was a category error. The 1957 paper had, in a way, already said this: conditions exist *in a relationship*, and the client's perception is a gate. The training culture had needed a trait anyway, because traits can be admissions criteria.

Other comparison papers — Feldstein and Gladstein's 1980 look at four empathy measures against Rogerian criteria — concluded that none of the popular instruments met all the conceptual tests, though they might be promising if empathy were defined as a verbal skill. That last clause is the quiet scandal. If you shrink empathy to a verbal skill, of course a verbal scale will look valid. The 1909 Titchener word, the 1957 "as if," and Kohut's 1971 immersion were larger than a verbal skill. The sheet of paper won because it could be Xeroxed.

Replication, in the ordinary scientific sense, was uneven. Conditions-and-outcome findings appeared, failed to appear, appeared in house journals, looked weaker in hostile reanalyses. This pack will not pretend to a meta-analytic last word that it has not performed. It will say that by the late 1970s a fair historian could no longer treat the 1967 tables as a closed case. The instruments remained in social-work and counselor-education classrooms long after many research labs had moved to other measures — Hogan's 1969 self-report, later the Interpersonal Reactivity Index, still later the Barrett-Lennard in revised forms, and, in motivational interviewing, entirely different coding manuals (MITI) that treated empathy as a global rating with a different pedigree.

## What a slice cannot hear

A two-minute extract cannot hear unconditionality over a year. It cannot hear whether the helper's tracking is accurate to a White feeling vocabulary and deaf to another. It cannot hear a CSD hour in which the "response" must wait on a motor plan. It cannot hear Luterman's parent, rearranging a life after a hearing diagnosis, who does not need a Level 4 additive empathy sentence. It cannot hear Kohut's prolonged immersion, which was never a slice. The Wisconsin project had already asked whether university conditions survived a hospital census. The scale is a Wisconsin object. Some of the skepticism about it is skepticism about that translation: a grant office's ear, a ward's mouth.

Feminist and multicultural critiques of "accurate" as a word are in the empathy essay. They belong again here as a measurement remark. Accuracy implies a correct inner object. If the inner object is shaped by what is safe to say, the rater scores the safety and calls it the person. Barrett-Lennard's unconditionality scale, over a longer relationship, is slightly better built for that problem. It was still a questionnaire, with a questionnaire's manners.

## Why the sheet still sits on desks

Counselor education kept Truax and Carkhuff because they graded the student. That is an administrative history as much as a scientific one. Medical and nursing schools borrowed the five-point empathy idea because it fit an OSCE. The original nine-point Accurate Empathy Scale is now often a citation rather than a tool. Carkhuff's five-point ghost remains wherever a syllabus says "interchangeable response." Barrett-Lennard's inventory remains wherever a researcher still wants the other chair.

A speech-language practice does not need these sheets on a public education page, and this series will not provide them. WOW Therapies' license is communication, not a Truax rating. The inheritance that earns a mention is only this: once empathy, regard, and genuineness were ladders, every later helping guild had to decide whether to climb, to ask the client instead, or to stop pretending a slice was a relationship. The 1961–1969 sequence is the climb. The 1962 BLRI is the other chair. The 1970–1973 *Bulletin* and *JCCP* papers are the refusal. Keep all three. A scale without its fight is an advertisement.
