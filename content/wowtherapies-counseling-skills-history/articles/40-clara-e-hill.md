**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

In 1999 the American Psychological Association issued Clara E. Hill's *Helping Skills: Facilitating Exploration, Insight, and Action*. Karen M. O'Brien is on the first edition as a contributor. The subtitle was the whole architecture. Three words, in that order, told a student what the hour was for, and in what sequence a beginner might try to be of use. APA putting a helping-skills book on its list was itself a historical event: a skills manual from the Association's press, not only from Brooks/Cole.

Hill is living at last verification (2026-09-14). Public academic pages list a 1948 birth year [VERIFY]. She joined the University of Maryland, College Park, Department of Psychology in 1974, the same year as her Ph.D. from Southern Illinois University, and retired from Maryland in 2023. Public books, public journal offices, and public research programs only. No family gossip. No health speculation. Portrait: none.

## A process researcher who wrote a training book

Hill's career is easy to misfile as "the helping-skills person." The skills book is the object most counselors own. The research program underneath it is older and stranger. She published *Therapist Techniques and Client Outcomes: Eight Cases of Brief Psychotherapy* (Sage, 1989), a case-level look at what helpers actually do, moment by moment, rather than what their school said they did. She edited the *Journal of Counseling Psychology* from 1994 to 1999. She served as president of the Society for Psychotherapy Research (1995; she had also presided over the North American SPR in 1990). Those offices are how a field decides what counts as a finding. They are not decorations.

Awards in the public Maryland biography include the Leona Tyler Award (Society of Counseling Psychology), Division 29's Distinguished Psychologist Award, and the Society for Psychotherapy Research's Distinguished Research Career Award. Name them as the profession's own markers. Do not turn them into a halo.

Consensual Qualitative Research (CQR), developed with colleagues, became a named method for interview studies in counseling psychology: domains, core ideas, cross-analysis, auditors. It is a research method. It is not a clinical protocol. Students who meet Hill only through *Helping Skills* can miss that she spent as much of her career arguing about how to study an hour as how to teach one.

## Exploration, insight, action — as a historical sequence

The 1999 book's three-part sequence has a family resemblance to older staged models (Carkhuff's exploration-to-action talk; Egan's skilled-helper stages). Hill's version was built for a psychology department's helping-skills course and for an APA audience that wanted measurable helper behaviors without pretending the behaviors were the whole of therapy. *Exploration* gathered the person's story and feeling. *Insight* aimed at new understanding. *Action* aimed at what might be tried outside the room. Later editions (2004, 2009, 2014, 2020, per this pack's bibliography) revised examples, research citations, and the surrounding ethics. Cite the edition on the desk.

The sequence is a teaching architecture. It is not a law of human change. It is not reprinted here as a worksheet. Beginners like sequences because they reduce panic. Supervisors like them because they can say where a tape got stuck. Critics, including Hill's own later research culture, know that a real hour does not always climb the three stairs in order. The book's honesty, across editions, was to keep attaching studies to the skills rather than letting the sequence float free as folklore.

Rogers is in the basement of any exploration chapter; the therapy-history pack already has him. Hill's contribution to *this* pack is a late-century American psychology press deciding that helping skills were a researchable undergraduate-to-graduate curriculum, with insight and action given as much page space as empathy. Carkhuff graded a response. Ivey isolated a microskill. Hill built a course that tried to hold a beginning, a middle, and a next step, and then went back to the lab to see whether the hour had done what the chapter promised.

## Dreams, without a couch poster

*Working with Dreams in Psychotherapy* (Guilford, 1996) and later papers on a cognitive-experiential model of dream interpretation are public research, not a Jungian brand and not a Freudian reprint. Hill and colleagues ran analogue studies that compared conditions — computer-assisted, empathy, empathy plus input — and published the comparisons in the *Journal of Counseling Psychology* and elsewhere. Dream work, in that program, was a process she could try to take apart. It is mentioned here because the user of this series should know that "the helping-skills author" also ran a decades-long research line on a topic many skills textbooks quietly drop. It is not mentioned so that a reader can interpret a dream tonight. This series does not teach dream work.

Meaning-in-life research and later books sit in the same late career: a process researcher turning to what clients said the hour was for. Again: public books. Not a workbook.

## What a Maryland career changed in the syllabus

Counseling psychology, as a Division 17 tradition, had long claimed to be the skills-and-research specialty. Hill's APA book gave that claim a transferable object. Egan remained the Brooks/Cole giant in many counselor-education programs; Hill gave psychology departments a parallel that spoke their citation language. Students who took both could be forgiven for thinking they had learned one thing twice. The sequences rhyme. The research cultures do not. Egan's later editions leaned into problem-management and organizational life. Hill kept publishing process studies, dream studies, and CQR manuals.

## Why this clinic files her under teaching, not treatment

WOW Therapies is a Southeast Arkansas speech-language practice. It does not run a dream clinic. It does meet students and early-career clinicians who have been taught a three-step helping model under someone's name. Sometimes the name is Egan. Sometimes it is Hill. Sometimes it is a slide with no name. The inheritance worth keeping is the researcher's stubbornness: a skill should be teachable *and* checkable. The inheritance to refuse is the sequence offered as a self-administered treatment. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice does not name Maryland 1999. Once helping was a course with a beginning, a middle, and an action chapter, every allied-health syllabus had to decide whether to borrow the architecture or to keep calling the same behaviors "rapport."

The therapy-history pack can keep the founders of schools. This pack keeps Hill as a teaching figure: the 1999 APA book, the process lab, the dream studies as a research program, and a living scholar whose private life is not our copy.
