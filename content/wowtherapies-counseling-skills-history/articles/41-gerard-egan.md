**Educational note.** This is history for a general reader. It is not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan, and not a substitute for care with a licensed clinician.

Brooks/Cole put Gerard Egan's *The Skilled Helper: A Model for Systematic Helping and Interpersonal Relating* into the 1975 catalog. Two hundred fifty-eight pages, Monterey, California, ISBN 0-8185-0133-2 in early listings. The subtitle said *systematic*. Not inspired. Not Rogerian as a church. A model a student could walk through in a semester. Counselor-education programs bought it in numbers that later marketing copy would exaggerate; the historical fact does not need the exaggeration. For a quarter-century you could walk into an American counseling classroom and find Egan on the desk beside, or instead of, Carkhuff and Ivey.

He is treated as living at last verification (2026-09-14), per this pack's claims file. Library of Congress name authority records a 17 June 1930 birth in the Cook County index and ties the 1975 title page to Loyola University of Chicago. Public books and public academic posts only. No private life. No health speculation. Portrait: none.

## Loyola, philosophy, psychology

Egan was born in Chicago. He graduated from Loyola Academy (1948) and Loyola University (A.B., 1953), studied philosophy at West Baden College, and later took graduate degrees in psychology at Loyola, including a Ph.D. in 1969. Library of Congress notes adjunct work at Loyola's Institute of Pastoral Studies from the early 1970s and a long stretch as professor of psychology and department chair at North Park University. Later public descriptions call him professor emeritus of organization development and psychology in Loyola's business school as well as IPS adjunct faculty. The double address is the interesting fact. *The Skilled Helper* was a counseling text. The same author wrote for managers about conversations and the "shadow side" of organizations (*Working the Shadow Side*, Jossey-Bass, 1994). A helping model that can be sold to a counselor and a supervisor is a historical traveler. Ivey's microskills made a similar trip. So did Carkhuff's later HRD titles. Egan's trip started in a Jesuit-and-psychology Chicago and did not stay in the clinic.

Other early books — *Encounter* (1970) is in the LOC record — sit in the encounter-group weather of that moment. *Interpersonal Living* and *People in Systems* (with Michael Cowan) belong to the same teaching decade: helping as a learnable interpersonal craft, then as a systems craft. *The Skilled Helper* is the object this series needs. It outlived the encounter decade by becoming a staged curriculum that counselor-education programs could assign without joining an institute.

## Stages as a teaching machine

Egan's model, across editions, taught helping as a sequence. Early language leaned on exploration, a middle stage of clearer understanding or goal-setting, and action. Later editions, including work with Robert J. Reese, branded a problem-management and opportunity-development approach. The eleventh edition is dated 2018 in publisher listings. Cengage inherited Brooks/Cole. Cite the edition on the desk. The stages moved. The bet did not: a beginner needs a map of *when* to use a skill, not only a list of skills.

That bet is why Egan sits in this pack's teaching-figures wave rather than in the therapy-history founders. He did not found a school with a membership pin. He wrote the book that made other people's skills usable in order. Reflection, questions, challenge, goal talk — the units Ivey and Carkhuff had isolated — acquired a semester-shaped plot. Supervisors could say "you are still in stage I" and mean something the student had read. The sentence can become a trap. Real hours do not always climb stairs. Egan's later prefaces were not naive about the "shadow side of helping": helper need, fads, the temptation to keep a person in exploration because action would be harder. Name the warning as a published theme. Do not print a self-audit.

Brooks/Cole was, in those years, the counseling-textbook house. A Monterey paperback could reach a state-college program that would never see a Wisconsin tape. Egan's stages gave that program a plot when Carkhuff had given it a grade and Ivey a unit. Three objects, one decade, three answers to "what should a beginner do next?" The 1975 book is the one that answered with a map of purposes. Later editions added opportunity-development language so the model would not sound only like problem-hunting. The addition is a historical revision, not a new treatment.

Hill's 1999 APA sequence (exploration, insight, action) is a cousin, not a copy. Carkhuff's five-point ratings graded the *quality* of a response. Egan staged the *purpose* of a stretch of talk. A student who took both courses met two clocks. This page will not merge them.

## What the textbook did to the profession

A widely assigned text becomes an unofficial standard. Accreditation visits, in the CACREP era after 1981, did not require Egan. Many programs assigned him anyway. International editions and translations carried a Chicago model into rooms that had never heard of Loyola. The book's success created a problem it could not solve: helpers who could name the stages and still not hear the person. That problem is older than 1975. The textbook made it countable as a training failure rather than a mystery of character.

Egan's later *TalkWorks* titles with Andrew Bailey took the conversational claim into workplace English. Organization development was not a betrayal of the 1975 book. It was the same claim — systematic helping — under a different budget. A speech clinic that borrows "skilled helper" language from a workshop should know it may be borrowing a business book as easily as a counseling one. The words travelled.

## Limits of this portrait

This series will not reprint stage charts. It will not give "try these questions in stage II." Egan's model is a historical teaching package. It is not a consumer treatment. It is not "the" counseling. Person-centered, cognitive, feminist, and multicultural critiques of staged, goal-heavy helping are part of the record; they live in other essays in this pack. Egan's Chicago Catholic-and-psychological formation is public educational history. It is not a cue to invent a pastoral scene.

## A rural clinic and a Monterey paperback

WOW Therapies is a Southeast Arkansas speech-language practice. Its new clinicians may have met Egan in a counseling elective, or they may have met a three-box slide with his name peeled off. The useful inheritance is modest: a helping hour can be talked about as having purposes that change. The danger is the same modest thing inflated: a swallow evaluation forced into "stage III action" because the textbook said the hour should end that way. ASHA's 2016 Scope of Practice names counseling about communication, feeding, and swallowing. It does not name Brooks/Cole 1975. The paperback still reached the allied-health shelf because once helping was systematic, every later syllabus had to decide whether to teach a map or to keep calling the work kindness.

The therapy-history pack can keep the founders. This pack keeps the 1975 textbook as a teaching machine, Loyola as the address on the title page, and a living author whose private life is not copy.
